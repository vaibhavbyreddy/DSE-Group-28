\section{Trade-Off Summary}
\label{sec:trade-off_summary}

Given the strict requirements and ambitions of the project, the design of HUGO rapidly converged towards a lightweight, fixed-wing drone with varying degrees of modularity. Thus, four different configurations were developed to accommodate, depicted in \autoref{fig:hugo-configurations}, to allow researchers to perform different sets of experiments, in different manners. 

HUG-CFG-301 (\autoref{fig:hug-cfg-301}) is a low-wing concept with static wing mounts and the ability to install a canard. HUG-CFG-302 (\autoref{fig:hug-cfg-302}) is instead a high-wing drone, with static wing mounts and no canard mounting port. HUG-CFG-303 (\autoref{fig:hug-cfg-303}) maintains the high-wing and static wing mounts, and further adds the ability to mount a canard. Lastly, HUG-CFG-304 (\autoref{fig:hug-cfg-304}) has a high-wing configuration with variable mounting locations for the main wing, as well as a static canard port.

The weight assigned to each criterion was determined using the analytic hierarchy process approach. The process compares each of the trade-off criteria pairwise, on a scale from 1 to 9. The normalised priority vector is then calculated, giving the relative weight amongst compared parameters. The selected criteria and their calculated weights can be seen in \autoref{tab:trade_off_matrix}.

\section{Final Concept Design}
\label{sec:final_concept_design}
\todo[inline]{In this Section, we briefly present the configuration that is going to be designed in more detail in this report.}

Following the configuration trade-off, HUG-CFG-303 was selected as the final concept to be developed in this report. The selected high wing configuration comes with static modular wing mounting port, a canard mounting port, a T-tail, two jet engines mounted on the fuselage, and a retractable landing gear. The main mission of HUGO is to serve as a reusable flight test platform for high-speed aeroelastic and aeroservoelastic research. Therefore, the final concept is not designed around a single wing planform, but the ability to accommodate a range of various planforms.\\
The main wing is mounted to a fixed structural interface on top of the fuselage, which simplifies the load path and reduces the complexity of certification, inspection, and maintenance. The high-wing arrangement also provides favourable ground clearance for high aspect ratio wings and leaves a large amount of fuselage space. The canard port is a modular feature of the final concept, which is not required to be present in all possible test configurations. The port can covered to minimize aerodynamic disturbances. When installed, the canard allows HUGO to support additional experimental configurations. The empennage is configured as a T-tail to keep the horizontal tail away from the main wing wake and to provide sufficient pitch control authority for a considered planform envelope. The landing gear is integrated into the fuselage group rather than the wing, so that any other wings provided by customers do not need to include landing gear structure. Similarly, the propulsion system is mounted on and inside the fuselage to keep the wing interface independent of the installed test planform.\\
There are, therefore, two groups in the HUGO configuration: fixed and modular components. The fixed group of components, consisting of the fuselage, propulsion system, landing gear, avionics, and common safety and mission critical systems stays unchanged for all possible configurations. The second is the modular group, consisting of the wing, canard, and replaceable empennage components. In the further design procedure, the fixed group is designed to support the full range of missions, while envelopes for modular components and their standard versions for testing are defined.

\section{Engineering Design and Analysis}
\label{sec:engineering_design_and_analysis}
%\todo[inline]{In this Section, we briefly describe the steps required to reach the more detailed design.}

To fulfill its mission as a versatile flight test vehicle, HUGO is expected to house a range of wing planforms, later referred to as the Planform Envelope. This approach shall, for a wide range of missions to be performed, examples of which can be found in \autoref{sec:mission_envelope}. The need to house multiple wing geometries has been a system design-driving characteristic since the midterm design phase. The goals of engineering analysis for the final work phase are to design HUGO's fuselage and its associated subsystems, ensuring they accommodate the specified range of planforms provided by client, as well as develop a standard planform to be used for external system tests that do not require a dedicated wing profile.

Several analyses described in the Midterm Report \footnote{\label{foot:midterm}\href{https://github.com/Teo-Cokonaj/DSE-28/blob/main/docs/Projects/28_HUGO_Midterm_Report_final.pdf}{HUGO Midterm Report} (accessed on 11.06.2026)} can be used for this design phase, i. e. the drag estimation, tail sizing, and fuel mass fraction computation. The corresponding matching diagram can also be re-implemented. Based on these findings, a structural analysis of both the wing planform and fuselage can be performed to obtain a complete estimate of aircraft weight. Further design steps include a preliminary sizing of the fuselage, along with a planform aileron sizing.

Considering the wide scope of possible array designs, featuring more than one possible airfoil, the approach to aerodynamic analysis differs from that of the Midterm Report \footref{foot:midterm}.
Instead of defining an airfoil and conducting a lifting line analysis to obtain the properties of the three-dimensional wing directly, an envelope of possible airfoils is defined implicitly by ranges of values of lift slope, maximum lift and moment coefficients. The properties of the three-dimensional wing are deduced from those coefficients using semi-empirical formulae.

An additional challenge posed by the Planform Envelope is that it is a continuous design space spanning several parameters. Evaluating all combinations of those parameters at high resolution is not feasible. As such, a list of parameter combinations considered most constraining is justified in \autoref{sec:Planform envelope boundaries}.

To allow for general-purpose testing of control systems, the standard planform of HUGO needs to be free of aeroelastic instability. To ensure that, an aeroelastic analysis of the planform is conducted  \autoref{sec:flutter_analysis}. The standard planform also serves as the benchmark for evaluating the performance of the aircraft in \autoref{ch:performance_analysis}. The particular methods employed in the design of HUGO need to be verified, and the effect of all assumptions therein is to be quantified via sensitivity studies. \autoref{sec:VnV} contains an outline of how each method is verified, with further detail given further in the report, together with particular method descriptions.

\section{Boundaries of the Planform Envelope}
\label{sec:Planform envelope boundaries}

The Planform Envelope requires quantification - both for the user (to serve as specifications for wing design), and for further development. It is expected that a potential clients interested in planform research shall express interest in testing a wing of a particular geometry, not size, so long as the difference in Reynolds and Froude numbers can be accounted for. As such, the design specifications provided by the client are: the aspect ratio, taper, dihedral, sweep and thickness-to-chord profiles, which need not be constant along the wing. Additionally, the client is expected to specify wing cross sections along the span, provided the airfoil coefficients of those cross sections fit within the specified range. The wing area and structural thicknesses are deduced from the provided specifications.

\autoref{tab:planform_envelope} provides the range of allowed planform parameters. A planform is considered compatible with HUGO if it is described by any combination of the parameters within their ranges. To assess whether HUGO can accommodate those possible planforms, the most constraining values of each parameter are identified, and planforms represented by combinations of those critical values are analysed for compliance with requirements. Based on the number of identified critical value combinations, 96 planforms representing the critical envelope points can be defined. The critical values and parameter ranges were based on the following reasoning:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $AR$: the lower bound corresponds to low-$AR$ aerobatic aircraft characteristics \footnote{\label{foot:Aerobatic_AR}\href{https://web.archive.org/web/20060414172013/http://www.sukhoi.org/eng/planes/civil/su-26/characteristic/}{Sukhoi Su-26 Specifications} (accessed on 06.10.2026)}, while the upper bound is as considered in previous reports \footnote{\label{foot:baseline}\href{https://github.com/Teo-Cokonaj/DSE-28/blob/main/docs/Projects/DSE_Group_28_Baseline_Report.pdf}{Baseline Report} (accessed on 11.06.2026)} \footref{foot:midterm}. As increasing the $AR$ improves the aerodynamic efficiency while increasing wing weight, a single value of this parameter cannot be considered constraining. Therefore, four values from across the range are included as critical.
    \item $\lambda$: any taper profile that results in the wing not widening while moving inboard, but preventing it from becoming excessively thin, is considered acceptable. In the considered range, the most constraining case, when both aerodynamics and structural performance are negatively impacted, is when $\lambda$ is 1 \cite[p.401-405, p.438]{raymer1992aircraft, AndersonAerodynamics}.
    \item $\Lambda_{c/4}$: positive sweeps up to slightly above the Midterm Report value \footref{foot:midterm}. The extremum values of sweep are taken as critical, as for maximum sweep the effective airspeed is reduced, and the aerodynamic centre is most aft, and for the minimum sweep the aerodynamic centre is most fore. The former is challenging for tail sizing for stability and main wing performance, while for the latter, empennage sizing for controllability is compromised. 
    \item $t/c$: The range encompasses thin and potentially unstable wings and high-thickness profiles similar to those at wing roots of large aircraft \footnote{\label{foot:reference_tc_values}\href{https://m-selig.ae.illinois.edu/ads/aircraft.html}{David Lednicer, The Incomplete Guide to Airfoil Usage} (accessed on )}. As for the critical points, parasitic drag increases with $t/c$ \cite[p.283]{raymer1992aircraft}, but the wing weight decreases significantly \cite[p.401-405]{raymer1992aircraft}. Thus, three values across the parameter range are selected for evaluation.
    \item $C_{l_\text{max}}$: To ensure sufficient low-speed performance, a minimum value of this parameter is established as 1, which is below the $C_{l_\text{max}}$ of most aifoils \footnote{\label{foot:NACA_4415}\href{http://airfoiltools.com/airfoil/details?airfoil=naca4415-il}{Airfoil Tools, NACA 4415} (accessed on 11.06.2026)}\footnote{\label{foot:NACA_24112}\href{http://airfoiltools.com/airfoil/details?airfoil=naca24112-jf}{Airfoil Tools, NACA 24112} (accessed on 11.06.2026)}.
    \item $C_{m_{ac}}$: The extrema values of this parameter are most constraining for sizing the empennage for controllability and, as such, taken as critical points. The minimum $C_{m_{ac}}$ accounts for high camber sections such as NACA 4415 with $C_{m_{ac}}$ of around -0.12 \footref{foot:NACA_4415}, while the upper bound accounts for reflexed airfoils such as NACA 24112  with $C_{m_{ac}}$ of up to 0.03 \footref{foot:NACA_24112}.
    \item $C_{l_0}$: The impact of this parameter is secondary - provided $C_{l_\alpha}$ and $C_{l_{\mathrm{max}}}$ are satisfactory, it only changes the angle of attack required for a given amount of lift. Hence, any non-negative vlue of the parameter is allowed, with zero taken as the critical value.
    \item $C_{l_\alpha}$: The lift slope for thin airfoil is typically close to $2\pi$ and this value is taken as critical.
    \item Empennage type: both replaceable tail cone and canard port are available at the aircraft, and they may be used simultaneously. As the moment arms of both the tail and the canard are fixed, cases when only the canard or only the tail are present are taken as constraining.
    \item $\Gamma$: The lower bound of dihedral at zero is set not to worsen the wing tip clearance, and the upper bound is where the lift reduction from dihedral becomes approximately 1\%. Due to its secondary influence, $\Gamma$ is excluded from the analysis, with a value of 0 used for landing gear clearance computations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Preliminary Design Workflow}
There are two similar, but independent analyses that need to be conducted in parallel - the planform envelope analysis and the standard planform analysis. The planform envelope analysis concerns each planform identified as critical in \autoref{sec:Planform envelope boundaries} combined with a provided fixed group. It allows for assessing whether a given HUGO fixed group meets the performance requirements for all planforms.

The standard planform analysis performs more in-depth checks, such as flutter analysis and noise and emissions estimations for the standard planform. This is to ensure that the standard planform is safe to operate and has known performance, as well as to establish the nominal environmental impact of the aircraft.

These two analyses require common prior steps such as final material selection and in-depth fuselage group design involving selection of internal components and structural sizing of the fuselage and the landing gear. Each design analysis and sizing step from \autoref{fig:workflow_diagram} has been accompanied with a verification and/or validation procedure (\autoref{tab:analysis_modules}) to ensure that the methods are implemented correctly and are applicable to the design. Approaches such as sanity checks, sensitivity studies quantifying the effect of input parameters on outputs, as well as code verification via unit tests, have been employed. In addition, results have been verified by hand and compared with results from reference models where possible. Aeroelastic analysis, the matching diagram, the noise estimation and fuel mass fraction analysis have been verified against examples present in their respective references. Additionally, cross-verification between the semi-empirical formulae involved and panel method solver flow5 \footnote{\href{https://flow5.tech/flow5.html}{flow5} (accessed 17.06.2026)} has been conducted.

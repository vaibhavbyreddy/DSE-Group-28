The goals of the aerodynamic characteristics estimate is to obtain the lift and induced drag of the aircraft required for the later calculation of the $L/D$ ratio, the lift curve slopes of all the lifting surfaces (both individually and combined with other lifting surfaces), the lift distribution along the span, the aerodynamic centre position, and the determination of the angle of attack required for trimming.

High subsonic speeds experienced by HUGO during the nominal mission necessitate the use of a supercritical airfoil to avoid large drag penalties due to shock waves and boundary layer separations induced by locally supersonic flows \cite{Whitcomb1974}. A preliminary sizing of the wing and tail surfaces performed in previous design phases identified NASA SC(2)-0012 as a suitable airfoil choice for HUGO \cite{Harris1990}, based on the performance requirements and preliminary wing design lift coefficient of 0.074 \footref{ft:midterm_rep}.

\section{Lift Distribution}
\label{sec:lift}
The lift distribution was obtained using a Schrenk approximation evaluated at the positive limit load factor \cite{Schrenk1940}, excluding the portion of the planforms enclosed by the fuselage. The approach followed a methodology for obtaining load distributions for light aircraft proposed by EASA \cite{Easa LSA load calculations}, in which the design condition implies the aircraft flying at its manoeuver stall speed $V_{s_\text{n max}}$

To achieve an approximation of the load distribution, the planforms were divided into \textbf{TBD} sections evaluated individually. Schrenk's method assumes that the aerodynamic loading on the planform is equal to the average of the chord distribution, and an equivalent elliptical lift distribution, as shown in \autoref{eq:schrenk_element_lift} \cite[p. 20]{EASA LSA}. Therefore, for each segment, an equivalent chord length $c_i$ was defined based on the spanwise position $y_i$, and a corresponding lift increment $l_{\text{Schrenk},i}$ was assigned. Sections incorporated into the fuselage were then excluded from the model by scaling the sections with respect to the avergae fuselage width at each section $b_0$ and the total wingspan $b$. The remaining lift was rescaled to compensate for the losses. 

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:schrenk_element_lift}
l_{\text{Schrenk},i} = \frac{1}{2}\rho V_{s_\text{n max}}^2C_{L_\text{n max}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}
\left(c_i+\frac{4}{\pi}c_{\mathrm{MAC}}\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{y_i-\frac{b_0}{2}}
{\frac{b-b_0}{2}}\right)^2}\right)
\end{equation}

Applying the modified Schrenk's method was deemed as a conservative approach, as it is expected to slightly overestimate the loads acting on the planform root. It is additionally consistent with methodologies proposed by Raymer \cite{Raymer} and Roskam \cite{roskam} for the design of subsonic to high-subsonic regime aircraft, and has been verified as sufficiently accurate to be used for the preliminary structural analysis of UAVs when compared to CFD methods \cite{Kamal 2019}. It shall be noted, however, that the method is only accurate for planforms with low sweep angles, and does not account for complex aeroelastic phenomena, such as coupling, flutter, gusts, and load reliefs due to deformations. Therefore, the use of CFD-based models is recommended for the development of a detailed design.

\section{Drag Estimation}
\label{sec:drag}
The parasite drag ($C_{D_0}$) estimation was performed using the drag build-up method described by Raymer \cite{raymer1992aircraft}. The subsonic parasite drag of each component was estimated separately, and then summed to obtain the total $C_{D_0}$. The component drag was defined as a product of the wetted area $S_\text{wet}$, calculated flat-plate skin-friction drag coefficient $C_f$, form factor $FF$ that estimates the pressure drag due to viscous separation, and interference component $Q$ that estimates the increase in drag due to the interference between the different components, as shown in \autoref{eq:CD0} \cite[p. 281]{raymer1992aircraft}.
\begin{equation}
    (C_{D_0})_\text{subsonic} = \frac{\sum(C_{f_c} \cdot FF_c \cdot Q_c \cdot S_{wet_c})}{S_\text{ref}} + C_{D_\text{misc}} + C_{D_{L\&P}}
    \label{eq:CD0}
\end{equation}

Here, $C_{D_\text{misc}}$ is miscellaneous drag due to flaps, un-retracted landing gear etc., and $C_{D_{L\&P}}$ is an estimate of the drag due to leakages and protuberances. $S_{wet}$ is the wetted area, and $S_{ref}$ is the reference area. The subscript $c$ refers to a single component.

The $C_f$ is estimated as a weighted average of the laminar and turbulent flow values, and can be calculated according to Raymer  \cite[p. 282]{raymer1992aircraft}. In case the skin roughness is high, the $C_f$ value can be expected to increase. An adjusted value can be calculated by introducing a cut-off Reynold number $Re_\text{cut-off}$ for subsonic flight \cite[p. 282]{raymer1992aircraft}, for a given  characteristic length and skin roughness.

The $C_{D_0}$ was further utilised to construct the drag polar (see: \autoref{}) and compute glide ratios required for fuel consumption estimations, which will be performed in \textbf{TBD}.

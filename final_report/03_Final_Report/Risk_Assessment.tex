\chapter{Technical Risk Assessment and Certification}
\label{ch:risk_assessment}

Due to HUGO's unconventional configuration and mission plan, it is important to consider if such a UAV is safe and certifiable by the competent authorities. This chapter focuses on the analysis of technical risks associated with the UAV's development and operational risks, and the steps required to obtain the necessary certification for operations testing the aeroelastic effects of HAR wings. A qualitative assessment of technical risks is performed in \autoref{sec:technical_risks_identification}. Suitable means of risk prevention anf contingency management are expanded upon in \autoref{sec:risk_mitigation_contingency_management}. Then, in \autoref{sec:type_certificate}, the classification process is described that places HUGO into a type class category in consultation with EASA. Following the assessment of flight class, the certification for a Design Organisation Approval (DOA) is covered in \autoref{sec: DOA}. A note on the applicable Means of Compliance (MOC) and recommendations regarding future certification steps are presented in \autoref{sec:means_of_compliance}.

\section{Identification of Technical Risks}
\label{sec:technical_risks_identification}
Throughout the development of HUGO, key technical risks were identified by evaluating the likelihood of occurrence $p$ and severity of impact $s$ \cite{Lanzafame2024}. Both of the criteria were evaluated on a scale from 1 to 5, with 1 being the lowest score and 5 being the highest. Details regarding the allocation of both scores are explained in \autoref{tab:risk_map_scores}.

Operation risks refer to hazardous scenarios which can occur both on the ground and in the air. According to the JARUS guidelines for risk assessment of UAVs, they can be identified and classified based on the following criteria \cite{Jarus_main}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The extent to which third parties or property on the ground could be endangered by the activity;
    \item The complexity, performance and operational characteristics of the unmanned aircraft involved;
    \item The purpose of the flight, UAS type, probability of collision with other aircraft, class of airspace;
    \item The type, scale, and complexity of the UAS operation or activity, including, where relevant, the size and type of the traffic handled by the responsible organisation or person;
    \item The extent to which the persons affected by the risks involved in the UAS operation are able to assess and exercise control over those risks.
\end{itemize}

The tolerance level of both development and operational risks can be determined by analysing the corresponding risk matrix (\autoref{fig:initial_risk_matrix}). Based on the position of each risk in the matrix, it can be assigned one of the following tolerance levels:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Low/controlled (green):} The combined likelihood and severity of the risk make it acceptable, and their impact of the project is deemed as acc.
    \item \textbf{Moderate/tolerated (yellow): } The combined likelihood and severity of the risk make it tolerable under the condition that appropriate preventive measures are applied and contingency procedures are clearly outlined and followed in case the risks manifests itself.
    \item \textbf{Extreme/unacceptable (red): } The combined likelihood and severity of the risk make it impossible for the design or operations of HUGO to proceed. Unless extensive preventive measures and contingency procedures are developed and adhered to, the success of the project cannot be ensured.
\end{enumerate}

As can be seen in \autoref{fig:initial_risk_matrix}, some of the initial risks fall within the "unacceptable" category, and several others come close to the boundary values between "unacceptable" and "tolerated". Therefore, preventive measures and contingency strategies shall be developed to ensure that not only the likelihood of each risk is lowered, but also the consequences to HUGO and its surroundings can be minimised shall the risk manifest itself.

\section{Type Certificate}
\label{sec:type_certificate}

According to the EASA eRules for Initial Airworthiness and Environmental Protection (eRules), the Type Certificate (TC) is a document issued by the competent authority declaring an aircraft meets all required airworthiness standards \cite{EASAInitialAirworthiness2025}. The eRules describes the process and all necessary documents to obtain a type certificate inside Annex I. Section A details all the obligations of the \textbf{applicant}, and section B details those of the \textbf{competent authority (EASA)}. The eRules manual is difficult to navigate, as such,  \autoref{fig:Certification} will serve as a visual guide for the reader. The grey blocks indicate the starting point, purple indicates the applicant's obligations, blue indicates the authority's obligations, and yellow indicates any additional sources of information.

The requirements for a TC are given in section (21.A.21). They state that the following must be met:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item demonstration of capabilities (21.A.14),
    \item demonstration of compliance with the type certification basis, operational suitability data certification basis and environmental protection requirements (21.A.20),
    \item demonstration of compliance with UAS type certification basis (21.B.80). 
\end{enumerate}

Section (21.A.14) will be further elaborated in \autoref{sec: DOA}, as it pertains to the process for obtaining a Design Organisation Approval (DOA) under 21.A.14 (a), with further guidance in Subsection J. Both (21.A.20) and (21.B.80) are covered under the section (21.A.15): Application for a TC, and will be expanded on below.

As previously stated, (21.A.15): Application for a TC, requires compliance with section (21.A.20): Demonstration of compliance with the type certification basis, operational suitability data certification basis and environmental protection requirements Regulation. 

\subsection{Compliance with certification basis, operational suitability, environmental protection}
\label{sec:CS-23 and SC-UAS}

For compliance with (21.A.20) after the certification programme has been accepted by EASA, HUGO's design will have to demonstrate compliance in the following areas: type-certification basis for a TC or restricted TC (21.B.80), operational suitability (21.B.82), and environmental protection (21.B.85).

According to (21.B.80), HUGO requires special conditions on top of the specified eRules due to its unmanned design and operational complexity. Additionally, HUGO falls outside the scope of (21.B.82) due to its unconventional design and operations. Therefore, the use of Special Conditions for Light Unmanned Aircraft Systems - High Risk \cite{easa_light_uas_crd_is3} is required for certification purposes.

Section (21.B.85), detailing the necessary compliance with environmental protection requirements as dictated in Regulation (EU) 2018/1139 Annex III, further expanded on in \autoref{sec:environmental_protection}. 

Sections (21.A.33) and (21.A.35) contains requirements for the applicant regarding inspections and tests regulation, and flight tests respectively. Lastly, (21.B.100) details the authority's obligation to provide guidance on the "Level of involvement", and the likelihood and impact of non-compliance. 

With a certification basis established, the remainder of the requirements for the application of a TC achievable, it can be determined that HUGO's intended mission is not regulatorily inviable.

\subsection{Special Conditions for UAS}
\label{sec: special conditions}
The special conditions detailed under the Special Condition for Light Unmanned Aircraft Systems - High Risk define additional requirements for: Design and Construction, and Systems and Equipment. The scope of these additional requirements covers risk of failure, environmental hazards, robustness in the event of a lightning strike, and additional High Intensity Radiated Fields protection \cite{easa_light_uas_crd_is3}.

\subsection{Environmental protection}
\label{subsec:environmental_protection}
Environmental compatibility was introduced in the European legislation Regulation (EU) 2018/1139 \footnote{\label{doc_1139}\href{https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2018/1139/oj/eng}{EU Document 32018R1139} (accessed on 13.05.2026)}. The intention was to create a common framework between member states for the environmental certification of aircraft. HUGO is exempt from those rules as stated in recital 1 in Regulation (EU) 2025/870 \footnote{\href{https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX\%3A32025R0870}{EU Document 32025R0870} (accessed on 13.05.2026).}: \textit{"Aircraft, other than unmanned aircraft, and their engines, propellers, parts and non-installed equipment should comply with the environmental protection requirements. Regulation (EU) 2018/1139"}
A specific rule-set has been established for unmanned aircraft: Regulation (EU) 2018/1139 \footref{doc_1139} of the European Parliament and of the Council in Annex~IX: \textit{"Essential Environmental Requirements for Unmanned Aircraft"} states that unmanned aircraft shall comply with the environmental performance requirements set out in Annex~III.
Looking at Annex~III, the main point is a general approach for the design and trade-off between minimizing noise and emissions, which has to be taken into account in the area of design and operation.



\subsection{Application for a Type Certificate}
As mentioned in \autoref{sec:type_certificate}, HUGO's application for the TC is done in accordance with (21.A.15) \cite{EASAInitialAirworthiness2025}. The detailed procedure after the project is in compliance with (21.A.21) is detailed below:

According to the guidelines, the TC application should be made in a format stipulated by the authority, including as a minimum: a preliminary description of the UAS or the Control and Monitoring Unit (CMU), and the kind of operations for which certification is requested. In addition it shall comply with 21.A.20, which outlines the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a detailed description of the design, including all the configurations to be certified,
    \item the proposed operating characteristics and limitations,
    \item the intended use of the UAS or the CMU, and the kind of operations for which certification is requested,
    \item a proposal for the initial TC basis, operational sustainability data certification basis, and environmental protection requirements in accordance with (21.B.80), (21.B.82), and (21.B.85),
    \item a proposal for the breakdown of the certification program into meaningful groups of compliance demonstration activities and data, including a proposal for the means of compliance and related compliance documents,
    \item a proposal for the assessment of meaningful groups of compliance demonstration activities and data addressing the likelihood of an unidentified non-compliance with the TC basis operational sustainability data certification basis, or environmental protection requirements and the potential impact on the non-compliance on the UAS safety or environmental compatibility or on the safety of the CMU. The proposed assessment shall take into account at least the elements set out in points 1 to 4 of (21.B.100(a)),
    \begin{enumerate}
    \item novel or unusual features of the certification project, including operational, organisational and knowledge management aspects;
    \item complexity of the design and/or demonstration of compliance;
    \item criticality of the design or technology and the related safety and environmental risks, including those identified on similar designs; and
    \item performance and experience of the design organisation of the applicant in the domain concerned.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}


\section{Design Organisation Approval (DOA)} \label{sec: DOA}
This section discusses the approach towards certifying modifications to the original configuration of HUGO, following a successful application for a Type Certificate. Special attention is dedicated towards the DOA, its implications for the feasibility of HUGO as a flying testbed, and the necessary steps towards obtaining necessary approval from EASA.

\subsection{Certification of Design Modifications}
For the certification procedure of an aircraft, the competent authority in Europe, EASA, requires extensive documentation and validation procedures of the design. For this stage several EASA-imposed operational restrictions must be considered, including the applicable airworthiness class and the permitted operational environment \cite{EASAInitialAirworthiness2025}.

DOA application is a step in the process of obtaining a Type Certificate, according to 21.A.14 (a), capability demonstration. Along with 21.A.20 (compliance documentation) and 21.B.80 (configuration demonstration), these three documents will facilitate the application for a Type Certificate.

The application for a DOA involves cooperation with EASA from early stages of the design. The steps necessary for successful certification are \footnote{\href{https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/aircraft-products/aircraft-certification?}{EASA Aircraft Certification} (accessed on 04.05.2026)}: technical familiarisation and certification basis, establishment of the certification programme, compliance demonstration, and technical closure and issue of approval.

Costs involved in this process and the time required for analysis of the design are not well defined, but can range from months to years, depending on the complexity of the concept \footnote{\href{https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/light/topics/idea-sky-how-easa-ensures-aircraft-safe-fly}{EASA - From idea to the sky: how EASA ensures that aircraft is safe to fly} (accessed on 12.05.2026)}. This is direct with conflict with HUGO's mission, which requires frequent structural modifications and configuration changes. Applying for a Supplemental Type Certificate or completely re-certifying HUGO for each test case due to design changes would result in an excess of downtime of the UAV, loss in revenue, and ultimately loss of purpose as a research testbed \cite{EU2019_2153_fees-and-charges}.

According to EASA classification, re-certification is required each time a change is implemented into the design of an aircraft, with a clear distinction between "minor changes" and "major changes"
(21.A.91) \cite{EASAInitialAirworthiness2025}. A "minor change" is classified as any type of modification that does not show any discernable effect on weight, structural strength, reliability and other specifications. A change that is not a "minor change" is considered as a "major change". This distinction determines the steps that needs to be taken in order to certify modifications to the design of an aircraft.


\subsection{The Importance of DOA for HUGO's Mission}
The expected structural changes in the wing and tail platforms between experiments are considered as "major changes" according to the EASA classification discussed previously. Typically, this would require direct supervision from EASA, significantly increasing the time and resources required to ensure that the new configuration can be classified as airworthy. To facilitate this process, a DOA application can be made \footnote{\label{DOA_site}\href{https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/aircraft-products/design-organisations/design-organisations-approvals}{EASA - Design Organisations Approvals} (accessed on 05.06.2026)}.

A  DOA is a certification that permits the holder to perform the certification process internally, reducing the workload of EASA to documentation approval as the certification authority. In the context of HUGO, this would allow the design team to internally implement all "minor changes" without unnecessary delay and with minimal dedicated resources. Additionally, "major changes" to the platforms can be classified as research procedures, excluding the need for full re-certification and reducing EASA's involvement \footnote{In conversation with EASA employee}.

%Under the DOA, research within certification limits can be conducted without scrutiny from EASA.  \todo{cite email} 

In conversation with Hugo Manuel Rodrigues Lima da Silva, an EASA expert, it was established that in order for HUGO's design team to obtain a DOA: a specific department shall be formed within EASA that will obtain the permission to supervise the changes brought to HUGO, perform required validation steps, and ensure compliance with the applicable certification basis. It was additionally established that personnel comprising of three EASA employees shall be sufficient given the complexity of HUGO's design. 

The pricing of a DOA is set at \EUR{10,170} for the initial certification, after which a 12-month billing cycle fee of \EUR{11,050} is charged by EASA to continue oversight of the approved organisation  \footnote{\href{https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2025/2347/oj}{EASA Prices} (accessed on 13.05.2026)}.


The average time required to obtain a DOA was estimated as one year, provided that all procedures have been properly developed. The certification process itself is more dependent on the schedule of the design team and EASA employees, with an estimate duration of three years. Notably, both the DOA and the certification processes can run at the same time.

\section{Means of Compliance and Future Recommendations}
\label{sec:means_of_compliance}
Based on the analysis performed in this chapter, it can be said that not only is HUGO's design certifiable and operable within an acceptable risk level, but also that the necessary certification can be complete within a reasonable timeframe. Moreover, thanks to the application for DOA, a wide variety of tests can be performed without the need for a time- and resource-intensive recertification. However, according to EASA's SC Light-UAS \cite{easa_light_uas_crd_is3}, no Means of Compliance have been defined so far for the UAS certified category operating in medium or high risk. Suitable ways of demonstrating compliance within the certification category can be individually proposed by the applicant and reviewed by EASA once the detailed design has been completed.

Since HUGO's capability of operation within an EASA-controlled airspace has been confirmed, the design can proceed towards the definition of operations and logistics, including the selection of an appropriate airfield, defining an exemplary flight day, and describing tests that can be performed within the Planform Envelope.

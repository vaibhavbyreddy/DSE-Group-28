\subsection{OOA procedure for prepregs}
\label{sub:OOA}

The datasheet for the selected CYCOM 5320-1 prepreg includes recommended Vacuum Bag Only (VBO) cure cycles. These are generally divided into two categories: a continuous cure cycle and a free-standing post-cure cycle. In the continuous process, the part remains on the tool for the entire duration of the cure. In contrast, the free-standing post-cure method involves removing the part from the tool after an initial cure, allowing it to cool before completing the cure in an oven. 

The post-cure approach is particularly advantageous for rapid prototyping, as it frees up the mould earlier in the process. This enables the next part to be prepared while the previous one continues curing, thereby improving overall production efficiency. The recommended curing cycles are summarized in \autoref{tab:cure_cycles}.

It is worth mentioning that the post-cure ramp rate for the freestanding cycles is significantly slower. That is deliberate since the part is unsupported, and a slow ramp minimizes thermal gradients and keeps it from distorting as the resin softens before the extra crosslinking locks in the final geometry. 

An important factor to consider is the total manufacturing time associated with each cycle. The freestanding cycles enable the simultaneous production of two parts: while one part undergoes post-curing, the next can begin its initial cure. This reduces overall time compared to Cycle A, where the part remains in the mould for the entire curing process. However, in Cycles B and C, the cooling phase and lower ramp rates lead to a longer overall manufacturing time.

For comparison, the total manufacturing time is estimated assuming a single mould is used for both the wing and fuselage monocoque, as well as a single out-of-autoclave (OOA) oven, which reflects a realistic setup for producing a total of five HUGO units. Cycle A results in a significantly shorter manufacturing time than Cycles B and C, primarily because of the very low post-cure ramp rates. Cycle C is further extended due to the prolonged initial cure of 10 hours at $200^\circ$F. In the case of a single oven, the advantage of simultaneous manufacturing cannot be fully realised, as the post-cure duration significantly exceeds the combined duration of the initial cure and cooling stages.

In terms of dimensional accuracy, Cycle A offers the best performance because the part remains constrained within the mould for the entire curing process. In contrast, the freestanding curing cycles are more susceptible to warping or distortion once the part is removed from the mould. Nevertheless, this risk is mitigated by the low post-cure ramp rates.

Another important consideration is the maximum temperature that the selected honeycomb and foam core materials can withstand during curing. Furthermore, the mould temperature requirements differ substantially between the curing cycles. For the freestanding cycles, the moulds are only exposed to temperatures of approximately $200-250^\circ$F during the initial cure. In contrast, Cycle A requires the moulds to withstand the full cure temperature of $350^\circ$F, which may increase tooling costs and impose stricter material and design requirements on the moulds.

To achieve the temperatures required for curing, an out-of-autoclave (OOA) furnace will be rented. Suitable furnaces are commercially available with sufficient internal volume to accommodate both the fuselage and wing composite components. Rental costs are estimated to be between \texteuro{7,000-12,000}  for a period of 3-5 months\footnote{\href{https://www.airtec-gmbh.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Renting-an-industrial-furnace.pdf}{Technical data: AHT 890-ek tempering furnace} (accessed on 10.06.2026)}, which is a realistic time frame for the manufacturing and remains within the allocated manufacturing budget of \texteuro{45,000} \footnote{\label{url:baseline_report}\href{https://github.com/Teo-Cokonaj/DSE-28/blob/main/docs/Projects/DSE_Group_28_Baseline_Report.pdf}{Baseline Report} (accessed on 21.05.2026)}.



\subsubsection{\textbf{Foam wings}}

As shown in \autoref{tab:foam_props}, the selected foam core material is Rohacell 71 IG-F (PMI), which has a maximum service temperature of $180^\circ$C ($356^\circ$F). Since this temperature exceeds the maximum curing temperature considered in all three curing cycles, the foam core does not impose any constraints on the curing process. As Cycle A is the fastest and provides the best dimensional accuracy, it is selected for the curing process.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Honeycomb fuselage}}

The fuselage monocoque excludes the swappable tail cone. However, the tail cone will be manufactured using essentially the same process. Separate moulds will be produced for the upper and lower halves of the fuselage.

The mould manufacturing process begins with the machining of a male plug that reproduces the external geometry of the fuselage. This plug can be fabricated from an inexpensive polyurethane (PU) board, as it does not need to withstand elevated curing temperatures. Once machined, the plug is sealed and coated with a release agent to facilitate separation of the final mould.

A high-temperature epoxy tooling gelcoat is then applied to the prepared plug surface. This gelcoat provides the mould with a highly accurate surface finish, which ultimately determines the quality of the outer skin of the manufactured part. Subsequently, multiple layers of carbon fibre and epoxy fabric are laminated over the gelcoat. The mould laminate is built up to a thickness of approximately 5 mm and is reinforced with a stiffening framework structure to ensure adequate rigidity and prevent distortion or sagging during curing.

After lay-up, the mould laminate undergoes an initial low-temperature cure while still supported by the plug. Once cured sufficiently, the mould is demoulded and subjected to a freestanding post-cure cycle in the oven. This post-cure process increases the mould's glass transition temperature (T$_g$) to a value above the cure temperature of the composite parts ($350^\circ$F), ensuring dimensional stability and durability during production. The moulds will require only minimal post-processing, consisting of trimming excess material from the edges, sanding the surface, and applying the final sealer and release agent layers.

The two mould halves will incorporate split-mould clamping bushes to ensure precise alignment during assembly and to prevent any relative misalignment between the upper and lower sections. In addition, these bushes help reduce wear on the mould, provide sufficient clamping force, and maintain dimensional accuracy throughout the curing process.

Once the moulds have been completed, the manufacturing of the honeycomb fuselage can begin. Cycle A will also be applied to the manufacturing of the honeycomb fuselage and tail cone, since the carbon-fiber moulds can tolerate the elevated temperatures associated with the post-curing process.

The outer prepreg skin is laid up on the inner surface of the mould and cured. Although co-curing the complete sandwich in a single cycle is possible with 5320-1, pre-curing the outer skin was selected because it is the aerodynamically critical surface: this prevents honeycomb core print-through, locks the moulded geometry before core placement, and allows surface inspection before committing further material. The trade is one additional cure cycle per fuselage. 

The curing process is carried out as follows. On the moulds treated with a release agent, the prepreg material is first laid up. A peel ply is then applied as the final layer of the laminate. After curing, it can be removed to yield a clean, textured surface suitable for bonding, eliminating the need for additional abrasion or solvent cleaning. A perforated release film is subsequently placed over the peel ply, allowing controlled resin bleed into the breather while preventing the breather from bonding to the laminate.

A breather cloth is then applied to provide an air evacuation pathway. Continuous breather coverage is required to prevent local loss of vacuum within the bag. A vacuum bag film is placed over the entire assembly and sealed against sealant tape positioned along the mould flange. Finally, vacuum is applied through dedicated vacuum ports to consolidate the laminate during the curing cycle. After the part has cooled, the vacuum bag and breather are removed, followed by the peeling off of the peel ply. The cured outer skin is then ready for bonding to the honeycomb core. Suitable processing materials are again listed in the manufacturer's datasheet \footref{fn:cycom}.

Following inspection, the honeycomb core is then placed onto the outer skin with a layer of FM 309-1 film adhesive at the interface, and the inner prepreg skin is laid up over the core with a second layer of FM 309-1 between the core and inner skin. The entire assembly then undergoes a single co-bond cure, simultaneously curing the inner skin and the bondlines. A more detailed overview of how this is done is given in \autoref{sec:assembly}

Since the fuselage terminates at the aft opening where it interfaces with the tail cone, the honeycomb core remains exposed at this location. Consequently, the core edge must be sealed with a suitable filler material to protect it from moisture ingress and local impact damage. EPOCAST 1617 A/B has been selected for this purpose, as it is a low-density, self-extinguishing compound specifically developed for sealing the edges of honeycomb sandwich structures \footnote{\href{https://products.huntsman.com/products/epocast-1617-a-b-ixgqyzlv}{EPOCAST 1617 A/B} (accessed on 11.06.2026)}.

An important consideration is the joining of the two sections before curing. At the seam where the sandwich structures are joined, a potential weak point arises from discontinuities in both the prepreg skins and the honeycomb core. To reinforce the joint at the parting line, the outer skin of the lower half is extended, while the outer skin of the upper half is slightly recessed to create a lap joint. This lap-joint configuration increases the bonding area compared with a direct honeycomb-to-honeycomb joint, resulting in improved load transfer and joint strength. Additionally, the inner skin of the upper half also extends beyond the edge of the honeycomb core, allowing it to overlap with the inner skin of the lower half. This overlap facilitates the formation of a strong and continuous bond during the curing process. A more detailed overview of this step is given in \autoref{sec:assembly}.

\subsection{Landing gear and engine pylons}
\label{sub:manufacturing_LG}

The manufacturing of the landing gear and engine pylons remains the same as stated in the Midterm Report \footref{ft:midterm_rep}. A short overview of the process is given below.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{CNC Machining and Turning} shall be used to produce three-dimensional component profiles and axial geometries. Stress-relieved stock (e.g., T7651/T76511 plate or extrusions) shall be specified to maintain dimensional stability during and after machining.

\item \textbf{Deep Hole Drilling} shall be applied to long, slender components such as piston rods, outer cylinders, and axles where high dimensional accuracy and surface quality are required.

\item \textbf{Heat Treatment} shall be applied to relieve residual stresses and enhance strength. Depending on the component requirements, processes may include annealing, solution heat treatment, ageing, or homogenising.

\item \textbf{Surface Treatment and Protection} shall be applied to improve corrosion and wear resistance. Protective measures against galvanic corrosion at CFRP interfaces may include anodising, conversion coatings, primers, isolation layers, fastener isolation, or sacrificial anodes.

\end{enumerate}



\section{Assembly}
\label{sec:assembly}

\subsection{Fuselage assembly}
\label{sub:fuselage_assembly}

After curing the outer skins, the honeycomb core is bonded to them using a layer of adhesive film. The inner skins are then positioned on the opposite side of the honeycomb core with an additional layer of adhesive film. Once both halves are assembled and ready for Cycle A, a further layer of adhesive film is applied at the outer-skin lap joint. Another adhesive film layer is placed along the parting line over the honeycomb core to ensure proper bonding during the cure cycle.

The selected honeycomb adhesive is FM 410-1, as it enables honeycomb splicing at ambient pressure and offers a broad curing temperature range, up to $177^{\circ}$C. Furthermore, it is manufactured by the same supplier as the previously selected FM 309-1 adhesive, providing confidence in material compatibility within the bonding system.

The two mould halves are then joined and secured using the split-mould clamping bushes. An internal vacuum bag is inserted through the rear opening at the tail cone interface. To prevent bridging during evacuation, the selected vacuum bag has a diameter larger than that of the internal cavity, allowing it to expand freely and conform to the geometry. A self-releasing tubular vacuum bag is used, eliminating the need for peel ply or release film and allowing direct contact with the prepreg surface. The tubular bag is positioned inside the fuselage cavity, while the entire mould assembly is enclosed within a second external vacuum bag. Once vacuum is applied, air is removed from both bags, causing them to conform tightly to the laminate surfaces. This arrangement ensures that sufficient consolidation pressure is applied to the inner skin throughout the curing process \footnote{\href{https://www.easycomposites.eu/learning/split-mould-carbon-fibre-tube}{Laminating and Bagging a Carbon Fibre Tube Using a Split Mould} (accessed on 11.06.2026)}. Finally, the fuselage assembly undergoes a final curing cycle to join the two halves together.

\subsection{Pylon-to-fuselage connections}
\label{sub:pylon_to_fuselage}

The engine pylons will be attached to the fuselage sandwich structure through the use of cylindrical metal inserts that facilitate mechanical fastening and efficient load transfer. These inserts address the inherent limitations of composite cores, which often exhibit low through-thickness stiffness, by providing reinforced attachment points for bolts or other fasteners, thereby minimizing risks of delamination, core shear, or facesheet failure under tensile, shear, or pull-through loads \footnote{\href{https://grokipedia.com/page/insert_composites#ref-1}{Insert (composites)} (accessed on 11.06.2026)}.

The engine pylons will be attached to the fuselage sandwich structure by first drilling holes on the rear side of the fuselage where the engine pylons will go. Access for the drilling is available through the rear opening where the tailcone will be attached. Metallic sleeves spanning the full sandwich thickness are then inserted in the holes and bonded with EPOCAST 1652 A/B epoxy \footnote{\href{}{}}. The bolts will then clamp through the sleeve (not through the core), and wide-area washers are uesd spread the clamping load across the skin.

\subsection{Landing gear assembly}
\label{sub:lg_assembly}

The landing gear assembly shall use mechanical fasteners rather than bonded joints. Bolted and lockbolted joints shall be applied for rigid structural connections, while pin joints shall be used where relative motion is required, such as at torque-link and actuator attachment points. Welding is excluded, as it can alter the microstructure and reduce the mechanical properties of high-strength steel alloys.

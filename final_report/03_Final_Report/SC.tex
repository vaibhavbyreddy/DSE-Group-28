This chapter presents the structural design and analysis of HUGO. The focus is on translating the aerodynamic configuration and mission requirements into a structurally sound airframe capable of withstanding both operational and ultimate loading conditions, while ensuring stability, stiffness, and aeroelastic safety across the full flight envelope.

First, \autoref{sec:aerodynamic_characteristics_estimate} presents the aerodynamic characteristics estimate, including airfoil selection, lift and drag modelling, and the resulting lift distribution used as the basis for structural loading. Then, the wing structural response is analysed in \autoref{sec:wing_structural_analysis}, where discretisation, internal load paths, stresses, deflections, and failure criteria are evaluated under critical loading conditions. Subsequently, \autoref{sec:fuselage_structural_analysis} extends the structural assessment to the fuselage, modelling global bending, shear, torsion, and landing impact loads to determine required skin thickness and assess abuse-load resistance. Finally, \autoref{sec:flutter_analysis} addresses aeroelastic stability through a simplified flutter model, evaluating bending-torsion coupling, natural frequencies, damping behaviour, and the onset of flutter and divergence within the operational envelope.

\section{Aerodynamic Characteristics Estimate}
\label{sec:aerodynamic_characteristics_estimate}
The goals of the aerodynamic characteristics estimate is to obtain the lift and induced drag of the aircraft required for the later calculation of the $L/D$ ratio, the lift curve slopes of all the lifting surfaces (both individually and combined with other lifting surfaces), and the lift distribution along the span to be used for determining the structural loads. This section describes the airfoil selection along with methods used for lift and drag estimations.

\subsection{Airfoil Selection}
\label{sub:airfoil_selection}
High subsonic speeds experienced by HUGO during the nominal mission necessitate the use of a supercritical airfoil to avoid large drag penalties. This is due to shock waves and boundary layer separations induced by locally supersonic flows \cite{Whitcomb1974}. A preliminary sizing of the wing and tail surfaces performed in previous design phases identified NASA SC(2)-0012 (see: \autoref{fig:airfoil_simplified_model}) as a suitable airfoil choice for HUGO \cite{Harris1990}, based on the performance requirements and preliminary wing design lift coefficient of 0.074 \footref{foot:baseline}.

The structural model uses a simplified, elliptical airfoil approximation based on the SC(2)-0012 parameters. The wing cross-section is assumed to be hollow, with the same chord length and maximum thickness to chord ratio as the reference airfoil. The lift resultant is applied at the quarter-chord point, consistent with the typical location of the aerodynamic centre for subsonic airfoils \footnote{\href{http://airfoiltools.com/airfoil/details?airfoil=sc20012-il}{NASA SC(2)-0012 airfoil data} (accessed on 10.06.2026).}.

The elliptical approximation increases the estimated second moment of area compared with a direct airfoil-skin representation. However, the model neglects the structural contribution of the foam core. The resulting section properties are therefore treated as a conservative, simplified model that can be used for preliminary structural sizing.

\subsection{Lift Distribution}
\label{sub:lift}
The lift distribution was obtained by using the modified Schrenk lift distribution as described in \cite{EASALSALoadCalculation}. Essentially, the Schrenk lift distribution \cite{Schrenk1940} has been  modified by shifting the lift from the part of the wing which is within the fuselage towards the tip. This yields conservative results since the resulting bending moment is higher. The approach followed a methodology for obtaining load distributions for light aircraft proposed by EASA \cite{EASALSALoadCalculation}, in which the design condition implies the aircraft flying at its manoeuvre stall speed $V_{s_\text{n max}}$ compliant with the flight envelope \footref{foot:baseline}.

To achieve the desired load distribution, the planforms have been divided into $n$ individually evaluated sections. Schrenk's method assumes that the aerodynamic loading on the planform is equal to the average of the chord distribution, and an equivalent elliptical lift distribution, as shown in \autoref{eq:schrenk_element_lift} \cite[p. 20]{EASALSALoadCalculation}. Therefore, for each segment, an equivalent chord length $c_i$ has been defined based on the spanwise position $y_i$, and a corresponding lift increment $l_{\text{Schrenk},i}$ has been assigned. Sections incorporated into the fuselage were then excluded from the model by scaling the sections with respect to the average fuselage width at each section $b_0$ and the total wingspan $b$. The remaining lift was rescaled to compensate for the losses. 

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:schrenk_element_lift}
l_{\text{Schrenk},i} = \frac{1}{2}\rho V_{s_\text{n max}}^2C_{L_\text{max}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}
\left(c_i+\frac{4}{\pi}c_{\mathrm{MAC}}\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{y_i-\frac{b_0}{2}}
{\frac{b-b_0}{2}}\right)^2}\right)
\end{equation}

With $C_{L_{\text{max}}}$ being the wing positive maximum lift coefficient determined as $0.9C_{l_{\text{max}}}\cos\Lambda_{c/4}$ \cite[p. 153]{torenbeek2013synthesis}. The resulting lift distribution is presented in \autoref{fig:force_distribution} together with an analysis of the weight contribution. Applying the modified Schrenk's method is deemed as a conservative approach, as it is expected to slightly overestimate the loads acting on the planform root. It is additionally consistent with methodologies proposed by Raymer \cite{raymer1992aircraft} and Roskam \cite{roskamVI} for the design of subsonic to high-subsonic regime aircraft, and has been verified as sufficiently accurate to be used for the preliminary structural analysis of UAVs when compared to CFD methods \cite{Kamal2019}. It shall be noted, however, that the method is only accurate for planforms with low sweep angles, and does not account for complex aeroelastic phenomena, such as coupling, flutter, gusts, and load reliefs due to deformations. Therefore, the use of CFD-based models is recommended for the development of a detailed design.

The structural model of the wing was developed to simulate the internal loads and their associated deformations, i. e. the wing twist and tip deflection. An analysis of the flight envelope and related structural requirements (HUG-STR-10) has shown that HUGO shall be able to sustain ultimate load factors of up to 9 during manoeuvres. This edge case has been chosen as the point of analysis for the model, as it are expected to yield limiting load and stress values which can further be used for material selection and to verify compliance with design requirements. Ultimate loads represent an extreme case that the airframe must sustain rarely in its life. There are loads at which the wing will be more often exposed, at which it must comply with requirements regarding deflection and rotation of the wing tip. As stated by HUG-STR-07, HUG-STR-08 and HUG-STR-10, the tip must remain within the allowed constraints at and the wing shall not buckle a load factor of 6. With the exception of the wing tip deformations, the rest of the structural analysis is performed for the ultimate case scenario.

\subsection{Discretisation and Assumptions}
\label{sec:discretisation_and_assumptions}
As mentioned previously in \autoref{sec:aerodynamic_characteristics_estimate}, the wing airfoil was modelled as an ellipse, and the structure has been divided into panels which can be evaluated independently and superimposed to obtain span-wise load and stress distributions. Additionally, the wing is treated as a beam clamped at the root, and all deformations and deflections are calculated based on Euler–Bernoulli beam theory as a first-order approximation. It is assumed that the structure is a thin-walled carbon composite profile. These assumptions were deemed conservative, since they do not account for the additional stiffness provided by any core materials, since the wing incorporates a foam interior that is able to resist deformation and absorb part forces.
%\todo[inline]{Write about why the assumptions are acceptable}


Each wing segment has its own weight, lift and moment contribution. To compute the applied and internal loads along the entire wing, it is necessary to first evaluate the individual cross-section perimeter $P$ \autoref{eq:perim_area} \footnote{\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0506384}{Ramanujan’s Perimeter of an Ellipse} (accessed on 16.06.2026)}, as well as surface area and moment of inertia contributions, where $a$ and $b$ are the semi-major and semi-minor axes of the ellipse. 

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
h &= \left(\frac{a-b}{a+b}\right)^2
\qquad\qquad
P &\approx \pi(a+b)
\left[
1 + \frac{3h}{10 + \sqrt{4 - 3h}}
\right]
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:perim_area}
\end{equation}

The structural analysis is formulated with several free variables for parametric sizing, including the wing skin thickness. To determine the wing response under applied loads, the cross-sectional area of the thin-walled material section can be computed according to Niu \cite[p.~765]{niu1999airframe}, along with the second moment of area of a hollow ellipse.

\subsection{Internal Load Distrbution}
For the chosen airfoil, the torque is given by the combination of the lift produced by the wing and the aerodynamic moment of the airfoil itself. The torque is computed by multiplying the quarter chord length by the lift of the section, and then summing the contributions one by one until the root chord is reached \autoref{eq:t_sec_cumulative}, where $i$ is the previous segment.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:t_sec_cumulative}
\begin{aligned}
    T_{\mathrm{sec}} &= L_{\mathrm{sec}} \cdot \frac{\mathrm{\bar{c}}}{4}
    \qquad &
    T_{i+1} &= \sum_{j=0}^{i} T_j
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{0.5\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures_final/Wing_structure/Torque distribution on the wing Cruise.png}
    \caption{Torsion distribution at load factor 9}
    \label{fig:torsion_dist}
\end{wrapfigure}
At $1 \deg $ angle of attack, the airfoil has a moment coefficient that tends towards 0\footnote{\label{ft:NASA_0012}\href{http://airfoiltools.com/polar/details?polar=xf-sc20012-il-500000-n5}{NASA-0012} (accessed on 10.06.2026)}. As an airfoil is exposed to larger Mach speed regimes, the magnitude and position of $x_{cm}$ will shift due to compresibility effects, but for the purpose of this report, $x_{cm}$ is assumed to remain constant for both flight regimes at $x_{ac}$ location along the chord. As such, for the cruise and test conditions, the torque graphs will look the same. The most critical case for the torque of the wing comes from the first case specified at the beginning of the chapter at a load factor of 6, with the distribution presented in \autoref{fig:torsion_dist}.

Torque will influence the twist of the wing tip, but to determine the deflection of the wing it is first required to calculate the internal forces and moments acting on the wing. The wing is exposed to forces originating from the lift and weight, acting tangential to the chord, with the exception of the bending moments introduced by bending that will be treated in the following. The internal moment and shear forces acting on the cross-section of the wing are presented in \autoref{fig:internal_shear_and_moments}. It can be observed that the moment is opposite in sign with respect to the internal shear force due to the sign convention and direction of forced action on the wing.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures_final/Wing_structure/Internal Shear Force and Moment Diagrames.png}
    \caption{Internal shear force and moment spanwise distribution at loading factor 6}
    \label{fig:internal_shear_and_moments}
\end{figure}

Once the torque, internal moments and shears are computed and tested, it is time to verify the compatibility of the structure with the criteria specified in \autoref{ch:requirements}, namely HUG-STR-08 and HUF-STR-09 that refer to the wing tip deflection and deflection. The equation used to determine the twist follows from Megson\cite[p.~578]{megson2016aircraft}(Eq. 18.4) and requires the integration of the slope at each point to obtain the rotation. This equation assumes that the wing is a closed-sectioned beam, assumption that is valid in HUGOs' wings case since there are no ribs present for the moment to dictate their necessity. This equation also neglects the presence of normal shear stresses. Their presence reduces the twist in a real scenario due to their action of opposing the torque.

Determining the wing tip deflection required, to first compute the internal moment distribution, followed by the implementation of the equation extracted from Hibbeler\cite[p.~599]{Hibbeler2018}(Eq. 12-5), where is assumed that the deflection is linear and minimal and does not take into account the bending of the beam. As stated by R.C. Hibbeler "the slope of the elastic curve, which is determined from $\frac{dv}{dx}$, will be very small, and its square will be negligible compared with unity." \cite{Hibbeler2018}. By modelling the simulation using this assumption, the values are correct only for small deflections; larger cases require solving more complex equations, left for future development of HUGO.

Simulating the test case as specified by the requirements at a load factor of 6, this time, with the wing planform chosen for delivery, yields the results seen in \autoref{fig:rot_def}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures_final/Wing_structure/rotation_and_vertical_deflection.png}
    \caption{Wing tip rotation and deflection under the load factor 6 case}
    \label{fig:rot_def}
\end{figure}

During the extreme case, the wing twists $ 6.08 \deg$ and deflects 0.354 m ($11.1\% $ of the span). These values show that the wing is within limits, and that it could sustain even larger loads wile remaining in limits.

Once the wing has been shown to meet the design requirements, the model must also verify that it remains structurally safe during operation at the ultimate load factor. The applied loads generate internal stresses within the wing, which are evaluated for the ultimate loading case to confirm that the material stays within its allowable limits. The stresses considered in this analysis are torsional shear stress, transverse shear stress due to internal forces, and normal stress due to bending.

\begin{wrapfigure}[15]{r}{0.43\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures_final/Wing_structure/shear_stress_total.png}
    \caption{Shear stress distribution due to torque and internal shear forces at a 9 loading factor}
    \label{fig:shear_dist}
\end{wrapfigure}
Shear stresses caused by bending and shear can be summed into a net total shear acting on the internal cross-section. Torsional shear stresses and shear stresses caused by the internal loads have been calculated using \autoref{eq:shear_eq} taken from Megson\cite[p.~569]{megson2016aircraft}. 
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{torsion}= \frac{q}{t} = \frac{T}{2tA_m}
\end{equation}
\begin{alignat}{2}
q &= -\frac{V_y I_{yy}}{I_{xx}}\int_{0}^{s} t y  \mathrm{d}s' + q_{s0}
\qquad&
\tau_{internal loads} &= \frac{q}{t}
\label{eq:shear_eq}
\end{alignat}

After both the shear components are computed, they are summed, and the results can be seen in \autoref{fig:shear_dist}, reaching a maximum of  42.788 MPa. The material has a yield stress of 896 MPa (\autoref{tab:prepreg_properties}), a much larger value than the maximum shear stress present in the wing material; as such, this loading case will not cause a shear failure.

When the wing bends, the top section is in compression while the bottom section in tension, the internal moments from the forces induce a normal stress in the material. To compute its distribution, \autoref{eq:normal_stress} was used from Megson\cite[p.~496]{megson2016aircraft}, whitch represents a simplified version of equation 16.17.

\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \frac{M \cdot y_{max}}{I_x}
    \label{eq:normal_stress}
\end{equation}

\begin{wrapfigure}[15]{r}{0.43\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures_final/Wing_structure/normal_stress.png}
    \caption{Normal stress distribution due to forces acting on the wing at 9 loading factor}
    \label{fig:normal_dist}
\end{wrapfigure}

The normal stress distribution shown in \autoref{fig:normal_dist} decreases towards the wing root, where the wing is integrated into the fuselage. This behaviour occurs because the bending moment becomes approximately constant once the load-bearing wing section enters the fuselage. At the same time, the cross-sectional area increases towards the root, which increases the second moment of area and therefore reduces the bending stress. The maximum normal stress is 696.121 MPa and occurs at the first connection point between the wing and the fuselage. This value remains below the yield stress of the material.


The last analysis considered verifies that the wing does not buckle to a load factor of 6. The ultimate load is not tested because buckling is not considered a structural critical case, the wing being able to buckle at certain loads. It is considered a structural problem when the phenomenon becoms catastrophic. Composite materials are known to be brittle and lose their structural properties once they buckle. To prevent a loss of integrity, the model tests if the wing buckles by comparing the buckling stress with the maximum internal shear stress. Is a force that exceeds the maximum load factor of 6 is reached, the wing will lose part of its properties, but will still be able to return the UAV for maintenance. 
HUGO has a ribless design, its wings relying on the thickness of the skin for the structural rigidity, as such it can be treated as a thin plate, and \autoref{eq:buckling} extracted from Dowell\cite[p.~458]{niu1999airframe} is used to calculate the buckling stress. Constant $C$ is a function of the boundary conditions and the aspect ratio of the plate. For additional prevention, the wing is considered to be simply supported on all sides, and, looking at the table 11.3.1 in Dowell\cite[p.~458]{niu1999airframe}, for b being the length of the half-span of the wing and a the chord length at the root (location with the height stress), C is 4. The remaining of the variables are taken from the material properties in \autoref{ch:material_characteristics}.

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{cr} = C \frac{\pi^2 E}{12(1-\nu^2)} \left(\frac{t}{b}\right)^2
    \label{eq:buckling}
\end{equation}

For the current wing, the buckling stress is 475.249 MPa, while the largest internal stress in the wing during the extreme manoeuvre is 461.249 MPa (using the same simulation environment as above but at a loading factor of 6).
Once the wing has been verified to comply with the requirements and to resist the most demanding load case, the fuselage has to be designed to accommodate the testing platforms.

\section{Fuselage structural analysis}
\label{sec:fuselage_structural_analysis}
The structural analysis of the fuselage has been conducted by modelling the fuselage as a beam and considering the most constraining loading cases. Namely, the pull-up manoeuvre at the maximum load factor from the flight envelope, and the main landing gear impact for maximum vertical deceleration. For both of these, the fuselage has been discretised into $n$ nodes, with each node being assigned a mass based on the estimated positions and masses (both structural and non-structural) of different components. The lift force of the canard was assumed to be a fraction of the total lift force (\autoref{eq:canard_lift_fraction}).

\begin{equation}
    L_c=f\cdot L_{tot}
    \label{eq:canard_lift_fraction}
\end{equation}

In the first case, the aircraft is assumed to be accelerating vertically due to a load factor $n$ and remaining in moment equilibrium.
The free-body and kinetic diagrams of the fuselage in flight, assuming detached canard and wing, are shown in \autoref{fig:fuselage_fbd_kd_flight}. The equations of motion are then

\begin{equation}
     \tikz[baseline=-0.6ex]{\draw[->] (0,-0.25) -- (0,0.25);}
    \quad
    \Sigma F: L_c+L_w+L_t-\Sigma_{i=0}^n m_i g = \Sigma_{i=0}^n m_i a_{i}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \tikz[baseline=-0.6ex]{\draw[->] (0,0) arc (-40:220:0.18)} \, \Sigma M_{x=0}: \quad -M_c - M_w + x_c L_c + x_w L_w + x_t L_t -\Sigma_{i=0}^n x_i m_i g  = \Sigma_{i=0}^n x_i m_i a_i
\end{equation}

where $a_i=a_{cg}=(n-1)g$.

\begin{equation}
    \tikz[baseline=-0.6ex]{\draw[->] (0,0.25) -- (0,-0.25);}
    \quad
    V(x_i)=\Sigma_{j=0}^i \left(F_{\text{applied},j}-m_j g-m_j a_j\right)
\end{equation}

and the internal bending moment as 

\begin{equation}
    \tikz[baseline=-0.6ex]{\draw[->] (0,0) arc (220:-40:0.18)} \quad M(x_i)=\Sigma_{j=0}^i(M_{\text{applied},j}+(x_i-x_j)(F_{\text{applied},j}-m_j(g+a(x_j))))
\end{equation}

Additionally, the torque on the fuselage due to the side force on the vertical tail has been accounted for by using

\begin{equation}

Subsequently, the internal normal and shear stresses have been calculated by superimposing the internal loads and assuming a thin-walled circular cross-section. Since this section is doubly symmetric, the normal stress due to internal bending moment $M$, the shear stress due to internal transverse shear force $V$, and the shear stress due to internal torque $T$ were found using \autoref{eq:stresses_fuselage} \cite{roark1966formulas}. Standard formulas for properties of thin-walled circular and semi-circular sections from \cite{roark1966formulas} and \cite{bruhn1973analysis} have been used.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{\text{bending}} &= \frac{My}{I}
\qquad
\tau_{\text{transverse shear}} &= \frac{VQ}{It}
\qquad
\tau_{\text{torque, max}} &= \frac{2Tr_o}{\pi (r_o^4-r_i^4)}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:stresses_fuselage}
\end{equation}

The calculated stresses were subsequently used to determine the minimum thickness by imposing maximum allowed normal and shear stresses associated with yield, fracture, and buckling. The critical normal buckling stress was calculated using \autoref{eq:buckling_fuselage_global} \cite{roark1966formulas} with a knockdown factor shown in \autoref{eq:knockdown_buckling} \cite{NASA_SP8007_2019}.

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{crit, buckling}}=\frac{Et}{\sqrt{3(1-\nu^2)}r}
    \label{eq:buckling_fuselage_global}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \gamma = 1-0.901(1-e^{-\frac{1}{16}\sqrt{\frac{R}{t}}})
    \label{eq:knockdown_buckling}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Abuse Loads}
Given the very low thickness set by manufacturing constraints, it became critical to analyse how the skin of the fuselage monocoque would withstand handling loads. These loads are commonly known as abuse loads. In the case of HUGO, it was assumed that the most constraining load would be that of an operator lifting and carrying the aircraft, applying a force equal to half of the weight of the aircraft at the position of each of the operator's hands. The cross-sectional deflection of the simplified cylindrical fuselage is then estimated using \autoref{eq: roark_ring_deflection} \cite{roark1966formulas}.

\begin{equation}
    \Delta D_V = -0.1488 \frac{WR^3}{EI}
    \label{eq: roark_ring_deflection}
\end{equation}

For the solid carbon configuration, the area moment of inertia (I) is calculated using the standard formula for a rectangular cross-section, \autoref{eq:inertia_solid}.
\begin{equation}
    I_{\text{solid}} = \frac{L t^3}{12}
    \label{eq:inertia_solid}
\end{equation}
where $t$ is the total thickness of the solid CFRP skin.

In the case of this analysis, the maximum deflection of the ring was set to 1mm. The area moment of inertia of the walls of the circular ring is given by the length of the fuselage segment where the operator places their hand, and the thickness of the skin. For this analysis, 150 mm was selected as the most constraining length of fuselage segment where the operator might place their hand, as it can be found in between the forward access hatch and the main wing port. This would be most constraining section of the fuselage. This analysis resulted in a minimum required skin thickness of 6.4mm of CFRP layup, with a weight of 9.47 kg/m of fuselage length, using a load of half the weight of the aircraft, with a safety factor of 1.5. This yielded a force of 353N.

Given the stark increase in skin thickness and consequently, fuselage weight, it becomes reasonable to analyse the drone monocoque as a composite sandwich, with CFRP on the outer layers and a Nomex honeycomb as the core, as described in \autoref{sec:composites}. For the composite sandwich configuration, the area moment of inertia is dominated by the CFRP skins. Assuming the Nomex core has negligible bending stiffness compared to the carbon faces, the equivalent moment of inertia is calculated using the parallel axis theorem. The local moment of inertia of the thin skins is omitted, yielding \autoref{eq:inertia_solid}.
\begin{equation}
    I_{\text{sandwich}} \approx 2 \left( L t_s \left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^2 \right) = \frac{L t_s d^2}{2}
    \label{eq:inertia_sandwich}
\end{equation}
where $t_s$ is the thickness of a single CFRP skin, $t_c$ is the thickness of the Nomex core, and $d = t_c + t_s$ is the distance between the midplanes of the two skins.

\section{Flutter Analysis}
\label{sec:flutter_analysis}
Since the aircraft has to reach the required maximum speed in a safe way, it has to be ensured that no aeroelastic instabilities occur before this speed. Hence, a preliminary flutter analysis has been performed by using the binary aeroelastic model as described in \cite{wright2008introduction}. 

The model assumes the wing to be a cantilevered beam with first bending ($q_b$) and first torsion ($q_t$) as degrees of freedom and with elastic axis at 50\% of the chord. The general aeroelastic equations of motions for determining the stability of the system are given by \autoref{eq:general_aeroelastic_eqs} \cite{wright2008introduction}:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}\,\ddot{\mathbf{q}}
+(\rho V \mathbf{B}+\mathbf{D})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}
+(\rho V^2 \mathbf{C}+\mathbf{E})\,\mathbf{q}
=\mathbf{0}
\label{eq:general_aeroelastic_eqs}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{C}$, $\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{E}$ are structural mass, aerodynamic damping and stiffness, and structural damping and stiffness matrices, respectively. Lagrange's equations were used to derive the matrices. The flutter diagrams were then created by calculating the eigenvalues of the system at a range of flight conditions (altitudes and airspeeds) and plotting the frequencies and damping ratios of the two modes. 


The mass and stiffness matrices, $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{E}$, are obtained from the kinetic and potential energy, represented by \autoref{eq: kinetic_energy} and \autoref{eq: potential_energy}, respectively \cite{wright2008introduction}.

\begin{equation}
T = \int_{\text{Wing}} \frac{1}{2} dm \dot{z}^2 = \frac{m}{2} \int_{0}^{s} \int_{0}^{c} \left( \left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^2 \dot{q}_b + \left(\frac{y}{s}\right)(x - x_f) \dot{q}_t \right)^2 dx dy 
\label{eq: kinetic_energy}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
U = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{s} EI \left(\frac{2q_b}{s^2}\right)^2 \mathrm{d}y + \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{s} GJ \left(\frac{q_t}{s}\right)^2 \mathrm{d}y
\label{eq: potential_energy}
\end{equation}

Lagrange's equation is then applied for both the bending and torsion degrees of freedom, according to \citeauthor{wright2008introduction} \cite{wright2008introduction}. However, their solutions to the resulting integrals were not employed in this analysis, since HUGO's wing is tapered. This makes the chord of the wing a function of span. Thus, in the case of kinetic energy, numerical integration was performed for each chord length along the span of the half wing. Regarding the potential energy, the contribution of chord length to the area moment of inertia $\mathbf{I}$ is also numerically integrated along the span. The resulting mass matrix is \autoref{eq: A_matrix}, and the resulting stiffness matrix is shown by \autoref{eq: E_matrix}.

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 
m \int_{0}^{s} \left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^4 c \, \mathrm{d}y & 
m \int_{0}^{s} \left( \left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^3 \frac{c^2}{2} - \left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^3 x_f c \right) \mathrm{d}y \\[1.5em]
m \int_{0}^{s} \left( \left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^3 \frac{c^2}{2} - \left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^3 x_f c \right) \mathrm{d}y & 
m \int_{0}^{s} \left( \left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^2 \frac{c^3}{3} - \left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^2 c^2 x_f + \left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^2 x_f^2 c \right) \mathrm{d}y 
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq: A_matrix}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{E} = \begin{bmatrix} 
\int_{0}^{s} \frac{4EI}{s^4} \, \mathrm{d}y & 
0 \\[1.5em]
0 & 
\int_{0}^{s} \frac{GJ}{s^2} \, \mathrm{d}y 
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq: E_matrix}
\end{equation}

The structural damping matrix $\mathbf{D}$ has been obtained by employing \autoref{eq:Rayleigh_damping} with coefficients $\alpha$ and $\beta$ as defined by \citeauthor{hellen2013finite} \cite{hellen2013finite}. The damping ratios of $1\%$ critical per mode were used \cite{wright2008introduction}.

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{D} = \alpha \mathbf{A} +\beta \mathbf{E}
    \label{eq:Rayleigh_damping}
\end{equation}

Furthermore, the aerodynamic matrices have been derived by applying strip theory to the unsteady aerodynamic forces on an oscillating airfoil \cite{wright2008introduction}. This has been simplified by only retaining the pitch damping oscillatory aerodynamic derivative as a constant value of $-1.2$, which is a valid assumption for a wide range of reduced frequencies when the elastic axis is at $50\%$ of the chord \cite{hancock1985teaching, wright2008introduction}. This greatly simplified the aerodynamic matrices, as it made them uncoupled from reduced frequency. Additionally, the Prandtl-Glauert compressibility correction and sweep correction have been applied to aerodynamic matrices.

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{B} =
\frac{\cos\Lambda_{c/4}}{\sqrt{1-M^2}}
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{a_w}{2}
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}
\left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^4 c \,\mathrm{d}y
&
0
\\[1.5em]
-\dfrac{e a_w}{2}
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}
\left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^3 c^2 \,\mathrm{d}y
&
-\dfrac{M_{\dot{\theta}}}{8}
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}
\left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^2 c^3 \,\mathrm{d}y
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:B_matrix}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C} =
\frac{\cos\Lambda_{c/4}}{\sqrt{1-M^2}}
\begin{bmatrix}
0
&
\dfrac{a_w}{2}
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}
\left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^3 c \,\mathrm{d}y
\\[1.5em]
0
&
-\dfrac{e a_w}{2}
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}
\left(\frac{y}{s}\right)^2 c^2 \,\mathrm{d}y
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:C_matrix}
\end{equation}

In summary, this chapter has developed a complete structural representation of HUGO by linking aerodynamic loading, internal load transfer, and material response across both wing and fuselage components. Through the above-mentioned modelling approaches, the critical structural responses have been quantified in terms of deflection, stress distribution, and aeroelastic stability, ensuring that the airframe remains within allowable limits under both operational and ultimate loading conditions. In particular, the analyses confirm that the wing and fuselage designs satisfy strength, stiffness, and flutter requirements within the defined flight envelope, while also identifying the governing cases for structural sizing.

With the structural integrity and aeroelastic safety of the aircraft established, the next step is to evaluate how these design choices translate into overall aircraft performance. The following chapter therefore focuses on the performance analysis of HUGO, assessing key mission-level metrics such as flight profile capabilities, range and fuel requirements, matching diagram constraints, and noise and environmental emissions.

The centre of gravity margin must be taken into account to meet the stability performance requirements. Specific location of the CG is driven by HUGO's external configuration, as well as the internal layout (i. e. electronics, propulsion system, payload). HUGO is expected to accommodate a wide variety of wing planforms, tail cones, and payload configurations, as well as operate in both stable and unstable regimes. As such, no dedicated CG range budget is defined. Rather, a CG analysis shall be performed for each configuration case to ensure that desired stability characteristics are achieved.

The determination of the CG for a given configuration is done by means of segmentation. In this approach, the CG of the fuselage is isolated from the rest of the components, and can be used as a first estimate for wing sizing. The final position of the CG can be adjusted during design iterations by distributing the electronics and propulsion system components inside the aircraft. \subsection{Communication Link Budget}
\label{sub:communication_link_budget}
Based on HUGO's mission profile, specifically the maximum flight speed and maximum distance from the aerodrome, a C2 communication link must be specified as part of the Type Design \cite{SC Light-UAS}. This custom protocol is required along with an efficient transmitter/receiver combination due to the Doppler shift, which becomes significant at the airspeed of 0.75 Mach. the radio waves will experience the Doppler effect. Using the Giancoli's formula for the Doppler shift \cite[p.477]{giancoli2020physics}, is can be estimated that the shifted frequency may attain values as high as four times its initial transmission counterpart (if HUGO performs the high-speed test flight in the direction of the observer), or as low as 57\% (if flying away from the observer).

C2 is a custom-made protocol that allows for bidirectional communication at such speeds and for a smooth video and radio transmission \footnote{\href{https://eudroneport.com/blog/c2-links/}{C2 Communication Link} (accessed on 03.06.2026)}. It shall be designed to fit the considered test cases. For HUGO, the C2 is paired with the electronic system, the design of which will be discussed in \autoref{sec:internal_configuration}. Appropriate radionavigation and landing equipment for the receiving end of C2 communication is available at Den Helder aerodrome \footnote{\href{https://eaip.lvnl.nl/web/eaip/AIRAC\%20AMDT\%2005-2026_2026_05_14/eAIP/EH-AD\%202\%20EHKD\%201-en-GB.html}{EHKD AD 2.19 Radio navigation and landing aids} (accessed 03.06.2026)}. \subsection{Landing Gear Design}
\label{sub:landing_gear_design}

The landing gear design is an important component of the external configuration. It is important for taxiing, take-off, and landing, and must be able to withstand landing conditions. Furthermore, it must be stowed away during cruise to minimize the area exposed to the airflow to reduce drag. Airplane Design by Roskam has guidelines to design landing gear for aircraft. These include the scrape angle, turn over angle, wing tip clearance, and total mass on the main versus front landing gear.

HUGO's modular design requires an in-depth analysis of the landing gear positioning as certain landing gear positions would lie outside of the acceptable design space. For example, the modular wing and tail cannot house the landing gear as prospective clients would then need to design landing gear mechanisms, slowing down testing and reducing HUGO's commercial attractiveness. 

To model the landing gear in the preliminary design phase, the fuselage was modelled as a cylinder with three sections; the nose cone, the main fuselage, and the tail cone. The tail cone was additionally modelled as a cylinder with different diameter circles as the base connected to the main fuselage, and the smaller diameter circle in the most aft part of the tail cone. Then, the FlexHop aircraft as a reference for the sizing of the simplified fuselage. Then, using SciPy's Sequential Least Squares Programming (SLSQP) optimizer, the position of the nose and main landing gears could be found to meet the necessary constraints (listed in \autoref{tab:lg_constraints}).

The optimiser could vary the longitudinal position of the nose and main landing gear, as well as the horizontal separation of the main landing gear ($Y_{lg}$), and the landing gear length ($l_{lg}$). The objective was to meet all the constraints while minimizing the main landing gear separation (as measured from the midline to the main landing gear), as well as the landing gear height (measured from the centreline of the fuselage). If these were not minimised, the landing gear length could become unbounded, requiring larger and larger horizontal separations, which the fuselage could not accommodate. The chosen objective function can be seen in \autoref{eq:objective_func}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective_func}
    \sqrt{l_{lg}^2 + Y_{lg}^2}
\end{equation}

To accommodate the compression, the landing gear will utilise a spring and dashpot in parallel.

During landing, the main landing gear must be able to withstand the impact loads. The EASA guidelines were originally interpereted to require a high risk UAS to withstand a 4g load. Later, it was determined that the requirement was simply for the structure to withstand 1.5 times the load transfered by the landing gear. Due to time constraints, the optimal trade off between loads withstood by the fuselage versus the landing gear could not be explored, but should be looked into in further design. To meet both force and displacement constraints, the landing gear were sized to withstand a 4g load shared equally by both landing gear, damping an impact of HUGO travelling at 36 m/s at a glide slope of 3 degrees. To allow for the absorption of the impact loads, the landing gear were modelled as a spring and dashpot in parallel with constants K and C respectively. C and K can then be tuned such that the maximum vertical displacement of the landing gear does not collide with the keel, and such that a 4g impact is not exceeded. This means not exceeding a compression of 7.75 cm, and a peak force of 0.98 kN. 

Using the python control library, the landing gear displacement and force responses to the aforementioned loads could be simulated. Using the spring and dashpot in parallel the following state space can be constructed:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}
    = \begin{bmatrix} \text{displacement} \\ \text{velocity} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
    \mathbf{u}(t) = \left(g - \frac{L}{m}\right) \cdot \mathbf{1}(t)
\end{equation}

\begin{align}
    \dot{\mathbf{x}} &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -k/m & -c/m \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}
    + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{u} \label{eq:state_equation}\\[6pt]
    \mathbf{y} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ k & c \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}
    + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{u} \label{eq:output_equation}
\end{align}

where \autoref{eq:output_equation} represents the displacement and force respectively, L is the lift at landing, m is the mass on the landing gear, and g is the acceleration due to gravity.

As previously mentioned, the displacement and force constraints will be applied to the state space model. Additionally, the system is also constrained to never be over-damped. If the system were over-damped, the spike in initial force through the landing gear would be too large. Similarly, if the damping is too low, HUGO will oscillate on the runway after landing. By varying the spring and dashpot constants, the landing gear can be tuned to meet all requirements.

\subsection{Environmental Control}
\label{sub:environmental_control}
Environmental control is the response of HUGO to different external factors such as rain or UV-radiation. An analysis was established were different flight conditions have been considered, and it was determined that the critical case for HUGO come from the presence of ice on the wings due to the low temperatures. 

As supercooled water hits the fuselage, it instantly turns into ice, increasing the weight \cite{andreev2014icing} and damaging the aerodynamic profile. Since the wings fitted on HUGO are very thin, optimised for high cruise speed, any perturbation of the aerodynamic profile may result in a catastrophic event. 

There are two primary way to combat this phenomenon, either by using a passive system (coating or phase changing materials) or b an active system (heating elements). Since the wings are subjected to regular replacement and modifications, it is more efficient to use a passive system for HUGO. 

A coating that could help HUGO delay the formation of ice on the wings involves coating them with a poly-siloxane elastomer coating that sticks to resin \cite{facco2025passive}. It delays the formation of ice on the wings as well as offering a very low ice adhesion, effectively protecting the wing from ice. Despite its weak mechanical properties, HUGO is not meant to fly for many missions with the same set of wings, as such it does not require withstanding a lot of cycles.

The electrical system of HUGO is designed to provide continuous power to the sensors, actuators, and propulsion system throughout the mission. It must support a range of testing configurations, enabling different experimental setups to be evaluated during flight. As a central element of the aircraft architecture, the system must be reliable, efficiently packaged, and dimensioned to fit within the available internal volume while minimising interference with the onboard test setup.An initial configuration of the electrical system was configured in the previous design stage\footref{ft:midterm_rep} that was used as a starting point. 

From \autoref{tab:electric_comp} it can be seen that there are a lot of electric components that are mission-critical. For the purpose of this application, autopilot VECTOR-600 was chosen as the subsystem control unit, incorporating many of the basic air sensors needed to conduct the flight. Working along a flight companion(FC), that processes all the information from the sensors as well as from the autopilot, storing the data in a memory unit use it for flight calculations and ultimately transmits it back to the base of operations. Additionally, this autopilot has been qualified by the industry, being tested against vibrations and crash loads. It incorporates: Dual Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), redundant inertial sensing for altitude, magnetometer, gyroscope, inertial magnetometer, altimeter, airspeed sensor, Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) receiver for gps signal. The signal and power distribution of the electric system is evident in \autoref{fig:electronics_mapping}. 

The autopilot communicates directly with the electronic control unit (ECU) of the engine system, providing the required speed commands and operating modes. The ECU also communicates with the companion computer, allowing engine-condition data to be stored for later processing by the experimental team. In this architecture, the flight controller coordinates the onboard communication flow and ensures that control and monitoring signals remain available during the high-speed testing phase.

For the external communication link, the RM 3000 Series communication unit was selected as a suitable component for HUGO, as it can operate in either the very high frequency (VHF) or ultra-high frequency (UHF) bands. VHF and UHF are considered because they are standard frequency ranges for aviation communication systems \footnote{\href{https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/FSMP/Doc.9718-Vol-I-AdvanceUneditedVersion-2021.pdf}{ICAO Doc 9718: Handbook on Radio Frequency Spectrum Requirements for Civil Aviation} (accessed on 15.06.2026)}. Since HUGO has a maximum operating range of 300 km, VHF operation is preferred because it offers better range performance for a given transmit power and is widely used for aviation communication. Den Helder Airport is rated for commercial aviation operations and is therefore expected to have the infrastructure required for communication in aviation VHF bands.

In \autoref{fig:electronics_mapping} it can be seen that the battery does not directly supply the components, but a power distribution unit (PDU) is responsible for power allocation. For HUGO, the chosen unit operates at minimum 24 V\footnote{\href{https://www.brancaro.com/product/power-distribution-unit/}{Power Distribution Unit} (accessed 02.06.2026)} input, and has multiple ports to supply power at different voltages.

Once all the components of the electronic system essential for flying and testing have been brought together, their individual power consumption and weight are computed \autoref{tab:electric_comp}. The total power consumed by all the electronic components, including the sensor payload represent the power budget that is used to size the battery. HUGO will only operate for a maximum of 45 minutes in the most extreme scenario, utilising a 846.06 W \autoref{tab:electronic_system} of power. This is a power requirement, and it represents the base value and an additional $10\%$ power increase to account for any error margins or extra usage from the components. After an analysis of possible sensor platforms, it was determined that the power consumed by the payload is estimated at 50 W.

With the total required power estimated at approximately 900 W and a minimum bus voltage of 24 V, the battery pack can be sized. To reduce packaging volume and allow the pack geometry to be adapted to HUGO, a custom battery pack is selected using Molicel INR21700 cells\footnote{\href{https://www.nkon.nl/en/molicel-inr21700-m65a-6500mah-26a.html}{Molicel INR21700} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}.

An 8-cell series configuration provides a nominal voltage of 28.8 V. To meet the required current demand, five cells are placed in parallel, resulting in an 8S5P configuration. At the nominal voltage, the required current for a 900 W load is approximately 31.3 A. This corresponds to about 6.3 A per cell, which is within the operating capability of the selected cells, at 6.5 A. The resulting pack can therefore supply approximately 936 W at nominal voltage, leaving a small power margin above the estimated requirement.

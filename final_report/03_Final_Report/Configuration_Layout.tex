The centre of gravity margin must be taken into account to meet the stability performance requirements. Specific location of the CG is driven by HUGO's external configuration, as well as the internal layout (i.e. electronics, propulsion system, payload). HUGO is expected to accommodate a wide variety of wing planforms, tail cones, and payload configurations, as well as operate in both stable and unstable regimes. As such, no dedicated CG range budget is defined. Rather, a CG analysis shall be performed for each configuration case to ensure that desired stability characteristics are achieved.

The determination of the CG for a given configuration is done by means of segmentation. In this approach, the CG of the fuselage is isolated from the rest of the components, and can be used as a first estimate for wing sizing. The final position of the CG can be adjusted during design iterations by distributing the electronics and propulsion system components inside the aircraft. For test cases focused on stability and requiring an in-flight shift in CG, its position can be adjusted by varying the fuel distribution between two bladders. The details of the propulsion system design will be further discussed in \autoref{sec:internal_configuration}.

\subsection{Communication Link Budget}
\label{sub:communication_link_budget}
Based on HUGO's mission profile, specifically the maximum flight speed and maximum distance from the aerodrome, a C2 communication link must be specified as part of the Type Design \cite{easa_light_uas_crd_is3}. This custom protocol is required along with an efficient transmitter/receiver combination due to the Doppler shift, which becomes significant at the airspeed of 0.75 Mach. the radio waves will experience the Doppler effect. Using the Giancoli's formula for the Doppler shift \cite[p.477]{giancoli2020physics}, is can be estimated that the shifted frequency may attain values as high as four times its initial transmission counterpart (if HUGO performs the high-speed test flight in the direction of the observer), or as low as 57\% (if flying away from the observer).

C2 is a custom-made protocol that allows for bidirectional communication at such speeds and for a smooth video and radio transmission \footnote{\href{https://eudroneport.com/blog/c2-links/}{C2 Communication Link} (accessed on 03.06.2026)}. It shall be designed to fit the considered test cases. For HUGO, the C2 is paired with the electronic system, the design of which will be discussed in \autoref{sec:internal_configuration}. Appropriate radionavigation and landing equipment for the receiving end of C2 communication is available at Den Helder aerodrome \footnote{\href{https://eaip.lvnl.nl/web/eaip/AIRAC\%20AMDT\%2005-2026_2026_05_14/eAIP/EH-AD\%202\%20EHKD\%201-en-GB.html}{EHKD AD 2.19 Radio navigation and landing aids} (accessed 03.06.2026)}. The specific communication link budget is defined by the electronics system design and will be discussed later.

The electrical system of HUGO is designed to provide continuous power to the sensors, actuators, and propulsion system throughout the mission. It must support a range of testing configurations, enabling different experimental setups to be evaluated during flight. As a central element of the aircraft architecture, the system must be reliable, efficiently packaged, and dimensioned to fit within the available internal volume while minimising interference with the onboard test setup. An initial configuration of the electrical system was configured in the previous design stage \footref{foot:midterm} that was used as a starting point. 

From \autoref{tab:electric_comp} it can be seen that there are a lot of electric components that are mission-critical. For the purpose of this application, autopilot VECTOR-600 was chosen as the subsystem control unit, incorporating many of the basic air sensors needed to conduct the flight. Working along a flight companion (FC), that processes all the information from the sensors as well as from the autopilot, storing the data in a memory unit use it for flight calculations and ultimately transmits it back to the base of operations. Additionally, this autopilot has been qualified by the industry, being tested against vibrations and crash loads. It incorporates: Dual Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), redundant inertial sensing for altitude, magnetometer, gyroscope, inertial magnetometer, altimeter, airspeed sensor, Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) receiver for gps signal. Storage of the flight data is provided by a 1 TB storage device system that is removable. The signal and power distribution of the electric system is evident in \autoref{fig:electronics_mapping}. 

The autopilot communicates directly with the electronic control unit (ECU) of the engine system, providing the required speed commands and operating modes. The ECU also communicates with the companion computer, allowing engine-condition data to be stored for later processing by the experimental team. In this architecture, the flight controller coordinates the onboard communication flow and ensures that control and monitoring signals remain available during the high-speed testing phase.

For the external communication link, the RM 3000 Series communication unit was selected as a suitable component for HUGO, as it can operate in either the very high frequency (VHF) or ultra-high frequency (UHF) bands. VHF and UHF are considered because they are standard frequency ranges for aviation communication systems \footnote{\href{https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/FSMP/Doc.9718-Vol-I-AdvanceUneditedVersion-2021.pdf}{ICAO Doc 9718: Handbook on Radio Frequency Spectrum Requirements for Civil Aviation} (accessed on 15.06.2026)}. Since HUGO has a maximum operating range of 300 km, VHF operation is preferred because it offers better range performance for a given transmit power and is widely used for aviation communication. Den Helder Airport is rated for commercial aviation operations and is therefore expected to have the infrastructure required for communication in aviation VHF bands.

In \autoref{fig:electronics_mapping} it can be seen that the battery does not directly supply the components, but a power distribution unit (PDU) is responsible for power allocation. For HUGO, the chosen unit operates at minimum 24 V \footnote{\href{https://www.brancaro.com/product/power-distribution-unit/}{Power Distribution Unit} (accessed 02.06.2026)} input, and has multiple ports to supply power at different voltages.

Once all the components of the electronic system essential for flying and testing have been brought together, their individual power consumption and weight are computed \autoref{tab:electric_comp}. The total power consumed by all the electronic components, including the sensor payload represent the power budget that is used to size the battery. HUGO will only operate for a maximum of 45 minutes in the most extreme scenario, utilising 846.06 W of power (\autoref{tab:electric_comp}). This is a power requirement, and it represents the base value and an additional $10\%$ power increase to account for any error margins or extra usage from the components. After an analysis of possible sensor platforms, it was determined that the power consumed by the payload is estimated at 50 W.

With the total required power estimated at approximately 900 W and a minimum bus voltage of 24 V, the battery pack can be sized. To reduce packaging volume and allow the pack geometry to be adapted to HUGO, a custom battery pack is selected using Molicel INR21700 cells\footnote{\href{https://www.nkon.nl/en/molicel-inr21700-m65a-6500mah-26a.html}{Molicel INR21700} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}.

An 8-cell series configuration provides a nominal voltage of 28.8 V. To meet the required current demand, five cells are placed in parallel, resulting in an 8S5P configuration. At the nominal voltage, the required current for a 900 W load is approximately 31.3 A. This corresponds to about 6.3 A per cell, which is within the operating capability of the selected cells, at 6.5 A. The resulting pack can therefore supply approximately 936 W at nominal voltage, leaving a small power margin of 36W above the estimated requirement, which can be utilised by additional systems brought by customers.

\subsection{Sensor Interaction}
\label{sub:sensor_interaction}
To measure and record the results from the experiments, it is necessary to use sensors. Since HUGO features versatility for a specific customer's needs, the electrical system was designed with a margin to make it possible to bring additional sensors.       
Using the mission envelope  (\autoref{sec:mission_envelope}), it is possible to identify the following phenomenon, which needs to be measured: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Deflection and twist: This is how much the wing is moved and rotated relative to the original axis. There are several approaches for the measurement of wing deflection. A way to measure the deflection is to record it from a fixed point on the aircraft.

    \item Strain: A strain gauge is used for measuring strain. It uses the changed resistance in a wire when mechanically deformed \footnote{\href{https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/strain-gauge}{Strain gauge} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}. A typical configuration is a strain rosetta which consists of three strain gauges oriented 0, 60, 120 degrees. This makes it possible to measure the strain in all directions in the surface   \footnote{\href{https://intertechnology.com/Vishay/pdfs/TechNotes_TechTips/TN-515.pdf}{Rosetta strain gauge} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}.  
    
    \item Loads: In this instance, the measurement of the loads acting on and through the wing is of most interest. For an aircraft this would be the resultant force (split into lift and drag) generated by the wing. These load cells are sensors which can measure a load in a specific direction. Six-axis load cells can also measure the moment generated in all three axial directions. These load cells are preferable to the three-axis cells because the load measurements are not influenced by moments.

This is typically achieved by using a pitot-tube \cite[p.~186]{anderson2016introduction}. The airspeed of HUGO will be used both for the operation and for the subsequent research.   
    \item Height: The height of the aircraft can be found by using measurements of the static pressure. This would be the barometric altitude of the aircraft and similarly, the airspeed would be used for operation and research.

Once the sensor types have been identified in \autoref{sub:sensor_interaction}, it is time to find the specific sensors that would be used. Moreover, a very important aspect of the configuration is the sensor-placement. This is not only due to a practical concern (for wiring and connections) but will also have a direct impact on the viability and usability of the data collected.

It is important to evaluate the precision and accuracy of each chosen sensor. Precision refers to how much repeated measurements deviate for a specific sensor. The accuracy specifies how much the measured value deviates from the true value. Knowing these makes it possible to access the quality of the measured data from the experiments \footnote{\href{https://web.chem.ox.ac.uk/teaching/Physics\%20for\%20CHemists/Errors/Precision.html}{Precision and accuracy} (accessed 03.06.2026)}.

The chosen strain gauges are of Series Y:RY4 from HBK \footnote{\href{https://delivery-p153383-e1601992.adobeaemcloud.com/adobe/assets/deliver/urn:aaid:aem:b6cbaaca-d372-4962-bc27-0ea3864fdd80/Y-Series-Data-Sheet-B04709.pdf}{HBK Y-Series strain gauge data sheet} (accessed 03.06.2026)}. An important aspect is the installation and fastening of the sensor to the measured surface. The exact location on the wing should be catalogued to ensure precision in the latter data processing concerning temperature issues and compensation. The strain gauges would be glued on the wing using  C80 (cyanoacrylate adhesive) and the wiring would lead to the same small cutout as for the LIDAR. 
This is a sensor which produces an analogue signal which must be read by an amplifier. The electrical signal generated by the amplifier is then sent to the flight companion. Strain gauges are placed on the wings at different intervals with higher density towards the wing tip. For the amplifier, the  QuantumX Bridge Amplifier \footnote{\href{https://assets.hbkworld.com/adobe/assets/deliver/urn:aaid:aem:65d123ca-53f3-487f-99c2-9bbc99c34e4b/QuantumX-MX1601B-Data-Sheet-B03882.pdf}{QuantumX} (accessed on 03.06.2026)}  was chosen as it has 16 channels, which in turn enable 16 strain gauges to be mounted of the wing. 
This enables the placement of eight strain-gauges on each wing, with four placed on top and four placed underneath. 


The LIDAR chosen is the HESAI FTX 140 \footnote{\href{https://www.hesaitech.com/product/ftx/}{LIDAR} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}. It is primarily chosen for its low weight and small size. There are two applications of the LIDAR system onboard the aircraft: weather detection and measurement of wing deflection. Even though it has been shown that LIDAR can be used to detect gusts ahead of an aircraft \cite{Schroeder2020}  there are currently no weather detecting LIDAR system small enough for HUGO, but it remains entirely possible for a potential client to construct a system based on existing components. Therefore it has been assumed that the weather-detecting LIDAR would operate similarly to the one chosen for the deflection. The weather LIDAR would be placed in the front of the aircraft, giving it an unobstructed view. A small cutout of the nose cone would need to be made to enable the lenses of the system to extend. 
For the wing-deflecting, a LIDAR needs to be one at each side of the aircraft fuselage, pointed towards the tip of the wing. Again, like before, a small cutout must be made to accommodate the sensors. 

To increase the accuracy and precision, certain modifications can be made on the wing. The approach would be fairly similar to the one presented in \cite{LopezHerreros2024}, where clusters of dots made in an infrared-reflective material were placed along the blade. These clusters would be placed on the leading edge and trailing edge and appear at intervals throughout the span of the wing. The high-wing configuration adds certain restrictions. A possible issue with this configuration is the risk of the tip of the wing moving out of sight of the LIDAR. Therefore, by using the preliminary CAD and maximum wing tip deflection, it was possible to roughly estimate the point of view of the LIDAR at maximum deflection.

The chosen load-cell is the six-axis M3564E1 \footnote{\href{https://www.srisensor.com/uploads/M3564E1.pdf}{SR load-cell} (accessed on 09.06.2026)} . This enables the measurement of both loads and torques generated by the wing.     
This configuration of the load-cell is different than a traditional sensor as the load-cells will be the connection between the wing and the fuselage.  
Since it must act as the structural link, the overload capacity (the total load the sensor can carry) is a very important characteristic of the sensor. For the specific sensor, the overload capacity was 200 percent of the maximum measurable load. For M3564E1, that would be:
\begin{equation}
    L_{max}=\SI{2500}{\newton} \cdot3= \SI{7500}{\newton}
\end{equation}

It is now possible to make a preliminary conservative load estimation by using the requirement of the total mass of 50 kg and using the maximum load-factor multiplied by the load-factor stipulated by HUF-STR-010 \footref{foot:midterm}):
\begin{equation}
    L_{max}=n_{max}\cdot m_{aircraft}\cdot g=9\cdot\SI{50}{\kg} \cdot \SI{9.81}{\frac{\m}{\s^2}}=  \SI{4414.15}{\newton}
\end{equation}
This estimate is conservative as load-alleviating effect of the wing mass is ignored. This means that one load- sensor would be able to carry the load at maximum wing loading. It was still decided to use four load-cells as the combined maximum measurable load would be $\SI{10000}{\newton}$. This was decided because four mounting points between the wing and fuselage was deemed the most stable configuration. This means that even for the highest limit load the sensors would be able to measure the loads, thereby ensuring continuously monitoring at all points of operation.  

The four load-cells would be screwed into an external holder. Since the outer ring uses three M5 bolts the outer holder would have three M5must sized hole to accommodate them. This holder would have a circular cut-out enabling the bolts going through the inner circle clearance. Then these would be first be bolted on the topside then a mounting plate would be put on top and then a second series of bolts would be mounted to keep it in , as shown in \autoref{fig:sensor_installation}. The most important part of the entire setup is that it most be the load-cell alone which transfers the loads from the wings to the fuselage and vice-versa.

\subsection{Landing Gear Design}
\label{sub:landing_gear_design}

The landing gear design is an important component of the external configuration. It is crucial for taxiing, and take off take-off, requiring it withstand their respective constraints and loads. Furthermore, it must be stowed away during the cruise to minimise the area exposed to airflow and reduce drag as to avoid the unnecessary increase in thrust provided and the corresponding increase in structural weight. Aeroplane Design by Roskam \cite{roskamIV} has guidelines on aircraft landing gear design. These include the scrape angle, turnover angle, wing tip clearance, and total mass on the main versus front landing gear.

HUGO's modular design requires an in-depth analysis of the landing gear positioning as many common landing gear positions would lie outside of the acceptable design space. For example, the modular wing and tail cannot house the landing gear as prospective clients would then need to design landing gear mechanisms, slowing down the testing and reducing HUGO's commercial attractiveness. 

To model the landing gear in the midterm design phase \footref{foot:midterm}, the fuselage was assumed cylindrical, with three sections: the nose cone, the main fuselage, and the upswept tail cone. %The tail cone was additionally modelled as a cylinder with different diameter circles as the base connected to the main fuselage, and the smaller diameter circle in the most aft part of the tail cone. \todo{remove: unnecessary detail, cylinder with diff diameter circles ain't a cylinder} 
Then, the FlexOp aircraft as a reference for the sizing of the simplified fuselage. Then, using SciPy's Sequential Least Squares Programming (SLSQP) optimizer, the position of the nose and main landing gears could be found to meet the necessary constraints (listed in \autoref{tab:lg_constraints}).

During landing, the main landing gear must be able to withstand the impact loads, and the shock absorber vertical deformation must be kept within allowable limits. As the landing gear needs to dissipate a predetermined downwards speed, the lower the impact load, the larger the vertical deflection required. The maximum allowable vertical deflection to avoid fuselage impacting the ground is 7.75 cm, based on the landing gear geometry. For this maximum deflection, the g-loads transferred to the structure are obtained. In case there was no feasible design space within the initial guess displacement constraints, the landing gear would have had to be enlarged.
% During landing, the main landing gear must be able to withstand the impact loads. The EASA guidelines were originally interpereted to require a high risk UAS to withstand a 4g load. Later, it was determined that the requirement was simply for the structure to withstand 1.5 times the load transfered by the landing gear. Due to time constraints, the optimal trade off between loads withstood by the fuselage versus the landing gear could not be explored, but should be looked into in further design. To meet both force and displacement constraints, the landing gear were sized to withstand a 4g load shared equally by both landing gear, damping an impact of HUGO travelling at 36 m/s at a glide slope of 3 degrees. To allow for the absorption of the impact loads, the landing gear were modelled as a spring and dashpot in parallel with constants K and C respectively. C and K can then be tuned such that the maximum vertical displacement of the landing gear does not collide with the keel, and such that a 4g impact is not exceeded. This means not exceeding a compression of 7.75 cm, and a peak force of 0.98 kN. 

Using the Python control library, the landing gear displacement and force responses to the aforementioned loads could be simulated using a linear time-invariant system (LTI). Using the spring and dashpot in parallel the following state space can be constructed using \autoref{eq:state_output_equations_1} as well as \autoref{eq:state_output_equations_2} to simulate the compression due to the vertical loads. Transverse loads were omitted in the analysis as they would effect the overall landing gear structure and material selection, and not the spring and dashpot tuning.

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}
    = \begin{bmatrix} \text{displacement} \\ \text{velocity} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
    \mathbf{u}(t) = \left(g - \frac{L}{m}\right) \cdot \mathbf{1}(t)
    \label{eq:state_output_equations_1}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \dot{\mathbf{x}} &=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 & 1 \\
        -k/m & -c/m
    \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}
    +
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 \\
        1
    \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}
\end{aligned}
\qquad
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{y} &=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & 0 \\
        k & c
    \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}
    +
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 \\
        0
    \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:state_output_equations_2}
\end{equation}

where \autoref{eq:state_output_equations_2} represents the displacement and force, respectively, L is the lift at landing, m is the mass on the landing gear, and g is the acceleration due to gravity. It is assumed that lift is equal to weight during landing.

Displacement and damping constraints will be applied to the state space model. If the system is over-damped, the spike in initial force through the landing gear would be too large. Similarly, if the damping is too low, HUGO will oscillate on the runway after landing. By varying the spring and dashpot constants, the landing gear can be tuned to meet all requirements.

\subsection{Verification of landing gear procedure}\label{sec:vnv_of_lg}

The landing gear was originally positioned using sciPy minimise. The implementation of the code was checked independently by 3 people as to prevent the accidental inclusion of bugs. In addition, a script that checked constraints given landing gear size parameters was used to check that this also met the previous requirements. The implementation of which, was also checked by 3 people to prevent the inclusion of bugs as well.

The same implementation check was used for the LTI system modelling the landing gear as a spring and dashpot. Because the landing gear was modelled as an LTI system, lsim utilises an analytical solver \cite{python_control_2026}. Consequently, the time step of 0.01s chosen for the simulation has no impact on convergence. 

The plotting of the parameter sweep was done using Claude. As input, it took the displacement and damping constraints, and the landing gear simulator using the control library. To ensure the graphs accurately depicted the computed results, points along the boundary and within the graph were selected and then simulated using the LTI system in \autoref{eq:state_output_equations_2} function. All points selected matched the graph produced by Claude with the exception of the points within the over-damped section. This is because if a combination of C and K values were over-damped, Claude would not continue the analysis to determine if it met the remaining constraints. A manual check was performed on the chosen values of C and K, and indeed those values produced over-damping. As such, the graphs produced were considered verified.


\subsection{Environmental Control}
\label{sub:environmental_control}
Environmental control is the response of HUGO to different external factors such as rain or UV-radiation. An analysis was established where different flight conditions have been considered, and it was determined that the critical case for HUGO come from the presence of ice on the wings due to the low temperatures. 

As supercooled water hits the fuselage, it instantly turns into ice, increasing the weight \cite{andreev2014icing} and damaging the aerodynamic profile. Since the wings fitted on HUGO are very thin, optimised for high cruise speed, any perturbation of the aerodynamic profile may result in a catastrophic event. 

There are two primary ways to combat this phenomenon, either by using a passive system (coating or phase changing materials) or by an active system (heating elements). Since the wings are subjected to regular replacement and modifications, it is more efficient to use a passive system for HUGO. 

A coating that could help HUGO delay the formation of ice on the wings involves coating them with a poly-siloxane elastomer coating that sticks to resin \cite{facco2025passive}. It delays the formation of ice on the wings as well as offering a very low ice adhesion, effectively protecting the wing from ice. Despite its weak mechanical properties, HUGO is not meant to fly for many missions with the same set of wings, as such it does not require withstanding a lot of cycles.

\subsection{Change of the centre of gravity}
\label{sub:Changeofthecentreofgravity}

As mentioned in the mission envelope (\autoref{sec:mission_envelope}), new control systems might be implemented and tested. As the controllability of a aircraft is highly dependent on the CG, it was decided to have it as a parameter that can be changed during flight. This is achieved by pumping the fuel between the two fuel tanks onboard as described in \autoref{sub:cg_location}. The range of the CG can be calculated by knowing the empty weight of the aircraft, fuel weight, and the placement of the fuel tanks. As stipulated in the Matching diagram (see \autoref{tab: Matching diagram constraints}), the fuel tanks would be at $50\%$. Then, assuming straight and level flight, the CG location can be estimated by calculating the moments generated by the fuel mass relative to the empty weight CG. This is then divided by the mass of the complete aircraft \footnote{\href{https://engineeringstatics.org/Chapter_07-center-of-gravity.html}{CG} (accessed 16.06.2026)}. The code for the calculations can be found in the project repository

\section{Material Options}
\label{sec:material_options}

In the Midterm Report \footref{ft:midterm_rep}, materials were selected for HUGO’s primary structure, including the wings, fuselage, and empennage, as well as for the landing gear, engine pylons, and connection interfaces. In particular, aluminium alloy Al 7075-T76 was chosen for the landing gear, engine pylons, and connection ports. However, this choice will be evaluated with a more detailed analysis before being confirmed. The wings, fuselage, and empennage will be manufactured out of CFRP. The prepreg material to be used has yet to be selected, along with the materials for the wing foam core and the honeycomb core structure of the fuselage monocoque.

\subsection{Metal Alloys}
\label{sub:metal_alloys}

Aluminium alloys, high-strength steels, and titanium alloys are the primary metals used in commercial aerospace \footnote{\href{https://www.flightmetals.com/metals-in-aerospace/}{Materials in Aerospace} (accessed on 01.06.2026)}. As mentioned in the Midterm Report \footref{ft:midterm_rep}, steel is used for safety-critical structures due to its high specific strength, while titanium is applied mainly in engine systems and landing gear because of its high strength, long fatigue life, fracture toughness, creep resistance, and excellent corrosion and oxidation resistance. 

While aluminium was selected for the landing gear, engine pylons, and connection ports, the landing gear material should be revisited due to high landing impact loads, where steel may be preferable. The engine pylons are also exposed to high temperatures, and given the aluminium alloy’s melting point of \SI{480}{\celsius} \footnote{\href{https://www.makeitfrom.com/material-properties/7075-T76-Aluminum}{7075-T76 Aluminum} (accessed on 01.06.2026)}, materials with a higher melting temperature should be considered.

For the landing gear, the material must be capable of resisting the high impact loads generated during touchdown without experiencing excessive deformation or yielding. A high elastic modulus contributes to structural stiffness, while high yield and ultimate tensile strengths ensure that the material can sustain large stresses without permanent deformation or failure. 
Although fatigue strength can be considered in the design, it is not expected to be a governing factor. Each HUGO is anticipated to perform only four test flights per day for 20 days per year over a 15-year operational lifetime, resulting in approximately 1,200 flight cycles. Inspection of the S–N curve for 18Ni (350) maraging steel \cite{Rack1974FatigueMaraging} shows that the highest stress levels investigated correspond to a fatigue life of approximately $10^4$ cycles. As HUGO is expected to experience only 1,200 operational cycles over its service life, fatigue failure is not expected to occur before end-of-life. A similar assessment can be made for Al 7075-T76, for which the highest tested stress levels correspond to fatigue lives exceeding $10^5$ cycles \cite{Zalnezhad2012}, and for Ti-6Al-4V, whose S–N data indicate fatigue lives approaching $10^4$ cycles at the highest stress amplitudes considered \cite{Janeček2015}. Therefore, fatigue is not expected to be a limiting design factor for any of the candidate materials under the anticipated operating conditions.


To evaluate the holistic influence of material selection on component design, a structural analysis of the landing gear strut was performed. The strut is subjected to landing loads comprising an axial load, derived from the aircraft's (HUGO) weight and landing load factor, and a transverse load resulting from braking deceleration. Based on a landing load factor of 4 specified during landing by CS-23 \cite{EASA_CS23_Am3_2012}, and incorporating a safety factor of 2, the design axial load was calculated as 1962 N per gear. Additionally, assuming a peak braking deceleration of 2g with the same safety factor, the design transverse load was determined to be 981 N. 

The structural integrity of the strut was assessed against yield failure - combining bending and axial compression - as well as Euler buckling. The maximum combined stress $\sigma_{max}$ and the critical buckling load $P_{cr}$ were analyzed using the following formulae \cite[p.~256]{megson2016aircraft}, where $K$ is the length factor determined by the boundary conditions ($K = 2.0$ for the cantilever configuration):

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{max} = \frac{F_{axial}}{A} + \frac{M_{bending_{max}} c}{I}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 E I}{(K L)^2}
\end{equation}

Additionally, local shell buckling (skin crippling) under combined axial and bending loads was evaluated using the NASA SP-8007 design criteria \cite{nasa_sp8007}. The allowable local buckling stresses for pure axial compression ($\sigma_{c,all}$) and pure bending ($\sigma_{b,all}$) are given by:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{c,all} = \frac{\gamma_c E}{\sqrt{3(1-\mu^2)}} \frac{t}{r}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{b,all} = \frac{\gamma_b E}{\sqrt{3(1-\mu^2)}} \frac{t}{r}
\end{equation}

where $\mu$ is the Poisson's ratio, $r$ is the cylinder radius, and $\gamma_c$ and $\gamma_b$ are empirical knockdown factors accounting for manufacturing imperfections:

\begin{equation}
\gamma_c = 1 - 0.901(1 - e^{-\phi})
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\gamma_b = 1 - 0.731(1 - e^{-\phi})
\end{equation}

The geometric parameter $\phi$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\phi = \frac{1}{16}\sqrt{\frac{r}{t}}
\end{equation}

Structural adequacy under combined loading is satisfied if the stress ratios satisfy the linear interaction envelope:

\begin{equation}
R_c + R_b \le 1.0
\end{equation}

where $R_c = \sigma_c / \sigma_{c,all}$ and $R_b = \sigma_b / \sigma_{b,all}$ represent the applied-to-allowable stress ratios for the compression and bending components, respectively.

The results, summarized in Table \autoref{tab:strut_sizing}, demonstrate that Aluminum 7075-T76 and Titanium yield the lightest strut designs capable of withstanding the prescribed loads, whereas the Steel 18Ni(350) design is slightly heavier. However, the steel design results in the strut with the lowest diameter.

Beyond structural performance, material selection must also account for environmental impact over the component's lifecycle. In terms of recyclability, a primary obstacle of aluminium is separating it from other metals within the alloy \footnote{\href{https://www.recyclingtoday.com/news/tomra-aluminum-sorting-recycling-technology-van-de-winkel/}{Commentary: Recycling plays critical role in future aluminium production} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}. In the case of titanium, its high melting point and reactive nature create more processing challenges than aluminium \footnote{\href{https://ironandmetals.com/blog/titanium-vs-aluminum-recycling-what-you-need-to-know/}{Titanium vs Aluminum Recycling: What You Need to Know} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}. When considering also the embodied carbon per kilogram of material as listed in \autoref{tab:material_properties_metals}, the titanium alloy performs the worst, whereas the steel has the lowest overall environmental impact.

A final consideration, as previously mentioned, is the proximity of the engine pylons to the high temperatures generated during engine operation. Information gathered from communicating with a JetCat informed us that the outer temperatures of the nacelle will exceed 200$^\circ$C. This is far from the melting temperature of all the metals being considered, however, material properties like Young's Modulus (E) decrease linearly with temperature increase. Additionally, creep resistance is also impacted by temperature \cite[ch.~13, p.~553]{ashby2019materials}, as when the temperature of the material approaches its melting point, its resistance to creep degrades.

Aluminium 7076-T76 therefore cannot be used for the engine pylons, as its melting point is close to the nacelle temperature of the engines. Additionally, the optimal landing gear strut diameter for aluminium is 38.54 mm, which is very high, resulting in high drag and internal volume use. Titanium results in a lighter design than steel, however, due to the high material and manufacturing cost associated with titanium, as well as it's challenges in terms of recyclability, \textbf{Steel 18Ni(350)} is chosen as the material for the landing gear and engine pylons.

Plain weave consists of an over-under interlacing pattern that produces a symmetric, checkerboard-like structure with high dimensional stability. However, this stability limits its drapability, making it more suitable for flat or gently curved geometries. The frequent fiber crimp can also introduce stress concentrations that may reduce long-term performance. In contrast, satin weaves such as 8HS are designed for improved formability and draping capability, allowing them to conform more easily to complex shapes. This comes at the cost of reduced stability compared to plain weave \footnote{\href{https://www.elevatedmaterials.com/carbon-fiber-weaves-what-they-are-and-why-to-use-them/}{Carbon Fiber Weaves: What they are and why to use them} (accessed on 05.06.2026)}.

Given that the initial HUGO fleet will consist of only five aircraft, the manufacturing strategy becomes just as important as raw material performance. While autoclave-cured prepreg systems can deliver the highest achievable laminate properties, they are typically justified only when production volumes are high enough to offset the substantial capital and operational costs associated with autoclave processing. For a low-volume programme such as HUGO, Out-of-Autoclave (OOA) and Vacuum-Bag-Only (VBO) manufacturing offer a far more practical solution.

OOA manufacturing enables rapid prototyping, reduced tooling complexity, shorter production lead times, and significantly lower upfront investment. %Eliminating the need to purchase or rent autoclave facilities substantially reduces cost while retaining the ability to produce aerospace-quality composite structures. 
This manufacturing flexibility is particularly valuable during early development, when design iterations are likely, and production quantities remain limited, which is the current stage at which the HUGO project is. Therefore, despite exhibiting stiffness properties comparable to those of CYCOM 5320-1, HexPly 8552 was excluded from further consideration because it requires autoclave curing, which is incompatible with the selected low-cost, low-volume manufacturing strategy.

From a structural perspective, the choice of prepreg sheets must be evaluated in the context of HUGO's primary load cases. Tensile strength is not the governing parameter for the aircraft's major composite structures since the fuselage and wing skins are thin-walled, buckling-critical components where stiffness dominates structural behaviour. In this regard, CYCOM 5320-1 offers a significant advantage over MTM45-1 due to its higher elastic modulus ($\approx 9\%$), resulting in stiffer laminates and improved resistance to panel buckling. Moreover, the monocoque fuselage must withstand torsional loads arising from both aerodynamic forces and inertial effects, while the wing skin carries shear stresses induced by bending and pitching moments. In this context, shear stiffness is the governing material parameter. When comparing the $G_{12}$ values of CYCOM 5320-1 and MTM45-1, the CYCOM prepreg exhibits a shear modulus that is approximately 51\% higher than that of MTM45-1, making it more suitable for stiffness-critical structural applications.

Fatigue was not considered a critical design factor for the wings, as they are expected to experience significantly fewer loading cycles than the estimated 1,200 cycles over HUGO's operational lifetime. Each research wing is anticipated to undergo only a limited number of test campaigns, resulting in a negligible fatigue exposure. For the fuselage, however, fatigue performance was assessed using available fatigue studies on comparable composite sandwich structures, which served as a reference for evaluating the long-term structural integrity of the design.

Although MTM45-1 offers a marginal weight advantage of approximately 4\% at the laminate level, this benefit is outweighed by the superior stiffness of CYCOM 5320-1. For buckling-critical thin-walled structures, a 4\% reduction in areal density is a secondary gain compared to the 9\% modulus advantage that CYCOM 5320-1 provides in both fibre directions, since panel buckling resistance scales directly with laminate stiffness.

Another important consideration is the prepreg layup process. HUGO's monocoque fuselage and aerodynamic wing surfaces consist primarily of doubly-curved geometries, where the ability of the reinforcement to conform accurately to the mould is critical. While plain weave fabrics offer good handling stability during layup, they possess relatively poor drapability. This can lead to bridging, wrinkling, and fibre waviness when applied to complex curved surfaces. Such manufacturing defects introduce local stress concentrations and reduce the effective laminate stiffness and strength. In contrast, 8-harness satin (8HS) fabrics exhibit significantly superior drapability, allowing them to conform more easily to complex mould geometries and produce laminates with fewer defects. 
%Although 8HS fabrics are somewhat less stable during dry handling due to their longer float lengths, this disadvantage exists only during the layup process. Once the laminate is cured, the concern effectively disappears, leaving only the benefits of improved fibre alignment and the superior mechanical properties associated with the fabric architecture. For highly contoured aerospace structures such as HUGO's fuselage and wings, achieving accurate mould conformity and minimising manufacturing-induced defects is arguably more important than maximizing dry-fabric handling stability.

The resin system selection points toward the same conclusion. CYCOM 5320-1 was specifically developed for OOA/VBO processing and is widely used in UAV primary structures. According to the manufacturer's data \footref{fn:cycom}, the material is capable of producing autoclave-quality laminates with very low porosity despite requiring only vacuum-bag curing. This is a critical characteristic because it demonstrates that transitioning away from autoclave manufacturing does not necessarily entail a significant sacrifice in laminate quality. Instead, it allows much of the quality associated with traditional aerospace composite manufacturing to be retained while substantially simplifying the production process.

%Although autoclave-cured IM7 systems can achieve approximately 15-20\% higher mechanical properties than CYCOM 5320-1 laminates, the operational requirements of HUGO do not justify this additional performance. As a Mach 0.75 UAV with relatively modest structural loads, HUGO is not expected to be strength-limited to the extent that this performance margin becomes necessary. Designing around the highest available material properties would increase manufacturing complexity and programme cost without providing a proportional benefit in operational capability or structural efficiency.

Consequently, the selection of CYCOM 5320-1 represents a rational engineering optimisation rather than a compromise. The system offers sufficient mechanical performance for the anticipated load environment, superior manufacturability for low-volume production, compatibility with complex aerodynamic geometries through the use of drapable 8HS reinforcement, and the ability to produce aerospace-quality laminates without autoclave infrastructure. Additionally, as noted earlier, its processing characteristics are particularly well-suited to honeycomb structures, which will be the fuselage core.


\subsection{Core Material Selection}
\label{sub:core_materials}
As previously mentioned, the wings of HUGO will be produced using a machined foam core, with layers of pre-preg carbon sheets laid on top. The fuselage will be produced using a sandwich structure, comprising carbon laminates and an intermediate honeycomb layer \footnote{\href{https://www.dmcrf.com/benefits-of-honeycomb-core-structures-in-aerospace-and-automotive-sectors/}{Benefits of Honeycomb Core Structures in Aerospace and Automotive Sectors} (accessed on 04.06.2026)}. In this subsection, the material candidates for both the honeycomb and foam cores are evaluated and selected based on structural requirements and manufacturing constraints.

\subsubsection{Foam Core Candidates}
Structural foams used in composite manufacturing generally fall into three broad categories: Polymethacrylimide (PMI), Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC), and Extruded Polystyrene (XPS)\footnote{Campbell, F. C. (2010). \textit{Structural Composite Materials}. ASM International. (accessed on 04.06.2026)}. 
PMI foams are high-performance, closed-cell structures known for their exceptional thermal stability and compressive strength. PVC foams offer a good balance of mechanical properties and cost for low-temperature applications but soften rapidly under heat. XPS foams are highly cost-effective and easily machinable but possess very low mechanical strength and thermal resistance. \subsubsection{Final Material Selection}
The primary constraint driving core selection is the manufacturing process. As discussed in \autoref{sub:composites}, the airframe will be manufactured using autoclave-cured carbon fiber pre-preg. This exposes the core materials to consolidation pressures of up to 100 psi and temperatures exceeding $120^\circ$C. 

For the wings, \textbf{Rohacell 71 IG-F (PMI foam)} is selected. As demonstrated in \autoref{tab:foam_props}, it is the only candidate with a maximum service temperature ($180^\circ$C) capable of surviving the maximum curing temperatures of the chosen pre-preg. Both the PVC and XPS foams would thermally yield and crush under consolidation pressure.

For the fuselage sandwich structure, the \textbf{4.8mm Cell 48kg Nomex} aramid honeycomb is selected. While aluminum offers superior mechanical properties, the aramid core eliminates the risk of galvanic corrosion and provides the necessary out of plane flexibility to conform to the compound aerodynamic curvatures of the fuselage without buckling. The 48kg variant provides sufficient compressive strength (2.40 MPa) to resist localized crushing during the high-pressure autoclave cure.

    \item \ang{0}/\ang{90}/\ang{45}/\ang{-45}: closely represents isotropic material (2 stacked \ang{0}/\ang{90} sheets)
\end{enumerate}

For curved and complex shapes, orientation becomes harder to control.

Adding fibers in many directions improves strength, but increases material use and production time. Keeping the total thickness low saves weight.

\subsection{Metal Alloys}
\label{sub:metal_alloys}

Aluminium alloys, high-strength steels, and titanium alloys are the primary metals used in commercial aerospace \footnote{\href{https://www.flightmetals.com/metals-in-aerospace/}{Materials in Aerospace} (accessed on 01.06.2026)}. As mentioned in the Midterm Report (Git footnote), steel is used for safety-critical structures due to its high specific strength, while titanium is applied mainly in engine systems and landing gear because of its high strength, long fatigue life, fracture toughness, creep resistance, and excellent corrosion and oxidation resistance. 

While aluminium was selected for the landing gear, engine pylons, and connection ports, the landing gear material should be revisited due to high landing impact loads, where steel may be preferable. The engine pylons are also exposed to high temperatures, and given the aluminium alloy’s melting point of \SI{480}{\celsius}\footnote{\href{https://www.makeitfrom.com/material-properties/7075-T76-Aluminum}{7075-T76 Aluminum} (accessed on 01.06.2026)}, materials with a higher melting temperature should be considered.

For the landing gear, the material must be capable of resisting the high impact loads generated during touchdown without experiencing excessive deformation or yielding. A high elastic modulus contributes to structural stiffness, while high yield and ultimate tensile strengths ensure that the material can sustain large stresses without permanent deformation or failure. 
Although fatigue strength can be considered in the design, it is not expected to be a governing factor. Each HUGO is anticipated to perform only four test flights per day for 20 days per year over a 15-year operational lifetime, resulting in approximately 1,200 flight cycles. This relatively low number of cycles is well below the range typically associated with fatigue-critical aerospace structures. 

To evaluate the holistic influence of material selection on component design, a structural analysis of the landing gear strut was performed. The strut is subjected to landing loads comprising an axial load, derived from the aircraft's (HUGO) weight and landing load factor, and a transverse load resulting from braking deceleration. Based on a landing load factor of 4 specified during landing by CS-23 \cite{EASA_CS23_Am3_2012}, and incorporating a safety factor of 2, the design axial load was calculated as 1962 N per gear. Additionally, assuming a peak braking deceleration of 2g with the same safety factor, the design transverse load was determined to be 981 N. 

The structural integrity of the strut was assessed against yield failure—combining bending and axial compression—as well as Euler buckling. The maximum combined stress $\sigma_{max}$ and the critical buckling load $P_{cr}$ were analyzed using the following formulae \cite[p.~256]{megson2016aircraft}, where $K$ is the length factor determined by the boundary conditions ($K = 2.0$ for the cantilever configuration):

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{max} = \frac{F_{axial}}{A} + \frac{M_{bending_{max}} c}{I}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 E I}{(K L)^2}
\end{equation}

Additionally, local shell buckling (skin crippling) under combined axial and bending loads was evaluated using the NASA SP-8007 design criteria \cite{nasa_sp8007}. The allowable local buckling stresses for pure axial compression ($\sigma_{c,all}$) and pure bending ($\sigma_{b,all}$) are given by:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{c,all} = \frac{\gamma_c E}{\sqrt{3(1-\mu^2)}} \frac{t}{r}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{b,all} = \frac{\gamma_b E}{\sqrt{3(1-\mu^2)}} \frac{t}{r}
\end{equation}

where $\mu$ is the Poisson's ratio, $r$ is the cylinder radius, and $\gamma_c$ and $\gamma_b$ are empirical knockdown factors accounting for manufacturing imperfections:

\begin{equation}
\gamma_c = 1 - 0.901(1 - e^{-\phi})
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\gamma_b = 1 - 0.731(1 - e^{-\phi})
\end{equation}

The geometric parameter $\phi$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\phi = \frac{1}{16}\sqrt{\frac{r}{t}}
\end{equation}

Structural adequacy under combined loading is satisfied if the stress ratios satisfy the linear interaction envelope:

\begin{equation}
R_c + R_b \le 1.0
\end{equation}

where $R_c = \sigma_c / \sigma_{c,all}$ and $R_b = \sigma_b / \sigma_{b,all}$ represent the applied-to-allowable stress ratios for the compression and bending components, respectively.

The results, summarized in Table \ref{tab:strut_sizing}, demonstrate that Aluminum 7075-T76 and Titanium yield the lightest strut designs capable of withstanding the prescribed loads, whereas the Steel 18Ni(350) design is slightly heavier. However, the steel design results in the strut with the lowest diameter.

In terms of recyclability, a primary obstacle of aluminium is separating it from other metals within the alloy \footnote{\href{https://www.recyclingtoday.com/news/tomra-aluminum-sorting-recycling-technology-van-de-winkel/}{Commentary: Recycling plays critical role in future aluminium production} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}. In the case of titanium, its high melting point and reactive nature create more processing challenges than aluminium \footnote{\href{https://ironandmetals.com/blog/titanium-vs-aluminum-recycling-what-you-need-to-know/}{Titanium vs Aluminum Recycling: What You Need to Know} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}. When considering also the embodied carbon per kilogram of material as listed in \autoref{tab:material_properties_metals}, the titanium alloy performs the worst, whereas the steel has the lowest overall environmental impact.

The pylon structure experiences complex dynamic loading arising from engine thrust, aerodynamic forces, vibrations, manoeuvres, and gust loads. Consequently, adequate shear modulus and shear strength are required to resist torsional and transverse loading, while high tensile strength is necessary to maintain structural integrity under normal stresses. Information gathered from communicating with a JetCat representative informed us that the outer temperatures of the nacelle will exceed 200$^\circ$C. This is far from the melting temperature of all the metals being considered, however, material properties like Young's Modulus (E) decrease linearly with temperature increase. Additionally, creep resistance is also impacted by temperature \cite[ch.~13, p.~553]{ashby2019materials}, as when the temperature of the material approaches its melting point, its resistance to creep degrades.

Aluminium 7076-T76 therefore cannot be used for the engine pylons, as its melting point is close to the nacelle temperature of the engines. Additionally, the optimal landing gear strut diameter for aluminium is 38.54 mm, which is very high, resulting in high drag and internal volume use. Titanium results in a lighter design than Steel, however, due to the high material and manufacturing cost associated with titanium, as well as it's challenges in terms of recyclability, Steel is chosen as the material for the landing gear and engine pylons.

Three types of resin systems were considered for the selection of carbon fibre prepregs: epoxy, bismaleimide (BMI), and cyanate ester. Epoxy resin prepregs are widely used in aerospace industry due to their established manufacturing processes and generally good chemical, thermal, and mechanical properties, and are commonly used in a broad range of structural applications \footnote{\label{fn:prepregs}\href{https://www.hgcomposites.com/info/various-types-of-carbon-fiber-prepreg-83847115.html}{Various Types of Carbon Fiber Prepreg and Usages} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}. In particular, the  HexPly 8552 woven carbon prepreg is considered due to its usage in primary aerospace structures \footnote{\label{fn:hexply}\href{https://www.hexcel.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/HexPly_8552_eu_DataSheet1.pdf}{HexPly 8552} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}.
\setcounter{fnhexply}{\value{footnote}}

Bismaleimide (BMI) prepregs are also used in aerospace structures, particularly for aircraft panels and engine-related components, where elevated temperature capability is required. They are known for their high-temperature performance while maintaining structural integrity \footref{fn:prepregs}. In particular, the CYCOM 5250-4 prepreg, developed for primary aerospace structural applications, is considered \footnote{\label{fn:cycom}\href{https://www.e-aircraftsupply.com/msds/11856cytec\%20cycom\%205250-4\%20tds.pdf?srsltid=AfmBOoohgVhUA4CUm3Pxu0xo0YGcufvnyWHlOen-2XMyMOu5qVQS0f1g}{CYCOM® 5250-4 PREPREG SYSTEM} (accessed on 03.06.2026)}.
\setcounter{fncycom}{\value{footnote}}

Cyanate ester prepregs are used in specialised aerospace applications such as radomes and engine components. They are selected where resistance to elevated temperatures and harsh chemical environments is required, along with suitable mechanical performance for structural use \footref{fn:prepregs}. In particular, the Toray RS-3/RS-3C prepreg is considered, which has demonstrated outstanding performance in airframe structures over the past three decades  \footnote{\label{fn:toray}\href{https://www.toraytac.com/media/fb396e4c-a85e-45f2-a189-533bb17039f0/CmbtRw/TAC/Documents/Data_sheets/Thermoset/UD\%20tapes\%20and\%20prepregs/RS-3_Cyanate-Ester_PDS.pdf}{Toray RS-3/RS-3C} (accessed on 03.06.2026)}.

\subsection{Metal Alloys}
\label{sub:metal_alloys}

Aluminium alloys, high-strength steels, and titanium alloys are the primary metals used in commercial aerospace \footnote{\href{https://www.flightmetals.com/metals-in-aerospace/}{Materials in Aerospace} (accessed on 01.06.2026)}. As mentioned in the Midterm Report \footref{ft:midterm_rep}, steel is used for safety-critical structures due to its high specific strength, while titanium is applied mainly in engine systems and landing gear because of its high strength, long fatigue life, fracture toughness, creep resistance, and excellent corrosion and oxidation resistance. 

While aluminium was selected for the landing gear, engine pylons, and connection ports, the landing gear material should be revisited due to high landing impact loads, where steel may be preferable. The engine pylons are also exposed to high temperatures, and given the aluminium alloy’s melting point of \SI{480}{\celsius} \footnote{\href{https://www.makeitfrom.com/material-properties/7075-T76-Aluminum}{7075-T76 Aluminum} (accessed on 01.06.2026)}, materials with a higher melting temperature should be considered.

For the landing gear, the material must be capable of resisting the high impact loads generated during touchdown without experiencing excessive deformation or yielding. A high elastic modulus contributes to structural stiffness, while high yield and ultimate tensile strengths ensure that the material can sustain large stresses without permanent deformation or failure. 
Although fatigue strength can be considered in the design, it is not expected to be a governing factor. Each HUGO is anticipated to perform only four test flights per day for 20 days per year over a 15-year operational lifetime, resulting in approximately 1,200 flight cycles. Inspection of the S–N curve for 18Ni (350) maraging steel \cite{Rack1974FatigueMaraging} shows that the highest stress levels investigated correspond to a fatigue life of approximately 10$^4$ cycles. As HUGO is expected to experience only 1,200 operational cycles over its service life, fatigue failure is not expected to occur before end-of-life. A similar assessment can be made for Al 7075-T76, for which the highest tested stress levels correspond to fatigue lives exceeding 10$^5$ cycles \cite{Zalnezhad2012}, and for Ti-6Al-4V, whose S–N data indicate fatigue lives approaching 10$^4$ cycles at the highest stress amplitudes considered \cite{Janecek2015}. Therefore, fatigue is not expected to be a limiting design factor for any of the candidate materials under the anticipated operating conditions.


To evaluate the holistic influence of material selection on component design, a structural analysis of the landing gear strut was performed. The strut is subjected to landing loads comprising an axial load, derived from the aircraft's (HUGO) weight and landing load factor, and a transverse load resulting from braking deceleration. Based on a landing load factor of 4 specified during landing by CS-23 \cite{EASA_CS23_Am3_2012}, and incorporating a safety factor of 2, the design axial load was calculated as 1962 N per gear. Additionally, assuming a peak braking deceleration of 2g with the same safety factor, the design transverse load was determined to be 981 N. 

The structural integrity of the strut was assessed against yield failure - combining bending and axial compression - as well as Euler buckling. The maximum combined stress $\sigma_{max}$ and the critical buckling load $P_{cr}$ were analyzed using the following formulae \cite[p.~256]{megson2016aircraft}, where $K$ is the length factor determined by the boundary conditions ($K = 2.0$ for the cantilever configuration):

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{max} = \frac{F_{axial}}{A} + \frac{M_{bending_{max}} c}{I}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 E I}{(K L)^2}
\end{equation}

Additionally, local shell buckling (skin crippling) under combined axial and bending loads was evaluated using the NASA SP-8007 design criteria \cite{nasa_sp8007}. The allowable local buckling stresses for pure axial compression ($\sigma_{c,all}$) and pure bending ($\sigma_{b,all}$) are given by:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{c,all} = \frac{\gamma_c E}{\sqrt{3(1-\mu^2)}} \frac{t}{r}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{b,all} = \frac{\gamma_b E}{\sqrt{3(1-\mu^2)}} \frac{t}{r}
\end{equation}

where $\mu$ is the Poisson's ratio, $r$ is the cylinder radius, and $\gamma_c$ and $\gamma_b$ are empirical knockdown factors accounting for manufacturing imperfections:

\begin{equation}
\gamma_c = 1 - 0.901(1 - e^{-\phi})
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\gamma_b = 1 - 0.731(1 - e^{-\phi})
\end{equation}

The geometric parameter $\phi$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\phi = \frac{1}{16}\sqrt{\frac{r}{t}}
\end{equation}

Structural adequacy under combined loading is satisfied if the stress ratios satisfy the linear interaction envelope:

\begin{equation}
R_c + R_b \le 1.0
\end{equation}

where $R_c = \sigma_c / \sigma_{c,all}$ and $R_b = \sigma_b / \sigma_{b,all}$ represent the applied-to-allowable stress ratios for the compression and bending components, respectively.

The results, summarized in Table \autoref{tab:strut_sizing}, demonstrate that Aluminum 7075-T76 and Titanium yield the lightest strut designs capable of withstanding the prescribed loads, whereas the Steel 18Ni(350) design is slightly heavier. However, the steel design results in the strut with the lowest diameter.

Beyond structural performance, material selection must also account for environmental impact over the component's lifecycle. In terms of recyclability, a primary obstacle of aluminium is separating it from other metals within the alloy \footnote{\href{https://www.recyclingtoday.com/news/tomra-aluminum-sorting-recycling-technology-van-de-winkel/}{Commentary: Recycling plays critical role in future aluminium production} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}. In the case of titanium, its high melting point and reactive nature create more processing challenges than aluminium \footnote{\href{https://ironandmetals.com/blog/titanium-vs-aluminum-recycling-what-you-need-to-know/}{Titanium vs Aluminum Recycling: What You Need to Know} (accessed on 02.06.2026)}. When considering also the embodied carbon per kilogram of material as listed in \autoref{tab:material_properties_metals}, the titanium alloy performs the worst, whereas the steel has the lowest overall environmental impact.

A final consideration, as previously mentioned, is the proximity of the engine pylons to the high temperatures generated during engine operation. Information gathered from communicating with a JetCat informed us that the outer temperatures of the nacelle will exceed 200$^\circ$C. This is far from the melting temperature of all the metals being considered, however, material properties like Young's Modulus (E) decrease linearly with temperature increase. Additionally, creep resistance is also impacted by temperature \cite[ch.~13, p.~553]{ashby2019materials}, as when the temperature of the material approaches its melting point, its resistance to creep degrades.

Aluminium 7076-T76 therefore cannot be used for the engine pylons, as its melting point is close to the nacelle temperature of the engines. Additionally, the optimal landing gear strut diameter for aluminium is 38.54 mm, which is very high, resulting in high drag and internal volume use. Titanium results in a lighter design than steel, however, due to the high material and manufacturing cost associated with titanium, as well as it's challenges in terms of recyclability, \textbf{Steel 18Ni(350)} is chosen as the material for the landing gear and engine pylons.

In \autoref{tab:prepreg_properties}, $\sigma_{TS_0}$ and $E_0$ denote the tensile strength and elastic modulus in the $\ang{0}$ fibre direction, respectively, while $\sigma_{TS_{90}}$ and $E_{90}$ represent the corresponding properties in the $\ang{90}$ direction. The Fiber/Style column in \autoref{tab:prepreg_properties2} indicates the type of carbon fiber and the weave architecture of the prepreg, with PW referring to plain weave and 8HS to 8 harness satin. 

Plain weave consists of an over–under interlacing pattern that produces a symmetric, dimensionally stable structure, but its high fiber crimp reduces drapability and can introduce stress concentrations. As a result, it is better suited to flat or gently curved geometries. In contrast, satin weaves such as 8HS offer improved formability and draping for complex shapes, at the expense of reduced structural stability \footnote{\href{https://www.elevatedmaterials.com/carbon-fiber-weaves-what-they-are-and-why-to-use-them/}{Carbon Fiber Weaves: What they are and why to use them} (accessed on 05.06.2026)}.

Given that the initial HUGO fleet consists of only five aircraft, the manufacturing strategy is as critical as material selection. While autoclave-cured prepregs deliver the highest laminate properties, their high capital and operational costs are only justified at high production volumes. For this low-volume programme, Out-of-Autoclave (OOA) and Vacuum-Bag-Only (VBO) processes are more appropriate due to their lower cost, faster turnaround, and reduced tooling complexity.

OOA manufacturing enables rapid prototyping, shorter lead times, and significantly lower upfront investment while still achieving aerospace-quality laminates. This flexibility is especially valuable during early design iterations. Consequently, HexPly 8552 was excluded despite comparable stiffness to CYCOM 5320-1, as its autoclave requirement conflicts with the selected low-cost, low-volume production strategy.

From a structural perspective, material selection is governed by stiffness rather than tensile strength, since HUGO’s thin-walled fuselage and wing skins are buckling-critical. CYCOM 5320-1 provides approximately 9\% higher elastic modulus than MTM45-1, improving panel buckling resistance. It also offers approximately 51\% higher shear modulus, making it more suitable for torsion- and shear-dominated load paths in the fuselage and wing skins.

Fatigue was not considered a critical design factor for the wings, as they are expected to experience significantly fewer loading cycles than the estimated 1,200 cycles over HUGO's operational lifetime. Each research wing is anticipated to undergo only a limited number of test campaigns, resulting in a negligible fatigue exposure. For the fuselage, literature on similar sandwich structures shows that fatigue behaviour strongly depends on loading level r, defined as the ratio of cyclic to static failure displacement. At a high loading level of r = 0.85, failure occurs within a few hundred cycles, whereas at r = 0.70, failure is reported between 10,000 and 100,000 cycles, and at r = 0.60, specimens survive beyond 10$^6$ cycles without failure, as reported in \cite{Boukharouba2014}. Limiting operational loads below r = 0.70 therefore provides a large safety margin for HUGO. The preliminary structural analysis predicts that the maximum normal stress of approximately 30 MPa within the fuselage. Consequently, the corresponding loading level is expected to remain well below 0.7. Further improvements can be achieved through increased skin-core adhesive content and greater skin thickness, both of which enhance fatigue resistance \cite{Jen2009Adhesive, Jen2009SheetThickness}.

Although MTM45-1 offers approximately 4\% weight advantage, this is outweighed by the higher stiffness of CYCOM 5320-1, which is more critical for buckling-dominated structures where stiffness scales directly with stability. Thus, stiffness is prioritized over marginal areal density reductions.

Regarding manufacturability, HUGO’s fuselage and wing skins involve doubly curved geometries where drapability is essential. Plain weave fabrics are stable but prone to bridging, wrinkling, and fibre waviness in complex molds, reducing effective stiffness. 8HS fabrics offer superior drapability, enabling better conformity and fewer manufacturing defects, which outweighs their reduced dry-fabric stability.

CYCOM 5320-1 is also well-suited to OOA/VBO processing and is widely used in UAV primary structures. Manufacturer data indicates it can achieve low porosity and near-autoclave-quality laminates under vacuum bag curing, allowing high-quality structures without autoclave infrastructure.

Overall, CYCOM 5320-1 is selected as a balanced solution, offering sufficient mechanical performance, superior buckling resistance, compatibility with OOA/VBO manufacturing, and good drapability when paired with 8HS reinforcement. It is therefore an optimisation for low-volume aerospace production rather than a compromise.

\subsection{Core Material Selection}
\label{sub:core_materials}
As previously mentioned, the wings of HUGO will be produced using a machined foam core, with layers of pre-preg carbon sheets laid on top. The fuselage will be produced using a sandwich structure, comprising carbon laminates and an intermediate honeycomb layer \footnote{\href{https://www.dmcrf.com/benefits-of-honeycomb-core-structures-in-aerospace-and-automotive-sectors/}{Benefits of Honeycomb Core Structures in Aerospace and Automotive Sectors} (accessed on 04.06.2026)}. In this subsection, the material candidates for both the honeycomb and foam cores are evaluated and selected based on structural requirements and manufacturing constraints.

\subsubsection{Foam Core Candidates}
Structural foams used in composite manufacturing generally fall into three broad categories: Polymethacrylimide (PMI), Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC), and Extruded Polystyrene (XPS)\footnote{Campbell, F. C. (2010). \textit{Structural Composite Materials}. ASM International. (accessed on 04.06.2026)}. 
PMI foams are high-performance, closed-cell structures known for their exceptional thermal stability and compressive strength. PVC foams offer a good balance of mechanical properties and cost for low-temperature applications but soften rapidly under heat. XPS foams are highly cost-effective and easily machinable but possess very low mechanical strength and thermal resistance. \subsubsection{Final Material Selection}
\label{sec:final_mat_sel}
The primary constraint driving core selection is the manufacturing process. As discussed in \autoref{sub:composites}, the airframe will be manufactured using OOA-cured carbon fiber pre-preg. This exposes the core materials to temperatures exceeding $120^\circ$C. 

For the wings, \textbf{Rohacell 71 IG-F (PMI foam)} is selected. As demonstrated in \autoref{tab:foam_props}, it is the only candidate with a maximum service temperature ($180^\circ$C) capable of surviving the maximum curing temperatures of the chosen pre-preg. %Both the PVC and XPS foams would thermally yield and crush under consolidation pressure.

For the fuselage sandwich structure, the \textbf{4.8mm Cell 48kg Nomex} aramid honeycomb is selected. While aluminum offers superior mechanical properties, the aramid core eliminates the risk of galvanic corrosion and provides the necessary out of plane flexibility to conform to the compound aerodynamic curvatures of the fuselage without buckling. The 48kg variant provides sufficient compressive strength (2.40 MPa) to resist localized crushing during the high-pressure autoclave cure.

\section{Manufacturing}
\label{sec:manufacturing}

The manufacturing section first discusses the out-of-autoclave (OOA) production of the honeycomb-core fuselage and tailcone, as well as the foam-core wings and tail stabilizers. Subsequently, the manufacturing approach for the landing gear and engine pylon is presented.

\subsection{OOA procedure for prepregs}
\label{sub:OOA}

The datasheet for the selected CYCOM 5320-1 prepreg includes recommended Vacuum Bag Only (VBO) cure cycles. These are generally divided into two categories: a continuous cure cycle and a free-standing post-cure cycle. In the continuous process, the part remains on the tool for the entire duration of the cure. In contrast, the free-standing post-cure method involves removing the part from the tool after an initial cure, allowing it to cool before completing the cure in an oven. 

The post-cure approach is particularly advantageous for rapid prototyping, as it frees up the mould earlier in the process. This enables the next part to be prepared while the previous one continues curing, thereby improving overall production efficiency. The recommended curing cycles are summarized in \autoref{tab:cure_cycles}.

It is worth mentioning that the post-cure ramp rate for the freestanding cycles is significantly slower. That is deliberate since the part is unsupported, and a slow ramp minimizes thermal gradients and keeps it from distorting as the resin softens before the extra crosslinking locks in the final geometry. 

An important factor to consider is the total manufacturing time associated with each cycle. The freestanding cycles enable the simultaneous production of two parts: while one part undergoes post-curing, the next can begin its initial cure. This reduces overall time compared to Cycle A, where the part remains in the mould for the entire curing process. However, in Cycles B and C, the cooling phase and lower ramp rates lead to a longer overall manufacturing time.

For comparison, the total manufacturing time is estimated assuming a single mould is used for both the wing and fuselage monocoque, as well as a single OOA oven, which reflects a realistic setup for producing a total of five HUGO units. Cycle A results in a significantly shorter manufacturing time than Cycles B and C, primarily because of the very low post-cure ramp rates. Cycle C is further extended due to the prolonged initial cure of 10 hours at $95^\circ$C. In the case of a single oven, the advantage of simultaneous manufacturing cannot be fully realised, as the post-cure duration significantly exceeds the combined duration of the initial cure and cooling stages.

In terms of dimensional accuracy, Cycle A offers the best performance because the part remains constrained within the mould for the entire curing process. In contrast, the freestanding curing cycles are more susceptible to warping or distortion once the part is removed from the mould. Nevertheless, this risk is mitigated by the low post-cure ramp rates.

Another important consideration is the maximum temperature that the selected honeycomb and foam core materials can withstand during curing. Furthermore, the mould temperature requirements differ substantially between the curing cycles. For the freestanding cycles, the moulds are only exposed to temperatures of approximately $95-120^\circ$C during the initial cure. In contrast, Cycle A requires the moulds to withstand the full cure temperature of $175^\circ$C, which may increase tooling costs and impose stricter material and design requirements on the moulds.

To achieve the temperatures required for curing, an out-of-autoclave (OOA) furnace will be rented. Suitable furnaces are commercially available with sufficient internal volume to accommodate both the fuselage and wing composite components. Rental costs are estimated to be between \texteuro{7,000-12,000}  for a period of 3-5 months\footnote{\href{https://www.airtec-gmbh.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Renting-an-industrial-furnace.pdf}{Technical data: AHT 890-ek tempering furnace} (accessed on 10.06.2026)}, which is a realistic time frame for the manufacturing and remains within the allocated manufacturing budget of \texteuro{45,000} \footnote{\label{url:baseline_report}\href{https://github.com/Teo-Cokonaj/DSE-28/blob/main/docs/Projects/DSE_Group_28_Baseline_Report.pdf}{Baseline Report} (accessed on 21.05.2026)}.



\subsubsection{\textbf{Honeycomb fuselage}}

The fuselage monocoque excludes the swappable tail cone. However, the tail cone will be manufactured using essentially the same process. Separate moulds will be produced for the upper and lower halves of the fuselage.

The mould manufacturing process begins with the machining of a male plug that reproduces the external geometry of the fuselage. This plug can be fabricated from an inexpensive polyurethane (PU) board, as it does not need to withstand elevated curing temperatures. Once machined, the plug is sealed and coated with a release agent to facilitate separation of the final mould.

A high-temperature epoxy tooling gelcoat is then applied to the prepared plug surface. This gelcoat provides the mould with a highly accurate surface finish, which ultimately determines the quality of the outer skin of the manufactured part. Subsequently, multiple layers of carbon fiber fabric and epoxy are laminated over the gelcoat. The mould laminate is built up to a thickness of approximately 5 mm and is reinforced with a stiffening framework structure to ensure adequate rigidity and prevent distortion or sagging during curing.

After lay-up, the mould laminate undergoes an initial low-temperature cure while still supported by the plug. Once cured sufficiently, the mould is demoulded and subjected to a freestanding post-cure cycle in the oven. This post-cure process increases the mould's glass transition temperature (T$_g$) to a value above the cure temperature of the composite parts ($350^\circ$F), ensuring dimensional stability and durability during production. The moulds will require only minimal post-processing, consisting of trimming excess material from the edges, sanding the surface, and applying the final sealer and release agent layers.

The two mould halves will incorporate split-mould clamping bushes to ensure precise alignment during assembly and to prevent any relative misalignment between the upper and lower sections. In addition, these bushes help reduce wear on the mould, provide sufficient clamping force, and maintain dimensional accuracy throughout the curing process \footnote{\href{https://www.easycomposites.co.uk/learning/precision-split-moulds-using-tooling-prepreg}{Making Precision Split-Moulds using Tooling Prepreg \& Mould Bushes} (accessed on 11.06.2026)}.

Once the moulds have been completed, the manufacturing of the honeycomb fuselage can begin. Cycle A will also be applied to the manufacturing of the honeycomb fuselage and tail cone, since the carbon-fiber moulds can tolerate the elevated temperatures associated with the post-curing process.

The outer prepreg skin is laid up on the inner surface of the mould and cured. Although co-curing the complete sandwich in a single cycle is possible with 5320-1, pre-curing the outer skin was selected because it is the aerodynamically critical surface: this prevents honeycomb core print-through, locks the moulded geometry before core placement, and allows surface inspection before committing further material. The trade is one additional cure cycle per fuselage. 

The curing process is carried out as follows. On the moulds treated with a release agent, the prepreg material is first laid up. A peel ply is then applied as the final layer of the laminate. After curing, it can be removed to yield a clean, textured surface suitable for bonding, eliminating the need for additional abrasion or solvent cleaning. A perforated release film is subsequently placed over the peel ply, allowing controlled resin bleed into the breather while preventing the breather from bonding to the laminate.

A breather cloth is then applied to provide an air evacuation pathway. Continuous breather coverage is required to prevent local loss of vacuum within the bag. A vacuum bag film is placed over the entire assembly and sealed against sealant tape positioned along the mould flange. Finally, vacuum is applied through dedicated vacuum ports to consolidate the laminate during the curing cycle. After the part has cooled, the vacuum bag and breather are removed, followed by the peeling off of the peel ply. The cured outer skin is then ready for bonding to the honeycomb core. Suitable processing materials are again listed in the manufacturer's datasheet \footref{fn:cycom}.

Following inspection, the honeycomb core is then placed onto the outer skin with a layer of FM 309-1 film adhesive at the interface, and the inner prepreg skin is laid up over the core with a second layer of FM 309-1 between the core and inner skin. The entire assembly then undergoes a single co-bond cure, simultaneously curing the inner skin and the bondlines. A more detailed overview of how this is done is given in \autoref{sec:assembly}

Since the fuselage terminates at the aft opening where it interfaces with the tail cone, the honeycomb core remains exposed at this location. Consequently, the core edge must be sealed with a suitable filler material to protect it from moisture ingress and local impact damage. EPOCAST 1617 A/B has been selected for this purpose, as it is a low-density, self-extinguishing compound specifically developed for sealing the edges of honeycomb sandwich structures \footnote{\href{https://products.huntsman.com/products/epocast-1617-a-b-ixgqyzlv}{EPOCAST 1617 A/B} (accessed on 11.06.2026)}.

An important consideration is the joining of the two sections before curing. At the seam where the sandwich structures are joined, a potential weak point arises from discontinuities in both the prepreg skins and the honeycomb core. To reinforce the joint at the parting line, the outer skin of the lower half is extended, while the outer skin of the upper half is slightly recessed to create a lap joint. This lap-joint configuration increases the bonding area compared with a direct honeycomb-to-honeycomb joint, resulting in improved load transfer and joint strength. Additionally, the inner skin of the upper half also extends beyond the edge of the honeycomb core, allowing it to overlap with the inner skin of the lower half. This overlap facilitates the formation of a strong and continuous bond during the curing process. A more detailed overview of this step is given in \autoref{sec:assembly}.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Foam wings}}
As shown in \autoref{tab:foam_props}, the selected foam core material is Rohacell 71 IG-F (PMI), which has a maximum service temperature of $180^\circ$C. Since this temperature exceeds the maximum curing temperature considered in all three curing cycles, the foam core does not impose any constraints on the curing process. As Cycle A is the fastest and provides the best dimensional accuracy, it is selected for the curing process.

The wing and tail stabilizer cores are produced by CNC machining Rohacell 71 IG-F blocks to the final aerodynamic profile. After machining, the foam surface is lightly sanded to remove machining residue and open the surface cell structure, improving subsequent bond quality. No sealing of the foam surface is performed, as this would interfere with adhesive contact.

A structural film adhesive, FM309-1 (supported, with carrier scrim), is applied directly to the prepared foam surface prior to skin lay-up. The carrier scrim limits adhesive infiltration into the open-cell foam structure, maintaining a controlled bond line thickness and preventing resin starvation at the interface. FM309-1 was selected primarily for supply chain consolidation, as it is already qualified for bonding the honeycomb sandwich structures used elsewhere on the aircraft, reducing the number of qualified adhesive systems, freezer storage requirements, and associated handling procedures. Its cure temperature of $121^\circ$C is compatible with Cycle A, allowing the adhesive and prepreg resin to co-cure in a single cycle without requiring a secondary bonding step.

Following adhesive application, the required number of CYCOM 5320-1 prepreg plies are laid up directly onto the adhesive film. The assembly is then vacuum bagged and cured according to Cycle A, identical to the cycle used for the fuselage and tail cone structures.

\subsection{Landing gear and engine pylons}
\label{sub:manufacturing_LG}

The manufacturing of the landing gear and engine pylons remains the same as stated in the Midterm Report \footref{ft:midterm_rep}. A short overview of the process is given below.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{CNC Machining and Turning} shall be used to produce three-dimensional component profiles and axial geometries. Stress-relieved stock (e.g., T7651/T76511 plate or extrusions) shall be specified to maintain dimensional stability during and after machining.

\item \textbf{Deep Hole Drilling} shall be applied to long, slender components such as piston rods, outer cylinders, and axles where high dimensional accuracy and surface quality are required.

\item \textbf{Heat Treatment} shall be applied to relieve residual stresses and enhance strength. Depending on the component requirements, processes may include annealing, solution heat treatment, ageing, or homogenising.

\item \textbf{Surface Treatment and Protection} shall be applied to improve corrosion and wear resistance. Protective measures against galvanic corrosion at CFRP interfaces may include anodising, conversion coatings, primers, isolation layers, fastener isolation, or sacrificial anodes.

\end{enumerate}



\section{Assembly}
\label{sec:assembly}

The assembly section outlines the different methods employed for the fuselage assembly, the pylon-to-fuselage connections, and the landing gear assembly.

\subsection{Fuselage assembly}
\label{sub:fuselage_assembly}

After curing the outer skins, the honeycomb core is bonded to them using a layer of adhesive film. The inner skins are then positioned on the opposite side of the honeycomb core with an additional layer of adhesive film. Once both halves are assembled and ready for Cycle A, a further layer of adhesive film is applied at the outer-skin lap joint. Another adhesive film layer is placed along the parting line over the honeycomb core to ensure proper bonding during the cure cycle. This can be seen in \autoref{fig:joint}.

The selected honeycomb adhesive is FM 410-1, as it enables honeycomb splicing at ambient pressure and offers a broad curing temperature range, up to $177^{\circ}$C. Furthermore, it is manufactured by the same supplier as the previously selected FM 309-1 adhesive, providing confidence in material compatibility within the bonding system.

The two mould halves are then joined and secured using the split-mould clamping bushes. An internal vacuum bag is inserted through the rear opening at the tail cone interface. To prevent bridging during evacuation, the selected vacuum bag has a diameter larger than that of the internal cavity, allowing it to expand freely and conform to the geometry. A self-releasing tubular vacuum bag is used, eliminating the need for peel ply or release film and allowing direct contact with the prepreg surface. The tubular bag is positioned inside the fuselage cavity, while the entire mould assembly is enclosed within a second external vacuum bag. Once vacuum is applied, air is removed from both bags, causing them to conform tightly to the laminate surfaces. This arrangement ensures that sufficient consolidation pressure is applied to the inner skin throughout the curing process \footnote{\href{https://www.easycomposites.eu/learning/split-mould-carbon-fibre-tube}{Laminating and Bagging a Carbon Fiber Tube Using a Split Mould} (accessed on 11.06.2026)}. Finally, the fuselage assembly undergoes a final curing cycle to join the two halves together.

\subsection{Pylon-to-fuselage connections}
\label{sub:pylon_to_fuselage}

Engine pylons are attached to the fuselage sandwich structure using cylindrical metal inserts that enable mechanical fastening and efficient load transfer. These inserts mitigate the inherent limitations of composite sandwich cores, particularly their low through-thickness stiffness, by providing reinforced attachment points for bolts and other fasteners. This reduces the likelihood of failure modes such as delamination, core shear, or facesheet pull-through under tensile, shear, or pull-out loading \cite{Severson2024}.

The installation process begins with drilling holes from the aft side of the fuselage at the designated pylon attachment locations. Access for drilling is provided through the rear fuselage opening, which remains accessible prior to installation of the tailcone. Cylindrical metal inserts spanning the full sandwich thickness are then inserted into the prepared holes and bonded in place using 3M Scotch-Weld EC-3439 HT epoxy adhesive, a void-filling aerospace-grade compound designed for structural applications. The adhesive cures in approximately 60 minutes at $121^{\circ}$C without the need for applied pressure \footnote{\href{https://products.huntsman.com/products/epibond-8000-fr-a-b-qeuj1kf4}{EPIBOND® 8000 FR A/B} (accessed on 11.06.2026)}.

At the interface between metal inserts and the carbon fiber skin, galvanic corrosion can occur in the presence of moisture. This risk is particularly pronounced when aluminium is used in direct contact with carbon fiber, as the large difference in electrochemical potential between the materials drives galvanic coupling and subsequent corrosion of the less noble metal. To mitigate this issue, Ti-6Al-4V will be used for the metal inserts. Titanium offers improved electrochemical compatibility with carbon fiber composites, effectively reducing or eliminating galvanic corrosion risks. In addition, its high strength and corrosion resistance make it well-suited for structural applications where durability and long-term reliability are critical \cite{Melhem2019}.

Once the adhesive has cured, the bolts are installed through the metal sleeves rather than directly through the composite core. Wide-area washers are then used to distribute the clamping loads across the outer skins, thereby reducing local stress concentrations and improving overall joint durability.

\subsection{Landing gear assembly}
\label{sub:lg_assembly}

The landing gear assembly shall employ mechanical fastening rather than bonded joints. Bolted and lockbolted connections are used for rigid structural interfaces, providing reliable load transfer and ease of inspection and maintenance. In contrast, pin joints are implemented at locations requiring controlled relative motion, such as torque links and actuator attachment points, enabling necessary articulation during operation. Welding is excluded from the design due to its potential to alter the microstructure of high-strength steel alloys, which can result in reduced mechanical performance and compromised fatigue resistance.

\section{Integration}
\label{sec:integration}

The integration of the remaining systems will proceed in the same manner as outlined in the Midterm Report \footref{ft:midterm_rep}. A summary of the process is given below.

Aircraft system integration follows a carefully planned sequence based primarily on accessibility and system dependencies. Components that are difficult to access or that other systems rely on are installed first to avoid rework and installation conflicts later in the process.

The avionics wiring harness is typically installed first, before the airframe is obstructed by interior structures or equipment. This harness forms the backbone of the aircraft’s electrical and data networks, linking critical systems such as avionics, landing gear, engines, nacelles, and various airframe components. In modern fly-by-wire aircraft, these interconnections are especially vital, as they enable continuous communication between flight control computers, sensors, and actuators.

Next, the aircraft battery is installed as part of the electrical power system, followed by the installation of avionics equipment, including flight computers, sensors, and cockpit displays. These systems are then connected to the pre-installed wiring harness.

After the core electrical and avionics systems are in place, the engines are installed and integrated with the fuel, hydraulic, and electrical systems. Finally, the interior is fitted. Because it can be more easily removed for maintenance or modifications, it is installed last, sealing and protecting the underlying systems.

Following this structured sequence is essential - deviations can lead to costly rework, scheduling delays, increased expenses, and potential safety concerns.

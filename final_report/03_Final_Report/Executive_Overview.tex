\chapter{Executive Overview}
\label{ch:executive_overview}

The High-speed Unmanned General-purpose Observatory (HUGO) is a reusable, modular and adaptable 50 kg transonic flying laboratory designed to make high-speed aeroelastic and aeroservoelastic flight testing more accessible. Instead of being designed around a single fixed aircraft configuration, HUGO is a configurable testbed UAV. This allows HUGO to bridge the gap between wind tunnel testing and full scale flight testing by providing real-world atmospheric data at lower cost and lower risk than conventional flight test programmes.

The core feature of HUGO is its modularity. The aircraft can accommodate interchangeable main wing planforms within the defined Planform Envelope, allowing researchers to test different aspect ratios, sweep angles, taper ratios, thickness distributions, and aeroelastic characteristics. In addition, HUGO includes a modular canard port, which can be covered when not in use, and replaceable empennage components that enable alternative configurations. The internal layout further supports additional payloads, secondary flight computers, sensors, and additional control systems supplied by the client.

Through this architecture, HUGO can support a wide range of missions, including the testing of high-aspect-ratio (HAR) wings \cite{AFONSO201740, MA2024100983}, Active Flutter Suppression (AFS) \cite{Gilyard1983, Takarics2019}, and Gust Load Alleviation (GLA) systems. \cite{Sanghi2022} These experiments are performed during a mission profile that includes a five-minute test segment at Mach 0.75, a regime where conventional transonic wind tunnels suffer from wall interference and size limitations \cite{Stenfelt2015}, often costing upwards of €40,000 per occupancy day\footnote{\label{foot:schrijer-exec}In personal communication with Ferdinand Schrijer on 04.05.2026.}, while manned flight testing introduces severe safety risks and lengthy certification procedures \cite{Spitz2001}. By offering a certified, reconfigurable testbed operating on Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) \cite{Gaillot2023}, HUGO provides a repeatable and accessible platform for validating next-generation aircraft technologies.

\section{Profitability and Value Proposition}
\label{sec:exec-objective_and_value_poposition}

To evaluate the commercial feasibility of HUGO, a market volume analysis was conducted. While the Total Addressable Market (TAM) encompasses the broader global expenditure on aerospace testing and validation, HUGO's initial beachhead strategy specifically targets the domestic Dutch testing ecosystem. Based on the collective testing budgets of major institutions such as the Netherlands Aerospace Centre (NLR), the German-Dutch Wind Tunnels (DNW), and TU Delft, the Serviceable Available Market (SAM) is valued at €71 million annually \footref{foot:midterm}. By providing a direct alternative to the high-speed aeroelastic testing traditionally performed in wind tunnels, HUGO aims to capture roughly 20\% \footref{foot:schrijer-exec} of this regional market, yielding a Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) of €14.2 million per year. Furthermore, the drastic reduction in testing costs and lead times is expected to induce latent demand, expanding the overall market boundaries by allowing smaller academic and commercial entities to conduct research that was previously cost-prohibitive.

A financial analysis was performed to establish an economic factor of safety for HUGO's developmental and certification costs. The upfront Capital Expenditure (CAPEX) was estimated to be €3.1 million, encompassing the research and development, certification, prototyping and testing costs, as well as the cost of producing an initial fleet of 5 UAVs. At a targeted commercial rate of €25,000 per flight day, with the initial fleet performing 20 flights per year per drone, HUGO is projected to generate €2.5 million per year. This captures 18\% of the calculated €14.2M SOM. The direct costs per flight day total €4,500, including the logistics and transport costs, fuel, electricity, maintenance costs and crew wages. This yields a gross margin per flight day of €20,500. Additionally, the annual operational overhead of HUGO, including rent and permanent staff costs, was estimated to be €475,000.

The net annual operating profit of HUGO is thus €1.65M. Considering a venture debt model for the initial CAPEX acquisition, at a 10\% interest rate, project HUGO is able to afford the loan payments in order to pay off the debt in 5 years. A sensitivity study was performed on the different financial factors and costs to arrive at a margin of safety for each factor. The fixed and variable operational costs, as well as the research and development costs were found to have the highest margin of safety, and therefore are not the driving factors of the feasibility of this project. The number of flights per year and price per flight day had a margin of safety of 22\% and 18\% respectively. Therefore it is crucial for the success of HUGO to be able to reliably achieve the required number of flight days, requiring a highly reliable airframe. It is also critical to have a highly versatile testing platform which will be able to capture the largest possible market. Additionally, capturing high quality data reliably will allow HUGO to be priced at a premium, maximizing revenue.


\section{Final Configuration and Modularity}
\label{sec:exec-final_configuration}

Following a rigorous Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) trade-off, HUG-CFG-303 was selected as the optimal configuration. This high-wing configuration features static wing mounting points, a T-tail, and a modular canard port, providing maximum experimental versatility without the structural penalties associated with more complex airframes with multiple wing mounting points. 

To fulfil its role as a universal testbed, HUGO is designed around a defined ``Planform Envelope.'' This allows clients to test wings with aspect ratios ranging from 5 to 27, sweeps up to 20$^{\circ}$, and various dihedral and thickness profiles, all while ensuring stable and controllable flight characteristics \cite{nelson1998flight}. For missions that do not require a custom client wing, HUGO employs a Standard Planform featuring a NASA SC(2)-0012 supercritical airfoil \cite{Harris1990}, optimized for stable, high-subsonic operations. This is paired with a standard HUGO empennage, which features a T-tail configuration. 

Additionally, HUGO features a canard mounting point, which is covered when not being used to prevent an aerodynamic penalty. This allows HUGO to be configured with a canard on client request, expanding HUGO's mission envelope. An additional benefit of this feature is that it allows for the testing of highly experimental planform at a smaller scale on these ports, without having to design, manufacture and test a new main planform.

In order to minimize the drag profile of HUGO, the landing gear is a fully retractable system, which stows away during flight to produce a clean aerodynamic profile of the fuselage.

\section{Engineering Design and Performance Outcomes}
\label{sec:exec-eperformance_outcomes}

The technical execution of HUGO ensures that the aircraft can perform demanding test profiles while adhering to the strict user and operational requirements:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structural Integrity \& Materials:} The primary structure utilizes out-of-autoclave (OOA) CYCOM 5320-1 carbon fiber epoxy prepreg \cite{CENTEA2015132}, cured via a continuous cycle to maximize dimensional accuracy. The fuselage features a Nomex honeycomb core \cite{Boukharouba2014}, while the lifting surfaces utilize a Polymethacrylimide (PMI) foam core. Structural analysis confirms the airframe withstands the ultimate load factor of 9g, with a maximum normal bending stress of 696 MPa - well below the material's yield limit. Furthermore, aeroelastic modelling confirms flutter and static divergence occur well above the Mach 0.75 operating speed \cite{wright2008introduction}. This material choice informed the manufacturing plan, as well as the structural analysis done later. The airframe was decided to be built using a composite monocoque architecture. This simplifies construction by aleviating the need for ribs, stringers and other reinforcements, and maximizes the internal volume for the payload, sensors and other sub-systems.
    \item \textbf{Flight Test Instrumentation:} As a flying laboratory, HUGO's value relies on high-fidelity data acquisition. The standardized payload suite includes dual LIDAR sensors, aimed at the wings, for in-flight wing deflection measurement, 16 strain gauges placed on various points on the wing skin, and a DJI O4 camera system to visually monitor aeroelastic behaviour. Additionally, the wing-to-fuselage structural link is formed by four Sunrise Instruments M3564 EI 6-axis load cells \footnote{\href{https://www.srisensor.com/uploads/M8126-Users-Manual-V1.4-Eng11.pdf}{M8126} (accessed on 03.06.2026)}, which isolate and measure aerodynamic forces on the wing. This is in addition to standard avionics sensors such as a 6 axis Inertial measurement unit (IMU), Global Positioning System (GPS) sensor and gyroscope. HUGO also has space to accomodate an additional 5kg of payload in a 5 liter volume, to house test equipment or additional sensors based on the client's needs. The positioning of these components was done in a preliminary 3D model, along with their masses. Doing so allowed us to accurately estimate the CG of the aircraft, as well as wing positioning, and empennage sizing for stability.
    \item \textbf{Performance:} Using a typical mission profile for HUGO, including a climb phase, cruise phase, test phase, and descend phase, as well as contingencies in case of go arounds, fuel mass fractions were computed. Then, a payload-range analysis was used to validate that HUGO can successfully complete its 30-minute nominal mission—including a 5-minute Mach 0.75 test segment at FL270, while holding sufficient fuel reserves for loitering and up to three missed approach attempts \cite{Koutz1952Loitering}. This analysis allowed us to use a matching diagram to find a feasible engine and wing option. This was driven by constraints and requirements such as runway length, climb rate requirements, and cruise and maximum speed requirements. This yielded our choice for the standard planform, as detailed in \autoref{tab:exec:standard_wing_planform}. The thrust to weight ratio yielded by this analysis drove our choice of engines, namely, twin JetCat P100-RX-BL Jet turbine engines.
    \item \textbf{Propulsion and Power:} Thrust is provided by twin fuselage-mounted JetCat P100-RX-BL micro-turbines, providing a combined maximum thrust of 200N, fed by a dynamic fuel management system, utilizing two 7.5L fuel bladders. This allows us to dynamically shift the CG during flight in order to change the stability of the aircraft to run different experiments. Electrical power is supplied by a custom 8S5P Molicel INR21700 battery pack \cite{Tsung-Hsun2026, Ganjeizadeh2014}, delivering 936 W to sustain the entire avionics, sensor, and propulsion systems for the maximum 45-minute flight window. 
    \item \textbf{Landing Gear \& Kinematics:} The retractable, podded tricycle landing gear is manufactured from 18Ni(350) steel to withstand high impact loads while minimizing strut diameter. Optimized via a Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) spring-dashpot simulation ($C=781$, $K=48168$), the system restricts maximum vertical displacement to 6.84 cm during a 3 m/s impact (incorporating an EASA-mandated 1.5 safety factor), preventing fuselage ground strikes and oscillatory runway behavior. This system limits landing gear loads transferred to the fuselage to below 8g.
    
\end{itemize}

\section{Material Selection and Manufacturing, Assembly, and Integration (MAI)}
\label{sec:mai_plan}

The material selection and MAI strategy for HUGO are closely related. The chosen materials and manufacturing processes directly dictate the final mechanical properties, add constraints on design decisions and impact the economic feasibility of the product. Given HUGO's intended production scale (an initial fleet of five aircraft), the design heavily prioritizes out-of-autoclave (OOA) processes to minimize tooling infrastructure costs while maintaining the structural integrity required for Mach 0.75 aeroelastic testing.

\subsection{Material Selection and Design Impact}
\label{subsec:exec-material_selection}
Materials were selected to satisfy specific local loading conditions, thermal environments, and manufacturing limitations across the airframe:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Composite Sandwich Structures:} The primary fuselage and tailcone are constructed using CYCOM 5320-1 carbon fiber epoxy prepreg \cite{CENTEA2015132}. This specific resin system is optimized for Vacuum Bag Only (VBO) / OOA curing, dramatically reducing manufacturing costs and cycle times compared to traditional autoclave prepregs. A structural analysis performed on a portion of the fuselage showed that a composite sandwich structure, comprising of 2 quasi-isotropic lay ups of CFRP, separated by a honeycomb core, performs comparably to a purely carbon lay up several times the thickness. The results of this analysis are presented in \autoref{tab:exec-fuselage_handling_loads}. The honeycomb sandwich structure weighs 82\% less than the solid carbon structure, and was therefore chosen as the preferred construction method \footnote{\href{https://www.dmcrf.com/benefits-of-honeycomb-core-structures-in-aerospace-and-automotive-sectors/}{Benefits of Honeycomb Core Structures in Aerospace and Automotive Sectors} (accessed on 04.06.2026)}. A 48 kg/m$^3$ Aramid (Nomex) honeycomb core is utilized in the fuselage. Aramid was selected over aluminum honeycomb because it inherently prevents galvanic corrosion with the carbon fiber skins, possesses sufficient compliance to conform to the fuselage curvature without buckling, and provides the compressive strength necessary to resist local crushing under vacuum pressure during the curing cycle \footnote{\href{https://www.chalcoaluminum.com/blog/aluminum-honeycomb-core-vs-aramid-honeycomb-core-2604-zmy/}{Aluminum Honeycomb Core vs Aramid Honeycomb Core: How to Choose the Right Core Material} (accessed on 04.06.2026)}. 
    \item \textbf{Lifting Surfaces:} To support HUGO's modularity requirement, the wings and tail stabilizers utilize a Polymethacrylimide (PMI) foam core rather than honeycomb. PMI is highly cost-effective, easily CNC-machined to accommodate various custom airfoil profiles, and maintains excellent mechanical stability during the curing cycles required by our choice of pre preg.
    \item \textbf{Metal Components:} 18Ni(350) Maraging steel is utilized for the landing gear and engine pylons. Its exceptionally high yield strength permits smaller strut diameters, which is critical for minimizing the aerodynamic drag of the external landing gear pods. Furthermore, steel easily withstands the thermal radiation from the adjacent micro-turbines. For secondary connection ports where structural and thermal loads are minimal, standard aluminum alloys are employed to reduce weight and machining time. 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Manufacturing and Assembly Strategy}
\label{subsec:exec-MAI}
To maximize structural efficiency, HUGO's assembly relies on co-curing and adhesive bonding rather than heavy mechanical fasteners:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fuselage and Wing Assembly:} Achieving a smooth, aerodynamic surface finish is critical for the high velocities that HUGO will be operating at. Therefore, the fuselage outer skins are cured first in negative moulds. Once the outer, aerodynamic surface is established, the Nomex core and inner skins are layered with an adhesive film layer in between the honeycomb and carbon fiber sheets. The two fuselage halves are then joined by overlapping the outer skin edges into a continuous lap joint. A final curing cycle bonds the entire assembly into a rigid, seamless monocoque structure. The wings, however, will be produced by direct foam lay-up. The PMI foam core will be CNC machined, including the internal channels necessary for the subsystems within the wing. After sanding the surface of the foam, an adhesive layer will be used before laying up the required number of prepreg sheets to achieve the necessary mechanical properties of the wing. The wing will then be vacuum bagged and cured in an oven. Since different experiments performed by HUGO may require new wings to be produced, the minimization of production cost, time and complexity is of high importance. The major advantage of this method for the production of the wings and aerodynamic surfaces is the lack of moulds, drastically reducing the cost of production.
    \item \textbf{Pylon to Fuselage Integration:} The high, concentrated point loads generated by the micro-turbines must be safely distributed into the composite fuselage. This is achieved by potting metal inserts into holes drilled through the sandwich structure, sealing the exposed honeycomb core with structural epoxy. Crucially, these inserts are manufactured from titanium. Unlike aluminum, titanium is electrochemically compatible with carbon fiber, thereby preventing galvanic corrosion and ensuring the long-term reliability of the engine mounts.
    \item \textbf{Mechanical Subsystems:} The steel landing gear and engine pylons are CNC-machined and heat-treated to achieve their final temper, and treated with a protective surface coating to prevent corrosion. The landing gear integration utilizes a hybrid approach: bolted joints secure the rigid interfaces to the airframe, while precision pin joints are used exclusively where relative kinematic motion is required during retraction and deployment.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{System Integration}
\label{subsec:exec-system_integration}
The final integration of HUGO is heavily driven by system dependencies and physical accessibility. Because the fuselage is a bonded monocoque, internal component integration follows a strict bottom-up approach. Wiring harnesses, the structural load cells, and the dynamic fuel bladder system are installed prior to the integration of the modular lifting surfaces, ensuring that critical avionics and fuel lines remain accessible for inspection and condition-based maintenance between flight test days. The access bays are also sized and placed in strategic areas to allow for the removal of the internal components to facilitate servicing and repairs.


\section{Certification, and Safety}
\label{sec:exec-certification_and_safet}

In consultation with, and with guidance from EASA, HUGO was classified under SC-Light-UAS High risk. Successful certification of an Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) with such a high degree of modularity would require constant and expensive recertification, making this project infeasible. However, through the simultaneous application for a type certificate and a Design Organisation Approval (DOA), HUGO will be allowed to undergo major changes without requiring full recertification. Though more versatile, it is a process that will require an initial investment of \euro{10,170} for the certification, and then a yearly fee of \euro{11,050} for the maintenance of the Type Certificate. For compliance, a team of engineers will therefore have to apply for the DOA, and be responsible for approving any major changes proposed by a client.

To ensure operational safety and regulatory compliance within the EASA SC-Light-UAS High-risk category, a comprehensive technical risk assessment was conducted. Robust preventive measures and autonomous contingency protocols were developed for all operational hazards, ensuring the system can safely and reliably execute 100 test days per year without risking the safety of personnel or the airspace.

Safety is ensured through a selective redundancy philosophy and a rigorous condition-based maintenance (CBM) or time-based maintenance (TBM) schedule. Critical systems, including the VECTOR-600 autopilot and power distribution, are monitored continuously \cite{Imani2022}. In the event of communication loss or envelope exceedance, autonomous stabilization and flight termination protocols guarantee safe recovery. The overall system reliability is calculated to ensure the initial fleet of five aircraft can achieve and axceed the 100 operational test days per year \cite{Mencik2016, Lanzafame2024}.



\section{Operations and Mission Envelope}
\label{sec:exec-operations_and_mission}

Operations and Logistics are a crucial part in the development of a successful mission. Together they cover all aspects of HUGO's day-to-day usage. For a mission to be successful, HUGO must operate in a controlled airspace, fly over 100km in a straight line, and to perform a test at Mach 0.75 for at leasts 5 minutes. Given its classification as a High-Risk Unmanned Aircraft System under EASA Special Conditions \cite{easa_light_uas_crd_is3}, HUGO's operational and safety framework is designed for maximum reliability. Operations are based at Den Helder Airport (EHKD), capitalizing on its 1.27 km runway, emergency services, and direct access to ATC-controlled airspace over the North Sea \cite{DenHelderAirport2013DHANieuwsbrief}.

To facilitate the operations and reduce transport costs, HUGO is to be stored in a hangar in the area adjacent to Den Helder airport. Although not a commercial airport, Den Helder is the main gateway to off-shore platforms in the North Sea, thus daily traffic is expected. As such, the test flight plans will be coordinated with De Kooy Air Traffic Control. HUGO's propulsion system uses Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF), which will need to be stored in the hangar and brought to the airport for each flight day as Den Helder airport does not offer SAF refuelling services. Lastly, the handling of HUGO; transport, flight, and ground support will be performed by trained personnel.

When a client proposes a research campaign, the experiment is first evaluated against HUGO's established planform and mission envelopes. If the experiment requires a custom wing, it can either be manufactured by the client in strict accordance with HUGO's integration guidelines, or manufactured in-house to the client's specifications. Crucially, the internal engineering team utilizes the Design Organisation Approval (DOA) to independently certify the new configuration and structural integration for flight. Once certified, a mission profile is generated to meet the specific testing requirements of the client. 

HUGO is capable of executing a broad range of high-speed aeroelastic and flight dynamics missions, including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Active Flutter Suppression (AFS) and Gust Load Alleviation (GLA) using external, client-provided control systems and sensors.
    \item Aileron reversal control and software based mitigation techniques.
    \item Testing of unconventional effectors, such as leading-edge controls and canard control surfaces.
    \item Canard-specific aeroelastic testing utilizing the modular forward port.
    \item Validation of general aeroelastic behaviour, lift distributions, and stress profiles along custom planforms.
    \item Evaluation of highly complex architectures, such as high-subsonic truss-braced wings.
\end{itemize}

To safely execute these missions, operations are based at Den Helder Airport (EHKD), capitalising on its 1.27 km runway, emergency services, and direct access to ATC-controlled airspace over the North Sea \cite{DenHelderAirport2013DHANieuwsbrief}. To facilitate operations and reduce transport costs, HUGO is stored in an adjacent hangar. Here, the aircraft undergoes pre-flight preparations, payload integration, and is refuelled with Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) prior to being transported to the flight line.

On the flight day, following the comprehensive pre-flight systems checks and the acquisition of an approved flight window from De Kooy Air Traffic Control, HUGO is cleared for take-off. The flight is actively monitored from a Ground Control Station (GCS) manned by a minimum of three trained personnel: the primary pilot, a backup pilot, and a Visual Observer (VO). 

During the Mach 0.75 test segment over the North Sea, the GCS displays a live video feed from HUGO's onboard camera alongside critical flight telemetry. A subset of the experimental sensor data is also transmitted live to the researchers to verify that the experiment is proceeding correctly and that data is being successfully captured. Concurrently, the complete, high-fidelity dataset from the load cells, strain gauges, and LIDAR sensors is securely recorded to the onboard SSD for post-flight extraction and analysis.



\section{Environmental Responsibility}
\label{sec:exec-environmental-responsibility}

The entire project was created with the intention of enabling the research and development of novel wing configurations which could lower emissions. The research can also lead to safer and more comfortable aircraft. It was a requirement from the beginning that the HUGO should be powered by SAF to minimize the overall impact. A Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) was made to directly quantify the impact of the entire project.   

For any engineering project it is vital to understand how it directly affects its surroundings. This can be done  by looking at the noise and emissions generated by the  flight of the aircraft. By stipulating a maximum noise-level at 90 db(A) it is possible to have an upper bound for the noise. This number was chosen based on the general guidelines for jet-aircraft in ICAO Annex 16 \cite{ICAO_Annex16}. After performing the analysis the maximum noise was found to be 88 dB(A) thereby with this upper bound. Using emission data for the burning of Jet-A fuel it was possible to identify the different compounds which makes up the emission. Then evaluating each of the compounds based on the Global Warming Potential (GWP) of 1 kg of burned fuel to be 10.923 GWP. This emission model would be used in the LCA to estimate the impact of the operation. 

After finalizing the design, operations and the sizing of all systems and subsystems it was possible to run the final LCA. The complete estimate for the impact from cradle to grave for the HUGO project would be: $697642.50 \text{ kg CO}_2\text{ eq}$. 



\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:exec-conclusion}

HUGO represents a systemic shift in high-speed aeroelastic research. By providing a highly modular, DOA-certified platform capable of collecting highly relevant research data at Mach 0.75, it directly eliminates the financial and technical bottlenecks of traditional wind tunnel testing. Operating out of Den Helder on Sustainable Aviation Fuel at a disruptive commercial rate of €25,000 per flight day, HUGO represents a commercially viable product which facilitates the future of aviation. Through its efficient composite architecture and adaptable mission envelope, the platform successfully fulfils its core purpose: democratising access to transonic flight testing and accelerating the development of the next generation of highly efficient, low carbon impact aircraft. A final choice of C = 781.115 and K = 48168.2, for a maximum allowable displacement of 6.84 cm at a downward velocity of 2m/s with an added safety factor of 1.5 as prescribed by EASA in SC-UAS High Risk. The effect of transverse loads on the landing gear during landing were taken into account during the material selection.
    

-Material characteristics + MAI (they are now one section):
    Materials were selected to ensure reliable performance in the high-subsonic regime, including resistance to aeroelastic effects such as flutter, while keeping manufacturing costs low for limited production volumes.

    -Steel alloy was chosen for the landing gear and engine pylon due to its high melting temperature and ability to achieve the required strength with smaller strut diameters. Aluminium alloy was used for the connection ports, where loads and thermal demands are comparatively low.

    -A sandwich composite panel was selected for the fuselage and tailcone, using a prepreg system suitable for out-of-autoclave (OOA) curing. This reduces manufacturing cost and cycle time, making it appropriate for low-rate production. Although aluminium could offer higher intrinsic strength, the aramid core avoids galvanic corrosion and provides sufficient compliance to conform to fuselage curvature without buckling. The 48 kg core variant was selected for adequate compressive strength to resist local crushing during cure.

    -For the wings and tail stabilisers, a PMI foam core was used due to its cost-effectiveness and suitable mechanical performance for low-temperature curing cycles.

    Once the material has been selected, the next step is to come up with the MAI plan. The manufacturing and assembly of the plan are just as important as the material selection for the final product quality and material characteristics.

    - The selected prepreg is optimised for OOA/VBO curing cycle. A continuous curing cycle was selected, during which the part remains on the mould. It also provided the lowest manufacturing time and the best dimensional accuracy. The honeycomb fuselage and tailcone are manufactured by first curing the outerskins in a separate curing cycle to achieve the desired aerodynamic finish, then the honeycomb core and inner skin are layered on each half with films of adhesive and the final assembly (both moulds held together undergoes a final curing cycle. The manufacturing of the foam wings consists of CNC machining the foam and preparing it for the layering of the prepreg with adhesive film. It then undergoes the same curing cycle. The landing gear and engine pylons are CNC-machined, heat-treated, and undergo an application of a protective surface coating.

    -The assembly process for the honeycomb structures consists of connecting the two mould halves that would then undergo a final curing cycle to produce the monocoque structure. After the outer skins have been cured and the honeycomb core and inner skin have been layered on them, the outer skins have to be connected by an adhesive film by overlapping their edges as a lap joint. An adhesive film layer is also applied at the honeycomb interfaces, essentially connecting the two mould halves, and the assembly undergoes the final curing cycle.

    -the pylon to fuselage connections will utilise metal inserts that will be inserted in holes drilled in the sandwich structure. The exposed honeycomb core will be sealed at those locations with an epoxy that bonds the metal inserts to the inside of the fuselage structure. Titanium is used due to its improved electrochemical compatibility with carbon fiber composites.

    -the landing gear assembly uses bolted connections for the rigid interfaces and pin joints where relative motion is required

According to HUG-OPS-005\footref{url:baseline_report}, HUGO is required to operate at speeds up to Mach 0.75. This is the flight condition at which tests are to be performed. In this section, an estimation of the lift characteristics of HUGO is performed based on statistics from suitable reference aircraft, along with a preliminary airfoil selection suitable for the operations.

Airfoil selection begins with an analysis of reference aircraft and an estimate of the design lift coefficient $C_{l_\text{des}}$. Subsequently, the maximum lift coefficient $C_{l_{\text{max}}}$ is derived along with relevant aerospace parameters to facilitate the selection. For the purpose of this analysis, it is assumed that HUGO will operate at a speed of Mach 0.75, at an altitude of FL270 (8230 m).

Several steps have to be conducted beforehand to obtain these coefficients. Due to the specified testing conditions, $C_{L_\text{des}}$ was selected for the Mach 0.75 cruise airspeed condition at FL270 (8230 m).}

\subsection{Design Aerodynamic Parameters}
\label{subsec:cl_des}
The initial geometric parameters from \autoref{tab:DAST_HUGO} can be used to derive HUGO's preliminary aerodynamic characteristics.

\subsubsection*{Design Lift Coefficient}
To estimate $C_{L_\text{des}}$, \autoref{eq:C_L} \cite{Torenbeek1982} can be applied for a cruise air density $\rho_{cr}$ of $0.5112 \ kg \cdot m^{-3}$ at FL270, and a cruise speed $V_{cr}$ of $230.2945 \ m \cdot s^{-1}$ corresponding to the test condition. Using the initial value for $W/S$ from \autoref{tab:DAST_HUGO}, an initial $C_{L_\text{des}}$ of 0.074 is obtained.
%It includes the air density at the altitude of 27,000 feet $\rho_{cr}$ and the airspeed $V_{cr}$, for which it holds that $V_{cr} = M \cdot \sqrt{\gamma R T}$ at the ISA altitude of 27,000 ft.

\begin{equation}
    C_{L_\text{des}} = \frac{\left(\frac{W}{S}\right)}{\frac{1}{2} \rho_{cr} V_{cr}^2} \approx 0.074
    \label{eq:C_L}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Airfoil Selection}
\label{subsec:airfoil_selection}
Since the aircraft is expected to operate at Mach 0.75, it is necessary to take transonic phenomena into account. When flying in this region with a traditional airfoil, the airflow might locally be supersonic, which creates strong shock waves on the wing \cite{Whitcomb1974}. This leads to a large increase in drag, which is detrimental for the performance of the aircraft and introduces boundary layer separation. Such effects can be mitigated by the use of a supercritical airfoil. 

A supercritical airfoil is capable to operate at transonic conditions with only the onset of weak shock waves \cite{Harris1990}. An example of such airfoils used in the preliminary design for HUGO is a supercritical SC(2)-XXYY family developed by NASA, where XX is the airfoil design lift coefficient, and YY is the maximum thickness in percent \cite{Harris1990}. The advantage of using this system is the inclusion of the design lift coefficient, which makes it possible to narrow down the choices for HUGO using $C_{L_\text{des}}$ values found in \autoref{subsec:cl_des}.

Based on the data obtained in the previous subsections, NASA SC(2)-0012 was chosen as the preliminary airfoil for HUGO. It is a symmetrical, supercritical airfoil, with properties matching the requirements for HUGO's mission. The choice follows from the NASA report on supercritical airfoils \cite{Harris1990}, as well as $C_{l_\text{des}}$ of 0.074

%The ARW-2 wing of the DAST drone used supercritical airfoils with varying thicknesses along the wing span. The profile thickness varied between $\frac{t}{c} = 0.14$ at the wing root to $\frac{t}{c} = 0.10$ at the wing tip. \cite{ARW-2_NASA} Based on the initial design lift coefficient $C_{L_\text{des}} = 0.074$, the selected airfoil is based on a NASA report on supercritical airfoils. \cite{Harris1990}

A symmetrical supercritical airfoil is beneficial for low lift coefficient flight conditions.
%Only airfoils designed for $C_{l_{des}} = 0.4$ were tested, which is already significantly above the value suitable for HUGO. 
A symmetrical supercritical airfoil provides the desired lift coefficient at low angles of attack. %Furthermore, significant negative pitching moment is generated by non-symmetrical supercritical airfoils, like the NASA SC(2)-0412 \footnote{\href{http://airfoiltools.com/polar/details?polar=xf-sc20412-il-1000000-n5}{NASA SC(2)-0412} (accessed on 11.5.2026)}, which could result in a larger and, consequently, heavier empennage. 
Moreover, it does not create significant negative pitching moments that necessitate the use of a larger empennage, as opposed to their non-symmetrical counterparts\footnote{\href{http://airfoiltools.com/polar/details?polar=xf-sc20412-il-1000000-n5}{NASA SC(2)-0412} (accessed on 11.5.2026)}. A high thickness-to-chord ratio $t/c$ is expected to alleviate bending loads on the slender wing of HUGO. Although high $t/c$ ratio airfoils are at a relatively high risk of flow separation and weak shock waves forming further from the trailing edge, the reported Mach divergence numbers $M_{dd}$ at which those occur are above the operational regime of HUGO \cite{Harris1990}.
%The increased area moment of inertia of a thicker wing generally helps bending resistance. There are several risks associated with these aifoils with a relative high $\frac{t}{c}$ ratio. Compared to airfoils with higher $C_{L_\text{des}}$, flow separation and weak shock waves appear earlier, further from the trailing edge. However, at the same time, it has been reported that the overall drag divergence Mach number for airfoils with lower $C_{L_\text{des}}$ is generally higher. For the selected airfoil, the drag divergence Mach number $M_{dd}$ has been found to be around $M_{dd} = 0.8$, which is beyond the conditions HUGO will operate. \cite{Harris1990}

\subsubsection*{Cruise Reynolds number}
Based on the initial estimates from \autoref{tab:DAST_HUGO}, as well the cruise conditions $\rho_{cr}$, $V_{cr}$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu_{cr}$ corresponding to Torenbeek's ISA (International Standard Atmosphere) model \cite{AdvancedACDesign_Torenbeek}, a cruise Reynold's number $Re_{cr}$ can be calculated using \autoref{eq:Re} \cite{torenbeek2013synthesis}.
\begin{equation}
    Re_{cr} = \frac{\rho_{cr} \overline{c}V_{cr}}{\mu_{cr}} \approx 1.15\cdot10^6
    \label{eq:Re}
\end{equation}
%Using the cruise conditions $\rho_{cr}$, $V_{cr}$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu_{cr}$ corresponding to Torenbeek's ISA model \cite{AdvancedACDesign_Torenbeek}, a cruise Reynold's number $Re_{cr}$ of $1.15\cdot10^6$ is obtained for test conditions at FL270. The MAC $\overline{c} \approx 0.15 \ m$ is calculated from the wing parameters in \autoref{tab:DAST_HUGO}.

\subsubsection*{Maximum Lift Coefficient}
The maximum lift coefficient, $C_{L_\text{max}}$ has to be determined to obtain the stall speed of HUGO, $V_\text{stall}$. As stated previously, the use of a supercritical wing profile is highly recommended for the considered test conditions. A preliminary estimate of  $C_{L_\text{max}}$ is made by considering a NASA SC(2)-0010 airfoil and the DATCOM method for estimating the maximum lift coefficient for finite wings. 

An estimation of $C_{L_\text{max}}$ can be made using the software Xfoil \footnote{\href{http://airfoiltools.com/polar/details?polar=xf-sc20412-il-500000-n5}{AirfoilTools} (accessed on 07.05.2026)}, and an expression for estimating the wing $C_{L_\text{max}}$ from the $C_{l_\text{max}}$ of the wing profile. For a $Re \approx 1.15\cdot10^6$ and a critical amplification factor $N$ of 5, a $C_{l_\text{max}} \approx 1.4$ was found \footnote{\href{http://www.airfoiltools.com/polar/details?polar=xf-sc20010-il-1000000-n5}{NASA SC(2)-0010 airfoil} (accessed on 11.5.2026)}. The $N$ value is deemed suitable for this use case due to likely disturbed airflow from the wing surface roughness \cite{Koul}. Then, \autoref{eq:CL_max} is used to obtain $C_{L_{max}}$ \cite[p. 153]{Torenbeek1982}.

\subsubsection*{Stall speed}
The next step is to identify the preliminary stall speed of HUGO. With a maximum lift coefficient $C_{L_{max}} \approx 1.22$ and $S = 0.49 \ m^2$ at a mass $m = 50 \ kg$ and sea level ISA conditions. The stall speed $V_\text{stall}$ can be calculated using \autoref{eq:Vstall}.
\begin{equation}
    V_\text{stall} = \sqrt{\frac{2W}{\rho S C_{L_\text{max}}}}\approx 37\hspace{0.1cm}m\cdot s^{-1}
    \label{eq:Vstall}
\end{equation}
Compared to the stall speed of the X-56A UAS of 33 m/s\footref{url:x56a}, the result appears reasonable for an initial estimation.\\

In order to perform preliminary sizing, aerodynamic analyses of the aircraft were performed by means of the lifting line theory (LLT) with modifications for nonzero sweep provided by the AeroSandbox Python package \footnote{\href{https://aerosandbox.readthedocs.io/en/master/autoapi/aerosandbox/aerodynamics/aero_3D/lifting_line/index.html}{AeroSandbox} (accessed 18.05.2026)}. The goals of these analyses were to obtain: the lift and induced drag of the aircraft required for the later calculation of the $L/D$ ratio, the lift curve slopes of all the lifting surfaces (both individually and combined with other lifting surfaces), the lift distribution along the span, the aerodynamic centre position, and the determination of the angle of attack required for trimming. This section first outlines the generation of the model, followed by the description of the performed analyses and their verification.

To start sizing of the aircraft, including steps such as a matching diagram, it is vital to know the breakdown between induced and parasitic drag \cite{Vos}. Thus, the aerodynamic analysis shall focus on inviscid modelling to obtain the induced drag alone, while the parasitic drag will be obtained via drag build-up. As Aerosandbox LLT includes viscosity, an inviscid modification has been created for the purpose of this project. The LLT of Aerosandbox assembles total aerodynamic force and moments from viscous and inviscid components, so instead of the sum, only the inviscid components are returned. The inviscid analysis has been verified by comparing its output with that of the standard viscous analysis, i. e. by imposing the maximum relative difference of less than 1\% in the lift coefficient.

\subsection{Parametric model}
\label{subsec:parametric_model}
The model is created parametrically, with the planform parameters of lifting surfaces, the chosen set of airfoils, and relative positions and orientations between the lifting surfaces used as parameters. A lifting surface is generated by specifying the properties of an arbitrary number of sections along its span. Each section is, in turn, specified by the position of its leading edge, chord length, the airfoil, and twist. Specifically, the airfoil used in the aforementioned analyses was the same for all sections of all lifting surfaces, namely NASA SC(2)-0012. The measurement data from Mineck et al\cite{Mineck1987} has been used to obtain the lift curve slope of the airfoil, namely $0.125$ deg$^{-1}$. The sections in the HUGO model are spaced uniformly to simplify the extraction of the lift distribution results. Once all lifting surfaces are specified, the aircraft model is generated by combining the surfaces in a single airplane object. This object can include a single lifting surface (the main wing in isolation, for example), as well as all the lifting surfaces combined (main wing, canards, empennage).

\subsection{Parasitic drag estimation}
\label{subsec:parasitic_drag_estimation}
As mentioned previously, the parasite drag ($C_{D_0}$) is estimated using the drag build-up method described by Raymer \cite{raymer1992aircraft}. The subsonic parasite drag of each component is estimated separately, and then summed to obtain the total $C_{D_0}$. The component drag is a product of the wetted area $S_\text{wet}$, calculated flat-plate skin-friction drag coefficient $C_f$, form factor $FF$ that estimates the pressure drag due to viscous separation, and interference component $Q$ that estimates the increase in drag due to the interference between the different components, as shown in \autoref{eq:CD0} \cite[p. 281]{raymer1992aircraft}.
\begin{equation}
    (C_{D_0})_\text{subsonic} = \frac{\sum(C_{f_c} \cdot FF_c \cdot Q_c \cdot S_{wet_c})}{S_\text{ref}} + C_{D_\text{misc}} + C_{D_{L\&P}}
    \label{eq:CD0}
\end{equation}

Here, $C_{D_\text{misc}}$ is miscellaneous drag due to flaps, un-retracted landing gear etc., and $C_{D_{L\&P}}$ is an estimate of the drag due to leakages and protuberances. $S_{wet}$ is the wetted area, and $S_{ref}$ is the reference area. The subscript $c$ refers to a single component.

The $C_f$ is estimated as a weighted average of the laminar and turbulent flow values, and can be calculated according to Raymer  \cite[p. 282]{raymer1992aircraft}. In case the skin roughness is high, the $C_f$ value can be expected to increase. An adjusted value can be calculated by introducing a cut-off Reynold number $Re_\text{cut-off}$ for subsonic flight \cite[p. 282]{raymer1992aircraft}, for a given  characteristic length and skin roughness.

\subsection{LLT Analysis}
\label{subsec:LLT_analysis}

To create the LLT-based aerodynamic model, the airplane model, atmospheric properties, and flight conditions (angle of attack, sideslip angle, airspeed and rotational rates) are required as inputs. Since the results provided by AeroSandbox exclude the lift distribution, the positions of vortex centres and vortex strengths are extracted from the source code. Then, the Kutta–Joukowski theorem ($L=\rho V\Gamma$) is employed to compute the lift per unit span at each section. To find the lift curve slope, the analysis is run for a range of angles of attack, with a minimum of two values, and additional ones being used to rule out inconsistencies. 

The trim analysis requires a specific angle of attack. While the definition of the trim condition entails both vertical force and pitch moment equilibria, this analysis only considers vertical force equilibrium due to the preliminary nature of the design (no elevators, empennage assumed fixed for simplicity). The required angle of attack can be obtained in two ways:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Find the lift force required for a specific flight condition, run the simulation for a range (at least two) angles of attack, and then calculate the angle of attack for obtaining the required lift, while assuming no lift at $\alpha=0\deg$. 
    \item Use Aerosandbox's optimizer with the objective of finding the angle of attack at which the obtained total lift equals the weight.
\end{enumerate}
%Therefore, in order to find the required angle of attack, two approaches have been employed. In the first approach, the angle of attack is calculated by finding the lift force required for a specific flight condition, running the simulation for a range (at least two) angles of attack, and then calculating the angle of attack for obtaining the required lift, while assuming no lift at $\alpha=0\deg$. The second approach uses the Aerosandbox's optimizer with the objective of finding the angle of attack at which the obtained total lift equals the weight. Nevertheless, the second approach is not only more computationally expensive, but also more difficult to verify and validate. Therefore, only the first approach will be employed.
Due to convenience of implementation, verification, and validation, the first approach will be used. The results were verified with unit tests, which can be accessed in the project repository\footnote{\label{url:github_repo}\href{https://github.com/Teo-Cokonaj/DSE-28}{HUGO Project Repository} (accessed on 21.05.2026)}.

\section{Fuel Mass Fraction Estimation}
\label{sec:fuel_mass_fraction_estimation}
To estimate the fuel required for HUGO to perform its mission, one can express the speed and time requirements for different stages of the flight as range requirements. Then, a procedure for sizing the range can be followed, such as the one developed by Vos \cite{Vos}. 

The total range $R_{eq}$ can be expressed as a sum of the test segment range (distance flown at Mach 0.75), the distance flown at lower speeds (Mach 0.4), as well as the range required for go-around manoeuvres (for HUGO, up to four landing attempts can be expected\footnote{established in conversation with M.T.H. (Malcom) Brown}, as shown in \autoref{eq:fuel_mass_fraction_range}. Each of the aforementioned segments comprises the nominal (geometric) range, as well as lost range which accounts for energy loss on climb, turns, and acceleration.

\label{sec:structural_weight_penalty_analysis}


% In order to perform the trade-off analysis, a preliminary structural analysis of HUGO is performed in order to estimate the weight penalty of versatility for each configuration. The components under significant load mainly include the fuselage, main wing, canard, and empennage. 

%\subsection{Fuselage Initial Analysis}
%\label{subsec:fuselage_initial_analysis}
The purpose of this analysis is to investigate the extent to which a configuration's versatility, via the inclusion of a canard port and the ability to shift the main wing forward and aft, impacts the empty mass of the aircraft. To achieve this, it was assumed that the most significant contribution to a change in mass would be the additional fuselage reinforcements required to maintain structural integrity for each configuration. The mass of the canard wing itself is considered part of the tested planform and thus that penalty is neglected. Variable mounting of the main wing would require a range of wing attachments points, multiplying the weight penalty from wing attachment compared to a non-variable wing mount.  

It is assumed for this analysis that the Young's modulus $E$ is constant in all directions, i.e. the material used is isotropic. Quasi-isotropic properties of a carbon fibre reinforced polymer (CFRP) material from the Ansys GrantaEdupack database \footnote{\href{https://www.ansys.com/products/materials/granta-edupack}{Ansys Granta EduPack}(accessed on 21.05.2026)} is used as a reference material. Furthermore, a minimum fuselage skin thickness of $0.3mm$ was determined for the optimiser, based on the range of minimum ply thicknesses from Galos \cite{galos2020thin}.

The loads from the wing, canard, empennage and weight are placed as point loads in the direction transverse to the fuselage direction $x$. The simplified loading diagram is shown in \autoref{fig:fuselage_loading_model}. $L_c$, $L_w$ and $L_t$ represent the lift components of the canard, wing and empennage, respectively. $W$ is the weight, which is simplified as a point load acting through the centre of gravity located at $x_{cg}$. The main lifting loads are first determined from force and moment equilibrium. For the configurations with canard ports, an initial canard lifting fraction $L_c / L_w = 0.20$ was selected to minimise the possible impact of its wake whilst representing a significant load to the airframe \cite{AdvancedACDesign_Torenbeek}.

For this preliminary analysis, the fuselage was simplified and modelled as a hollow cylinder with an outer diameter $d = 0.315 \ m$ and length $l = 3.42 \ m$ based on FLEXOP dimensions \cite{Yu2022}. The model partitions the fuselage into discrete points along the fuselage length $l$ based on an initial resolution value $i=500$. The external loads where placed at the partition correspondent to their respective $x$ values.  The internal shear force at
each station is calculated by cumulatively summing all loads up to that point and the internal moments are obtained via numerical integration:

\begin{equation}
    V_z(x_i) = \sum_{j=0}^{i} F_z(x_j);\ M_y(x_i) = \sum_{j=0}^{i} V_z(x_j)\Delta x
\end{equation}

For each fuselage station, the required skin thickness is found by checking shear stress, bending stress and buckling. The latter is considered to be the cylindrical shell buckling stress \cite{NASA_SP8007_1968}:

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{cr}
    =
    \frac{E t}{\sqrt{3(1-\nu^2)}r}
\end{equation}

This leads to no lifting loads applied at the canard location and thus no additional reinforcements are required at that location. Without a canard, the empennage is wholly responsible for the moment equilibrium around the centre of gravity, and the main wing will have to provide more lift to counter the aircraft weight.

The values obtained for shear, moment, and skin thickness are then plotted. From these charts, it is possible to see the structural reinforcement required. \end{equation}

Next to the cruise speed constraint, the constraint of providing thrust in the Mach 0.75 condition with half of the fuel weight is considered. Due to the replaceable wing requirement, HUGO cannot be sized to require high lift devices. This means all constraints are evaluated for a clean wing. The following design approach for HUGO considers the conventional (i. e. non-canard) configurations featuring a T-tail. This is motivated by the fact that all configurations shall be able to perform operations without a canard, even if the ports for it are included in the design.
%since considering canards would make the distinction between design option configurations less clear. The reason is that the canard is movable for all the considered configurations, so these configurations will also have to be able to fly without a canard. 
The performance of HUGO is limited by two main factor which govern the allowable centre of gravity range: stability and controllability. The constraining relations are expressed by \autoref{eq:Stability} and \autoref{eq:Controllability}, respectively \cite[p. 309]{torenbeek2013synthesis}.

%Since the aircraft has to be able to allow for stable, neutrally stable, and unstable flight configurations, the neutral position of the c.g. is chosen as the design point. However, the aircraft has to be controllable at the same time, with a certain margin. These two conflicting properties of stability and controllability are governed by the equations shown in \autoref{eq:Controllability} and \autoref{eq:Stability}, respectively \cite{torenbeek2013synthesis}. Clearly, they describe the positions of the centre of gravity as a function of the aerodynamic centre position and numerous aerodynamic and geometric parameters. The two curves constitute the so-called scissor plot, where 
\begin{equation}
\bar{x}_{cg,\text{stability}} = \bar{x}_{ac} + \frac{C_{L_{\alpha_h}}}{C_{L_{\alpha_{A-h}}}} \left(1 - \frac{d\varepsilon}{d\alpha}\right) \frac{S_h l_h}{S\bar{c}} \left(\frac{V_h}{V}\right)^2
\label{eq:Stability}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\bar{x}_{cg,\text{controllability}} = \bar{x}_{ac} - \frac{C_{m_{ac}}}{C_{L_{A-h}}} + \frac{C_{L_h}}{C_{L_{A-h}}} \frac{S_h l_h}{S\bar{c}} \left(\frac{V_h}{V}\right)^2
\label{eq:Controllability}
\end{equation}

The aircraft is controllable when ${\bar{x}_{cg}}>\bar{x}_{cg,\text{controllability}} $ and stable when ${\bar{x}_{cg}}<\bar{x}_{cg,\text{stability}}$ \cite{torenbeek2013synthesis}. To determine the allowable $x_{cg}$ range, we enforce $\bar{x}_{cg}=\bar{x}_{cg,\text{stability}}$ and $\bar{x}_{cg}-\bar{x}_{cg,\text{controllability}}=\text{CGE}$, where $CGE$ is the minimum required excursion between the controllability curve and the design point. 


The aerodynamic parameters required for \autoref{eq:Stability} and \autoref{eq:Controllability} are obtained by the aerodynamic analysis, as described in \autoref{sec:aerodynamic_modelling}. It is assumed that $({V_h}/{V})^2=1$ and ${d\varepsilon}/{d\alpha}=0.0$ due to the use of a T-tail \cite{torenbeek2013synthesis}. That leaves the tail volume $S_h l_h$ as the only unknown. The horizontal tail position $l_h$ can be estimated based on the ratio between the horizontal tail arm and surface area of the FLEXOP, effectively reducing the tail volume to a function of only surface area. This calculation is part of a larger iterative design loop.

\subsection{Landing Gear Mechanism Concepts}
\label{sub:landing_gear_mechanisms}

The undercarriage concepts differ in both retraction mechanism and stowage arrangement. In the external side-pod configuration, the landing gear is housed in nacelles mounted outside the fuselage. This arrangement is expected to reduce the intrusion of the undercarriage into the main fuselage structure and thus simplify the internal layout design. However, the addition of side pods increases the wetted area and weight of HUGO, as well as introduces interference drag at the pod-fuselage junction.

In the internal fuselage-bay configuration, the landing gear is retracted into the fuselage structure. This provides a cleaner external geometry, but requires dedicated internal volume, local structural reinforcement, landing gear doors, and a more integrated retraction mechanism. The placement of the undercarriage is also constrained by the need to avoid interference with the wing box, especially if a modular wing structure is used. Therefore, the final landing gear arrangement will depend strongly on the selected HUGO configuration and on the available structural integration space.

%Both configurations have clear advantages and disadvantages. The side pods are great for slow-moving aircraft, such as propeller or cargo aircraft \cite{raymer1992aircraft}, as they produce a lot of parasitic drag due to the extra area. They are used when a design has to maximise the internal volume \cite{raymer1992aircraft}, as they present a complex mechanism that requires a lot of moving parts. The major penalty for this undercarriage is the added drag due to the extra skin present and weight.

%On the other hand, the fuselage integrated landing gear, it is widely used for fast aircraft, as HUGO, because it keeps the outer skin undisturbed, retaining a low parasitic drag coefficient. The system is easier to design, as it only needs 3 struts for the retraction mechanism. The downside of this model is the occupied internal volume as opposed to the previous design. If the fuselage has the appropriate dimensions that permit this undercarriage design to fold, this option will be more desirable. In the case of a weight penalty of this model due to the need of the rod to resist both bending and compression loads, it can be compensated by the simplicity of the model and the low weight of the plane.

\subsection{Design Constraints}
The major design constraints related to operating HUGO on the runway, as well as pivoting during take-off and landing include 
%Aircraft landing gear serve to taxi on the runway, but also to allow for an aircraft to pivot during take off and landing. As such, several constraints are placed on the design of the landing gear, the most important of which are 
the tip back angle, beta angle (the angle between the landing gear to the C.G. and from the landing gear vertically to the centre line), the turn over angle, and the wing-tip-to-landing-gear angle. The following restrictions must be obeyed by the landing gear \cite{roskamLandingGearSystems,Vos}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The scraping angle must be equal to, or larger than $15 \deg$ (see: \autoref{fig:lg_angles})
    \item The angle between the vertical line and the line through the main gear and the centre of gravity must not be less than the scraping angle (see: \autoref{fig:lg_angles});
    \item The turn over angle must not exceed than 55 deg;
    \item The wing-tip-to-landing-gear-wheel angle must be greater than 7 deg;
    \item The front landing gear must bear between 8\% and 15\% of the total aircraft weight.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Design Optimisation}
By using trigonometric relations between the landing gear and critical points, such as the location of the centre of gravity, the position of the tail cone and the wing tips as described in \cite{roskamLandingGearSystems}, the location of the main and nose landing gear, landing gear length, and main landing gear separation can be optimised for the given fuselage geometric parameters. Using Sequential Least Squares Quadratic Programming, the strut length, x-position, and the lateral track of the main landing gear, as well as the x-position of the nose landing gear, can be minimised. 

For the optimising tool to converge to acceptable values, further constraints were imposed to limit the design space. The chosen airfoil has a stall angle of attack of 15\degree\footref{url:nasa_airfoil}. This value was used as a constraint for the tip-back angle, as the purpose of the latter is to prevent the aircraft from tipping back when it contacts the ground during landing at the maximum landing angle of attack, or during take off. 
%Because the stall angle of attack is 15\degree for all relevant regimes, the optimizer restricts the tip back angle to be equal to 15\degree. 
This allows for the landing gear to be placed further back along the fuselage with increasing beta angle. 

By allowing the landing gear to be housed in the tail cone, the lateral separation of the main landing gear remains close the diameter of the fuselage. This prevents unfeasible landing gear configurations, which would require complex mechanisms, more housing space, and add unnecessary weight. Based on the input parameters of fuselage length and diameter, up-sweep of the tail cone, and location of the C.G., the calculated values using the optimiser will help determine the kind of housing required for the landing gear of each configuration. The nose gear shall be positioned in the middle of the fuselage at y = 0.

\section{Sensitivity Study}
\label{sec: sensitivity study}
During the preliminary sizing, several assumptions have been made, whose impact needs to be assessed. The uncertainty in fuselage and landing gear dimensions (taken from FLEXOP geometry), the dimensions of landing gear pod with respect to the gear itself, as well as the target CG excursion between minimum controllability and neutral stability points are identified as driving assumptions. This is a basis for a sensitivity study, the details of which are shown in \autoref{tab:sensitivity study}. The results of the sensitivity study have been presented as MTOM plots for every configuration across different parameter values in \autoref{fig:sensitivity-results}.

The design iteration failed to converge for fuselage lengths shorter than the baseline, as well as for the largest fuselage diameters, due to infeasible landing gear. This is attributed to the combination of current landing gear requirements creating a heavily constrained design space for the landing gear. As the tail cone is replaceable, a tricycle configuration is strictly enforced over tail dragger. 

Due to the modular nature of the aircraft, the gear has to retract into fuselage or fuselage-connected pods. At the same time, a track width wider than the fuselage diameter is needed to satisfy the tip clearance and turnover angle constraints. The nose landing gear is also set to retract forward. While beneficial in case of actuator failure, this constraint might be reconsidered due to severely impacting the landing gear design freedom for shorter fuselages. As for the landing gear becoming infeasible for high fuselage diameter, it may be attributed to increasing the height of the CG without increasing the nose to main gear x distance, which worsens the turnover performance.

Regarding parameter values in the feasible design space, increasing the fuselage dimensions, especially the diameter, causes a rapid increase in mass (nearly double for the highest considered fuselage diameter), with the exception of the longer fuselage of HUG-CFG-302, which slightly decreases the total mass. It is thus recommended for the final design to avoid excessive enlarging of the fuselage, especially in diameter.

Changing the CG excursion used to size the tail has a much weaker effect and differs significantly between the design options. HUG-CFG-301 and HUG-CFG-302 have their masses slightly decreased with larger C.G. Excursions, while the three remaining options experience a small increase in MTOM.

Changes to the landing gear bay width induce a small, roughly linear increase in mass for the affected design options, which is expected. Changes in the bay length have a significantly more minor effect. This can be explained by the fact that while a longer bay has a proportionally longer wetted area, its slenderness ratio is also proportionally larger. Changes in the wheel diameter of the landing gear affect the podded landing gear significantly more and similarly to increasing the bay width. This is because those options have a shorter main landing gear strut width, so the wheel diameter is more driving to their frontal and reference areas.

Overall, the results of the sensitivity study are plausible and highlight the importance of fuselage optimisation, as well as point to the challenging design space of the landing gear.

Structural Properties & 
\textbf{Weight: 1.0} \newline
It is in our best interest to minimize the weight of the structural componenets of the aircraft. This will allow us to maximize the performance, as well as account for the 5 kg payload mass.  Additionally,  For primary aerodynamic surfaces, high specific modulus ($E/\rho$) and plate buckling efficiency ($\sqrt[3]{E}/\rho$) are required to withstand aeroelastic effects at Mach 0.75. Additionally, concentrated load structures (landing gear, pylons) are driven by high specific yield strength ($\sigma_y/\rho$) and fracture toughness ($K_{Ic}/\rho$) to safely absorb dynamic landing loads. Because structural performance directly dictates the aerodynamic and operational feasibility of the platform, it warrants the highest trade-off weight, 1.0.
\\

Durability & 
\textbf{Weight: 0.85} \newline
HUGO is designed for a 15-year operational lifecycle with several flight days per year. The selected materials must withstand continuous high-impact cyclic loading, runway debris abrasion, and environmental degradation—including UV exposure for composites and galvanic corrosion at hybrid metallic-composite interfaces. While these issues can be solved using protective coatings and modern construction methods like using patches of fibreglass between the aluminium and carbon fibre components, the durability of the materials used is a large factor in the material selection process, warranting a weight of 0.85. 
\\

\textbf{Weight: 0.1} \newline

Manufacturing Cost & 
\textbf{Weight: 0.5} \newline

For localized structures, direct CNC machining of metallic billets offers a highly efficient, low-NRE production route. Manufacturing methodology directly controls the economic viability of unit scaling, However, as shown in the market analysis\footnote{\href{https://github.com/Teo-Cokonaj/DSE-28/blob/main/docs/Market\%20Analysis.docx} (accessed 19.05.2026)}, there is a wide margin in the unit cost in the overall financial viability.

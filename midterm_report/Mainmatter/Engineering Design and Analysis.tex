According to HUG-OPS-005\footref{url:baseline_report}, HUGO is required to operate at speeds up to Mach 0.75. This is the flight condition at which tests are expected to be conducted during operations. In this section, an estimation of the lift characteristics of HUGO is performed based on statistics from suitable reference aircraft, along with a preliminary airfoil selection suitable for the considered operations.

Airfoil selection begins with an analysis of reference aircraft and an estimate of the design lift coefficient $C_{l_\text{des}}$. Subsequently, the maximum lift coefficient $C_{l_{\text{max}}}$ is derived along with relevant aerospace parameters to facilitate the selection. For the purpose of this analysis, it is assumed that HUGO will operate at a speed of Mach 0.75, at an altitude of FL270 (8230 m).

\subsection{Design Aerodynamic Parameters}
\label{subsec:cl_des}
The initial geometric parameters from \autoref{tab:DAST_HUGO} can be used to derive preliminary aerodynamic characteristics of the UAV.

\subsubsection*{Design Lift Coefficient}
To estimate $C_{L_\text{des}}$, \autoref{eq:C_L} can be applied for a cruise air density $\rho_{cr}$ of $0.5112 \ kg \cdot m^{-3}$ at FL270, and a cruise speed $V_{cr}$ of $230.2945 \ m \cdot s^{-1}$ corresponding to the test condition. Using the initial value for $W/S$ from \autoref{tab:DAST_HUGO}, an initial $C_{L_\text{des}}$ of 0.074 is obtained.
%It includes the air density at the altitude of 27,000 feet $\rho_{cr}$ and the airspeed $V_{cr}$, for which it holds that $V_{cr} = M \cdot \sqrt{\gamma R T}$ at the ISA altitude of 27,000 ft.

\begin{equation}
    C_{L_\text{des}} = \frac{\left(\frac{W}{S}\right)}{\frac{1}{2} \rho_{cr} V_{cr}^2} \approx 0.074
    \label{eq:C_L}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Cruise Reynolds number}
Based on the initial estimates from \autoref{tab:DAST_HUGO}, as well the cruise conditions $\rho_{cr}$, $V_{cr}$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu_{cr}$ corresponding to Torenbeek's ISA model \cite{AdvancedACDesign_Torenbeek}, a cruise Reynold's number $Re_{cr}$ can be calculated using \autoref{eq:Re}.
\begin{equation}
    Re_{cr} = \frac{\rho_{cr} \overline{c}V_{cr}}{\mu_{cr}} \approx 1.15\cdot10^6
    \label{eq:Re}
\end{equation}
%Using the cruise conditions $\rho_{cr}$, $V_{cr}$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu_{cr}$ corresponding to Torenbeek's ISA model \cite{AdvancedACDesign_Torenbeek}, a cruise Reynold's number $Re_{cr}$ of $1.15\cdot10^6$ is obtained for test conditions at FL270. The MAC $\overline{c} \approx 0.15 \ m$ is calculated from the wing parameters in \autoref{tab:DAST_HUGO}.

\subsubsection*{Maximum Lift Coefficient}
The maximum lift coefficient, $C_{L_\text{max}}$ has to be determined to obtain the stall speed of HUGO, $V_\text{stall}$. As stated previously, the use of a supercritical wing profile is highly recommended for the considered test conditions. A preliminary estimate of  $C_{L_\text{max}}$ is made by considering a NASA SC(2)-0010 airfoil and the DATCOM method for estimating the maximum lift coefficient for finite wings. 

An estimation of $C_{L_\text{max}}$ can be made using Xfoil software\footnote{\href{http://airfoiltools.com/polar/details?polar=xf-sc20412-il-500000-n5}{AirfoilTools} (accessed on 07.05.2026)}, and an expression for estimating the wing $C_{L_{max}}$ from the $C_{l_{max}}$ of the wing profile. For a $Re \approx 1.15\cdot10^6$ and a critical amplification factor $N$ of 5, a $C_{l_\text{max}} \approx 1.4$ was found \footnote{\href{http://www.airfoiltools.com/polar/details?polar=xf-sc20010-il-1000000-n5}{NASA SC(2)-0010 airfoil} (accessed on 11.5.2026)}. The $N$ value is deemed suitable for this use case due to likely disturbed airflow from the wing surface roughness \cite{Koul}. Then, \autoref{eq:CL_max} is used to obtain $C_{L_{max}}$ \cite[p. ]{Torenbeek1982}. \autoref{eq:Lambda_conversion} is used to convert from the leading edge sweep angle $\Lambda_{LE}$ to the quarter-chord sweep angle $\Lambda_{0.25c}$ and derived from the expression in \cite{Torenbeek1982}.

\begin{equation}
    \Lambda_{0.25}
    =
    \tan^{-1}
    \left(
    \tan \Lambda_{\mathrm{LE}}
    -
    \frac{1-\lambda}{AR (1+\lambda)}
    \right)
    \label{eq:Lambda_conversion}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    C_{L_{\max}} = 0.9 \cdot C_{l_{\max}} \cdot \cos\Lambda_{0.25c}
    \label{eq:CL_max}
\end{equation}

The next step is to identify the preliminary stall speed of HUGO. With a maximum lift coefficient $C_{L_{max}} \approx 1.22$ and $S = 0.49 \ m^2$ at a mass $m = 50 \ kg$ and sea level ISA conditions. The stall speed $V_\text{stall}$ can be calculated using \autoref{eq:Vstall}.

\subsection{Supercritical Airfoils}
\label{subsec:supercritical_airfoil}
Since the aircraft is expected to operate at Mach 0.75, it is necessary to take transonic phenomena into account. When flying in this region with a traditional airfoil, the airflow might locally be supersonic, which creates strong shock waves on the wing \cite{Whitcomb1974}. This leads to a large increase in drag, which is detrimental for the performance of the aircraft and introduces boundary layer separation. Such effects can be mitigated by the use of a supercritical airfoil. 

A supercritical airfoil is capable to operate at transonic conditions with only the onset of weak shock waves \cite{Harris1990}. An example of such airfoils used in the preliminary design for HUGO is a supercritical SC(2)-XXYY family developed by NASA, where XX is the airfoil design lift coefficient, and YY is the maximum thickness in percent \cite{Harris1990}. The advantage of using this system is the inclusion of the design lift coefficient, which makes it possible to narrow down the choices for HUGO using $C_{L_\text{des}}$ values found in \autoref{subsec:cl_des}.

In order to perform preliminary sizing, aerodynamic analyses of the aircraft were performed by means of the lifting line theory (LLT) with modifications for nonzero sweep provided by the AeroSandbox Python package \footnote{\href{https://aerosandbox.readthedocs.io/en/master/autoapi/aerosandbox/aerodynamics/aero_3D/lifting_line/index.html}{AeroSandbox} (accessed 18.05.2026)}. The goals of these analyses were to obtain: the lift and induced drag of the aircraft required for the later calculation of the $L/D$ ratio, the lift curve slopes of all the lifting surfaces (both individually and combined with other lifting surfaces), the lift distribution along the span, the aerodynamic centre position, and the determination of the angle of attack required for trimming. This section first outlines the generation of the model, followed by the description of the performed analyses and their verification.

To conduct sizing of the aircraft, including steps such as a matching diagram, it is vital to know the breakdown of drag between induced and parasitic \cite{Vos}. Thus,the aerodynamic analysis shall focus on inviscid modelling to obtain the induced drag alone, while the parasitic drag will be obtained via drag buildup. As Aerosandbox LLT includes viscosity, an inviscid modification of it has been created for the purpose of this project. The LLT of Aerosandbox assembles total aerodynamic force and moments from viscous and inviscid components, so instead of the sum, only the inviscid components are returned. The inviscid analysis has been verified by comparing its output with that of the standard viscous analysis, i. e. by imposing the maximum relative difference of less than 1\% in the lift coefficient.

\subsection{Parametric model}
\label{subsec:parametric_model}
The model is created parametrically, with the planform parameters of lifting surfaces, the chosen set of airfoils, and relative positions and orientations between the lifting surfaces used as parameters. A lifting surface is generated by specifying the properties of an arbitrary number of sections along its span. Each section is, in turn, specified by the position of its leading edge, chord length, the airfoil, and twist. Specifically, the airfoil used in the aforementioned analyses was the same for all sections of all lifting surfaces, namely NASA SC(2)-0012. The measurement data from Mineck et al\cite{Mineck1987} has been used to obtain the lift curve slope of the airfoil, namely $0.125$ deg$^{-1}$. The sections in the HUGO model are spaced uniformly to simplify the extraction of the lift distribution results. Once all lifting surfaces are specified, the aircraft model is generated by combining the surfaces in a single airplane object. This object can include a single lifting surface (the main wing in isolation, for example), as well as all the lifting surfaces combined (main wing, canards, empennage).

\subsection{Parasitic drag estimation}
\label{subsec:parasitic_drag_estimation}
As mentioned previously, the parasite drag ($C_{D_0}$) is estimated using the drag build-up method described by Raymer \cite{raymer1992aircraft}. The subsonic parasite drag of each component is estimated separately, and then summed to obtain the total $C_{D_0}$. The component drag is a product of the wetted area $S_\text{wet}$, calculated flat-plate skin-friction drag coefficient $C_f$, form factor $FF$ that estimates the pressure drag due to viscous separation, and interference component $Q$ that estimates the increase in drag due to the interference between the different components, as shown in \autoref{eq:CD0} \cite[p. 281]{raymer1992aircraft}.
\begin{equation}
    (C_{D_0})_\text{subsonic} = \frac{\sum(C_{f_c} \cdot FF_c \cdot Q_c \cdot S_{wet_c})}{S_\text{ref}} + C_{D_\text{misc}} + C_{D_{L\&P}}
    \label{eq:CD0}
\end{equation}

Here, $C_{D_\text{misc}}$ is miscellaneous drag due to flaps, unretracted landing gear etc., and $C_{D_{L\&P}}$ is an estimate of the drag due to leakages and protuberances. $S_{wet}$ is the wetted area, and $S_{ref}$ is the reference area. The subscript $c$ refers to a single component.

The $C_f$ is estimated as a weighted average of the laminar and turbulent flow values. The value of $C_f$ for laminar flow is calculated with \autoref{eq:laminar_Cf} \cite[p. 282]{raymer1992aircraft}, and for turbulent flow with \autoref{eq:turbulent_Cf} \cite[p. 282]{raymer1992aircraft}.

\begin{equation}
    C_f = \frac{1.328}{\sqrt{Re}}
    \label{eq:laminar_Cf}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    C_f = \frac{0.455}{\sqrt{\log_{10}(Re)^{2.58} \cdot (1+0.144 M^2)^{0.65}}}
    \label{eq:turbulent_Cf}
\end{equation}

%An estimate of the fraction of laminar and turbulent flow over the component is taken from Raymer \cite{raymer1992aircraft}.
In case the skin roughness is high, the $C_f$ value can be expected to increase further. An adjusted value can be calculated by introducing a cut-off Reynold number $Re_\text{cut-off}$ determined by \autoref{eq:cutoff_Re} for subsonic flight \cite[p. 282]{raymer1992aircraft}, Where $l$ is the characteristic length and $k$ is the skin-roughness value.

\begin{equation}
    Re_\text{cut-off} = 38.21 \cdot \left(\frac{l}{k}\right) ^{1.053}
    \label{eq:cutoff_Re}
\end{equation}

\subsection{LLT Analysis}
\label{subsec:LLT_analysis}

To create the LLT-based aerodynamic model, the airplane model, atmospheric properties, and flight conditions (angle of attack, sideslip angle, airspeed and rotational rates) are required as inputs. Since the results provided by AeroSandbox exclude the lift distribution, the position of vortex centres and vortex strengths are extracted from the source code. Then, the Kutta–Joukowski theorem ($L=\rho V\Gamma$) is employed to compute the lift per unit span at each section. To find the lift curve slope, the analysis is run for a range of angles of attack, with a minimum of two values, and additional ones being used to rule out inconsistencies. 

The trim analysis requires a specific angle of attack. While the definition of the trim condition entails both vertical force and pitch moment equilibria, this analysis only considers vertical force equilibrium due to the preliminary nature of the design (no elevators, empennage assumed fixed for simplicity). The required angle of attack can be obtained in two ways:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Find the lift force required for a specific flight condition, run the simulation for a range (at least two) angles of attack, and then calculate the angle of attack for obtaining the required lift, while assuming no lift at $\alpha=0\deg$. 
    \item Use Aerosandbox's optimizer with the objective of finding the angle of attack at which the obtained total lift equals the weight.
\end{enumerate}

The following design approach considers the conventional airplane configuration (a T-tail), since considering canards would make the distinction between design option configurations less clear. The reason is that the canard is movable for all the considered configurations, so these configurations will also have to be able to fly without a canard. Since the aircraft has to be able to allow for stable, neutrally stable, and unstable flight configurations, the neutral position of the c.g. is chosen as the design point. However, the aircraft has to be controllable at the same time, with a certain margin. These two conflicting properties of stability and controllability are governed by the equations shown in \autoref{eq:Controllability} and \autoref{eq:Stability}, respectively \cite{torenbeek2013synthesis}. Clearly, they describe the positions of the centre of gravity as a function of the aerodynamic centre position and numerous aerodynamic and geometric parameters. The two curves constitute the so-called scissor plot, where the aircraft is controllable when ${\bar{x}_{cg}}>\bar{x}_{cg,\text{controllability}} $ and stable when ${\bar{x}_{cg}}<\bar{x}_{cg,\text{stability}}$. Hence, in order to constrain the design space, we enforce $\bar{x}_{cg}=\bar{x}_{cg,\text{stability}}$ and $\bar{x}_{cg}-\bar{x}_{cg,\text{controllability}}=\text{C.G.E.}$, where  C.G.E. is the minimum required excursion between the controllability curve and the design point. 

\begin{equation}
{\bar{x}_{cg,\text{controllability}} = \bar{x}_{ac} - \frac{C_{m_{ac}}}{C_{L_{A-h}}} + \frac{C_{L_h}}{C_{L_{A-h}}} \frac{S_h l_h}{S\bar{c}} \left(\frac{V_h}{V}\right)^2
\label{eq:Controllability}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
{\bar{x}_{cg,\text{stability}} = \bar{x}_{ac} + \frac{C_{L_{\alpha_h}}}{C_{L_{\alpha_{A-h}}}} \left(1 - \frac{d\varepsilon}{d\alpha}\right) \frac{S_h l_h}{S\bar{c}} \left(\frac{V_h}{V}\right)^2
\label{eq:Stability}
\end{equation}

The aerodynamic parameters from the two equations are obtained by the aerodynamic analysis (\autoref{sec:aerodynamic_modelling}), and it is assumed that $(\frac{V_h}{V})^2=1$ and $\frac{d\varepsilon}{d\alpha}=0.0$ due to the use of the T-tail. That leaves the tail volume $S_h l_h$ as the only unknown. The horizontal tail position is then estimated based on the ratio between the horizontal tail arm and surface area of the FLEXOP airplane (\autoref{tab:tail_params}), enabling the calculation of the required tail surface area. This calculation is part of a larger iterative design loop.


\section{Fuel Mass Fraction Estimation}
\label{sec:fuel_mass_fraction_estimation}
To estimate the fuel required for the aircraft on its mission, one can express the speed and time requirements for different stages of the flight as range requirements. Then, a sizing for range procedure, such as one used by \cite{Vos2025AirplaneDesignAnalysis} can be employed. The procedure consists of estimating the equivalent range from the nominal range (geometric range), lost range (from energy lost on climb, turns and acceleration), and diversion/loiter range (in case of HUGO, the \todo{ref 4 landing attempts req}). To account the fact that the mission plan consists of two stages of climb, acceleration and cruise (for Mach 0.4 and 0.75), the reference equation is applied to each segment separately:
\begin{equation}
    R_{eq} =  R_{0.4}+R_{0.75}+4R_{go-around}
    %M_{.75}a_{.75}t_{.75}+M_{.4}a_{.4}t_{.4} + \frac{1}{0.7} \left[\left( \frac{L}{D} \right)_{0.75}\left(h_{.75}+\frac{V^2_{.75}}{2g_0}\right)+\left( \frac{L}{D} \right)_{0.4}\left(h_{.4}+\frac{V^2_{.4}}{2g_0}\right)\right]+4[V]
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{cases}
    R_{0.4} = M_{0.4}a_{0.4}t_{0.4}+\frac{1}{0.7}\left( \frac{L}{D} \right)_{0.4}\left(h_{0.4}+\frac{a^2_{0.4}M^2_{0.4}}{2g_0}\right)\\
    R_{0.75}=M_{0.75}a_{0.75}t_{0.75}+\frac{1}{0.7}\left( \frac{L}{D} \right)_{0.75}\left(h_{0.75}-h_{0.4}+\frac{a^2_{0.75}M^2_{0.75}-a^2_{0.4}M^2_{0.4}}{2g_0}\right)\\

    R_{go-around}=4\left[V_{go-around}T_{turn}+\frac{1}{0.7}\left(\frac{L}{D}\right)_{go-around}\left(h_{go-around}+\frac{V^2_{go-around}-V^2_{approach}}{2g_0}\right)\right]
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

With $M$, $a$, $t$, $h$, $L/D$ being the Mach number, speed of sound, total time, altitude and glide ratio for the given flight phase. $t_{0.4}$, $t_{0.75}$ and $h_{0.75}$ are constrained by the requirements to 25 min, 5 min, and 27,000 ft respectively.
As the maximum allowed altitude of 27,000 ft is below the optimal for jet aircraft flying at Mach 0.4 \todo{add source and cite}, it can be assumed that the flight segment at Mach 0.4 will be conducted at the maximum altitude as well.The multiplier of 4 for the go-around range accounts for the four required landing attempts. The go-around altitude is constrained to 1,500 feet \todo{add source and cite}, while the go around airspeed is not constrained. This allows to use a value for which the maximum specific range is achieved, taking into account flying in a turn:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{cases}V_{go-around}=\sqrt{\frac WS \frac2\rho\frac{n_{turn}}{C_{L_{opt}}}}\\
    W\sqrt{n_{turn}^2-1}=mV_{go-around}\omega_{turn}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Where $m$ is the aircraft mass, $\omega_{turn}$ is the turn rate. Using a rate 1 turn, $\omega_{turn} = \pi/60\ \text{rad/s}$ and $T_{turn} = 120\ \text{s}$. Thus, knowing the lift coefficient, the equations can be solved for $n_{turn}$ and $V_{go-around}$. The optimal glide ratio and lift coefficient are found as:
\begin{equation}
    \left( \frac{L}{D} \right)_{max} = \frac{\sqrt 3}{4}\sqrt{\frac{\pi ARe}{C_{D, 0}}};\ C_{L_{opt}} = \sqrt{{\frac13\pi ARe}{C_{D, 0}}}
\end{equation}

Knowing the ranges for the flight segments, the fuel required can be computed for each segment, using the glide ratio for this segment:
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{m_{final}}{m_{initial}}\right)_i = 1-\exp\frac{R_ig_0}{\eta_i (L/D)_i e_f}
\end{equation}
where $e_f$ is the energy density of the fuel - a value of 42.8 MJ/kg is used \todo{source}, and $\eta$ is the total propulsion system efficiency. The efficiency can be estimated per flight segment from the thrust-specific fuel consumption $c_T$ of the microturbine as:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_i = \frac{P}{\dot{m}_ie_f}=\frac{T_iV_i}{\dot{m}_ie_f}=\frac{a_iM_i}{c_Te_f}
\end{equation}
The mass fractions of the segments can be later combined into the total mass fractions

\section{Landing Gear Configuration}
\label{sec:Landing_gear_config}
\todo[inline]{NOT DONE}
This section presents the selection analysis for the possible landing gear configurations of HUGO. Out of the five configurations, two of them use a buried design, with the wheels retracting vertically, while the other three configurations have a sideways diagonal retracting platform. 

The two undercarriage concepts differ in both retraction mechanism and stowage arrangement. In the external side-pod configuration, the landing gear is housed in nacelles mounted outside the fuselage. This arrangement can reduce the intrusion of the undercarriage into the main fuselage structure and can simplify the internal layout. However, the addition of side pods increases wetted area and introduces interference drag at the pod fuselage junction.

In the internal fuselage-bay configuration, the landing gear is retracted into the fuselage structure. This provides a cleaner external geometry, but requires dedicated internal volume, local structural reinforcement, landing gear doors, and a more integrated retraction mechanism. The placement of the undercarriage is also constrained by the need to avoid interference with the wing box, especially if a modular wing structure is used. Therefore, the final landing gear arrangement will depend strongly on the selected HUGO configuration and on the available structural integration space.

Both configurations have clear advantages and disadvantages. The side pods are great for slow-moving aircraft, such as propeller or cargo aircraft \cite{raymer1992aircraft}, as they produce a lot of parasitic drag due to the extra area and connection to the fuselage. They are used when a design has to maximise the internal volume \cite{raymer1992aircraft}, as they present a complex mechanism that requires a lot of moving parts. The major penalty for this undercarriage is the added drag due to the extra skin present.

On the other hand, the fuselage integrated landing gear, it is widely used for fast aircraft such as HUGO, because it keeps the outer skin undisturbed, retaining a low friction coefficient. The system is easier to design, as it only needs 3 struts for the retraction mechanism. The downside of this model is the increased weight and occupied internal volume as opposed to the previous design. If the fuselage has the appropriate dimensions that permit this undercarriage design to  fold, this option will be more desirable. The weight penalty of  this model due to the need of the rod to resist both bending and compression loads, can be compensated by the simplicity of the model and low weight of the plane.
%Since all five aircraft configurations use the same general landing gear concept, namely a retractable undercarriage, it does not directly affect the main trade-off selection.

The HUGO UAV will have swappable wings for aeroservoelastic testing, and thus cannot support landing gear within them. This restricts the landing gear to be housed in the fuselage. 

Aircraft landing gear serve to taxi on the run way, but also to allow for an aircraft to pivot during take off and landing. As such, several constraints are placed on the design of the landing gear, the most important of which are the tip back and beta angle, the turn over angle, and the wing-tip-to-landing-gear angle. The following restrictions must be obeyed by the landing gear \cite{roskamLandingGearSystems,Vos2025AirplaneDesignAnalysis}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The scraping angle must be equal to, or larger than $15 \deg$ (pitch angle req.)\autoref{fig:lg_angles}
    \item The angle between the vertical line and the line through the main gear and the C.G. has to be at least equal to the scraping angle (pitch angle req.) \autoref{fig:lg_angles}
    \item The turn over angle must be less than 55 degrees;
    \item The wing-tip-to-landing-gear-wheel angle must be greater than 7 degrees
    \item The front landing gear must bear between 8\% and 15\% of the total aircraft weight
\end{itemize}

By using trigonometric relations between the landing gear and critical points such as the location of the C.G., the positions of the beginning and end of the tail cone, and the wing tips as described in \cite{roskamLandingGearSystems}, the location of the main and nose landing gear, as well as the landing gear length, and main landing gear separation can be optimised for the given geometric parameters of the fuselage. Using Sequential Least Squares Quadratic Programming (SLSQP), the strut length, x-position, and the lateral track of the main landing gear, as well as the x-position of the nose landing gear, can be minimised. 

For the optimiser to converge to reasonable values, further constraints were imposed to limit the design space. The chosen airfoil has a stall angle of attack of 15\degree. The purpose of the tip-back angle is to prevent the aircraft from tipping back when it contacts the ground during landing at the maximum landing angle of attack, or during take off. Because the stall angle of attack is 15\degree for all relevant regimes, the optimizer restricts the tip back angle to be equal to 15\degree. This allows for a variable beta angle (the angle between the landing gear to the C.G. and from the landing gear vertically to the centre line), placing the landing gear further back as beta increases. By allowing the landing gear to be housed in the tail cone, the lateral separation of the main landing gear remains close the diameter of the fuselage. This prevents unfeasible landing gear configurations, which would require complex mechanisms, more housing space, and add unnecessary weight. Based on the input parameters of fuselage length and diameter, up-sweep of the tail cone, and location of the C.G., the calculated values using the optimiser will help determine the kind of housing required for the landing gear of each configuration.

The nose gear will be positioned in the middle of the fuselage at y = 0.

\subsection{Fuselage Initial Analysis}
\label{subsec:fuselage_initial_analysis}
First, the influence of versatility and the presence of canard port on the fuselage is investigated. The goal is to find the required skin thickness along the fuselage length, compare it between all considered HUGO configurations and report the influence of the features in each configuration, such as canard addition and movable wing port. Quasi-isotropic properties of a carbon fibre reinforced polymer (CFRP) material from the Ansys GrantaEdupack database \cite{ansysgranta2022r1} is used as a reference material.\\

The fuselage is initially modelled as a cylindrical shape with diameter $d = 0.30 \ m$ and length $l = 3 \ m$. The model uses $i=500$ discrete points along the fuselage length $l$. The internal loading is obtained by summing the applied vertical forces along the fuselage axis. The main lifting loads are first determined from force and moment equilibrium. These forces are then applied as point loads at their corresponding fuselage stations. The internal shear force at
each station is calculated by cumulatively summing all loads up to that point:

\begin{equation}
    V_z(x_i) = \sum_{j=0}^{i} F_z(x_j)
\end{equation}

The bending moment is obtained by integrating the shear force along the fuselage. Numerically, this is done by cumulative summation over the fuselage grid spacing:

\begin{equation}
    M_y(x_i) = \sum_{j=0}^{i} V_z(x_j)\Delta x
\end{equation}

This corresponds to the standard beam relations

\begin{equation}
    \frac{dV_z}{dx} = q_z(x)
    \qquad
    \frac{dM_y}{dx} = V_z(x)
\end{equation}


For each fuselage station, the required skin thickness is found by checking shear stress, bending stress and buckling. For a trial thickness $t$, the cross-sectional properties $Q(t)$ and $I(t)$ are calculated. The shear stress is estimated using: \cite{bruhn1973analysis}

\begin{equation}
    \tau = \frac{V_z Q}{I t}
\end{equation}

while the bending stress at the outer radius of the shell is calculated as: \cite{bruhn1973analysis}

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_b = \frac{M_y r}{I}
\end{equation}

The compressive bending stress is also compared with the cylindrical shell buckling stress \cite{NASA_SP8007_1968}:

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{cr}
    =
    \frac{E t}{\sqrt{3(1-\nu^2)}r}
\end{equation}

The code expresses each failure criterion as a ratio $U$ describing the actual structure loading compared to its failure point:

\begin{equation}
    U_\tau = \frac{\tau}{\tau_{\mathrm{allow}}}
    \qquad
    U_\sigma = \frac{\sigma_b}{\sigma_{\mathrm{allow}}}
    \qquad
    U_b = \frac{\sigma_b}{\sigma_{cr}}
\end{equation}

The required skin thickness is then the smallest thickness for which all
$U < 1$. If the minimum thickness already satisfies all criteria, it is kept for the section. Otherwise, a root-finding algorithm determines the thickness at which the governing failure mode first reaches the limit.\\

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fuselage structure:}
    The fuselage is the main structure of the aircraft, which acts as a tethering point for the wings, empennage, and propulsion \cite{CHATTERJEE2019199}. Additionally, it contains the major control system and payload. The main loads acting on a fuselage are compression, tension, and bending, and main design constraints are based on phenomena such as buckling, yielding, and deflection \cite{Arnold2012MaterialsSelection}. For HUGO, the largest loads are expected to occur at a flight speed of Mach 0.75.
    
    \item \textbf{Wing structure:} The wings of an aircraft are subjected to varying types of loads during flight, including bending, tension, compression, twist, vibration, and fatigue \cite{Arnold2012MaterialsSelection}. The main design constraints are related to an analysis of buckling, vibrations, yielding, and deflection. The material for HUGO's wing planform shall therefore be chosen to provide a sufficiently stiff and strong structure (e.g. to withstand the load factors defined in the flight envelope shown in \autoref{fig:V-n_diagram}). Depending on the nature of the test, and especially the required stiffness, the choice of material for this subsystem is expected to vary.

    \item \textbf{Empennage and control surfaces:} The empennage will experience loads of varying degrees during the flight. The structure need to act as a moment-arm for the generated forces at the tailplane. This makes it possible to generate moments around the centre of gravity both in the vertical and horizontal plane. This is not only to ensure straight and steady flight but also to make manoeuvring (change of heading, flight direction and etc.) possible for the aircraft.   

    
    \item \textbf{Landing gear:} 
    The landing gear must be able to carry the weight of the aircraft, as well as withstand take-off and landing loads. According to Albuquerque\cite{structural_loads_handbook}, the key loads acting on the undercarriage can be split into: ground loads (static load, braking, braked roll, ground turns, reversed braking, nose wheel yaw and steering, pivoting, towing etc.), landing loads, and take-off loads (associated with retraction of the landing gear and gyroscopic effects of changing wheel direction). All of these cases are relevant for HUGO, as the in its final configuration, the landing gear retracts into the aircraft during flight (see: \autoref{sec:trade-off_final_design}).  

    \item \textbf{Pylons:} For the design of HUGO, it is assumed that an already existing engine will be chosen, together with a nacelle provided by the manufacturer. However, the design of pylons still has to be considered. The main loads acting on the pylons come from the engines, specifically the associated vibrations and thermal loads \cite{pylon_load_analysis}. Therefore, key considerations for this structural element are related to fatigue life and resistance to high temperatures.    

    \item \textbf{Wing/Empennage Ports:} The modular ports defined for the empennage and/or wing structures of HUGO will have to withstand the loads acting at the connection points between the fuselage and the given planform. Structural analysis shows that both the bending and shear loads are the highest at the root \cite{structural_loads_handbook}. Therefore, the material for the ports shall be chosen such that the resistance to the aforementioned load types is prioritised.
\end{itemize}

It is important to note that at this design stage, the materials are only chosen for the primary components of each structural subsystem. A more detailed design of elements such as antenna mounts, access panel hardpoints, fastener inserts etc., as well as the details of internal supporting structure, will be analysed in more detail once the final external configuration is established for HUGO. 


\subsection{Material Analysis of Reference Aircraft}
\label{sub:materials_reference_aircraft}
The material selection shall begin with an analysis of reference aircraft, X56-A and DASR ARW-2 previously introduced in \autoref{sub:lift_reference_aircraft}, as well as existing trends in the aerospace industry. The purpose of this research is to ensure that HUGO can model the behaviour of contemporary aircraft as accurately as possible.

X56-A, the high aspect ratio testbed developed by NASA in the 2010s, features a carbon fibre composite centrebody with honeycomb bulkheads \cite{X56-AMaterials}. Carbon fibre composites were also used for the wing spars and ribs, as well as the skin for stiff wings. Flexible wing configurations featured glass fibre skins due to their lower stiffness. It also features a stiff landing gear designed for parachute landing loads. While specific materials used for the landing gear do not seem to be publicly available, accessible footage suggests that the structure is metallic.

The DAST ARW-2, a slightly older concept from the 1980s, featured a wing using 7075-T73 aluminium spars and ribs covered with a fibreglass honeycomb sandwich skin material (36 layers at inboard end, 27 at outboard, with 25-percent of the layers at $\pm$45 degree orientation) \cite{Eckstrom1986ARW2}. High stiffness was achieved by incorporating a thick aluminium plate in the centre section of the wing, and strain gauge bridges were mounted on the spars and ribs.

The prevalence for composite usage in external structures is also present in modern commercial aircraft. Leading aircraft manufacturers, such as Boeing and Airbus, both incorporate carbon laminates into the airframes of their products as a lighter alternative to metallic alloys\footnote{\label{url:cfrp_airbus}\href{https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/news/2017-02-aviations-material-evolution}{Airbus - Aviation's material evolution} (accessed on 13.05.2026)}\footnote{\label{url:cfrp_boeing}\href{https://www.boeing.com/content/dam/boeing/boeingdotcom/features/innovation-quarterly/2024/IQ-2024-Q3-full.pdf}{Boeing - 787 Dreamliner Difference} (accessed on 13.05.2026)}. Additionally, Airbus A330neo nacelles are made with 60\% composites, together with a honeycomb structure for acoustics\footnote{\href{https://www.safran-group.com/products-services/airbus-a330neo-nacelles}{Airbus A330neo nacelles} (accessed on 13.05.2026)}, while Boeing 777x uses fine metallic alloys instead\footnote{\href{https://www.safran-group.com/products-services/boeing-777x-exhaust-system}{Boeing 777x exhaust system} (accessed 13.05.2026)}. In the recent years, NLR has also began experimenting with implementing composites into upper spars of pylons    \footnote{\href{https://www.toraytac.com/newsroom/story/HelI/Thermoplastic-Upper-Spar-for-an-Aircraft-Engine-Pylon}{Automated Production of Large and Thick Thermoplastic Composite Aircraft Parts} (accessed on 18.05.2026)}. Aluminium, steel, and titanium alloys are also among the most commonly used materials for typical landing gear systems \cite{typicallandinggear}, as well as engine pylons (specifically Ti and Al-Li alloys)\cite{TiAl_pylon_materials}.

Structural Properties & 
\textbf{Weight: 1.0} \newline
It is in our best interest to minimize the weight of the structural componenets of the aircraft. This will allow us to maximize the performance, as well as account for the 5 kg payload mass.  Additionally,  For primary aerodynamic surfaces, high specific modulus ($E/\rho$) and plate buckling efficiency ($\sqrt[3]{E}/\rho$) are required to withstand aeroelastic effects at Mach 0.75. Additionally, concentrated load structures (landing gear, pylons) are driven by high specific yield strength ($\sigma_y/\rho$) and fracture toughness ($K_{Ic}/\rho$) to safely absorb dynamic landing loads. Because structural performance directly dictates the aerodynamic and operational feasibility of the platform, it warrants the highest trade-off weight, 1.0.
\\

Durability & 
\textbf{Weight: 0.85} \newline
HUGO is designed for a 15-year operational lifecycle with several flight days per year. The selected materials must withstand continuous high-impact cyclic loading, runway debris abrasion, and environmental degradation—including UV exposure for composites and galvanic corrosion at hybrid metallic-composite interfaces. While these issues can be solved using protective coatings and modern construction methods like using patches of fibreglass between the aluminium and carbon fibre components, the durability of the materials used is a large factor in the material selection process, warranting a weight of 0.85. 
\\

\textbf{Weight: 0.1} \newline

\textbf{Weight: 0.5} \newline

Its advantage in specific modulus and plate buckling efficiency ($\sqrt[3]{E}/\rho$) is critical for a high-aspect-ratio (HAR) aircraft operating at Mach 0.75. This is what drove its score in the structural properties section. Furthermore, CFRP offers maximum design freedom, allowing for the maximum freedom to design complex aerodynamic profiles necessary for a flying laboratory. It also allows for the development and construction of new wing designs without astronomical tooling costs. Utilizing CFRP ensures that weight of the fuselage and wings can be minimized, while retaining the maximum payload volume required for the 5 kg experimental payload.

For localized, high-stress components such as the landing gear struts, engine pylons and modular port connection points, Aluminum 7075-T76 outperformed the composite alternatives. These components require a balance of high specific fracture toughness to absorb dynamic landing impacts and high manufacturability. Aluminum 7075-T76 provides excellent specific strength ($\sigma_y/\rho$) while keeping manufacturing and material costs significantly lower than titanium or composite alternatives. The selection of high-strength aluminum heavily supports the project's modularity requirement. Hardpoints, pylons, and gear attachments can be rapidly CNC-machined, tapped, and modified to accommodate swappable test articles without the need for expensive composite tooling or complex inserts.

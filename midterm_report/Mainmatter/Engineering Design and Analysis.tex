\section{Airfoil and wing preliminary design}
According to \textbf{HUG-OPS-005}, HUGO is required to operate at speeds up to Mach 0.75\cite{BaselineReport}. This is the flight condition at which tests are expected to be conducted during operations. In this Section, an estimation of the lift characteristics of HUGO is performed, along with a preliminary airfoil selection suitable for the considered operations.


\subsection{Reference aircraft parameters}
\label{sub:lift_reference_aircraft}
The initial step in selecting an airfoil is to determine the design lift coefficient $C_{L_\text{des}}$ and maximum lift coefficient $C_{L_\text{max}}$. Several steps have to be conducted beforehand to obtain these coefficients. Due to the specified testing conditions, $C_{L_\text{des}}$ was selected for the Mach 0.75 cruise airspeed condition at FL270 (8230 m). For initial estimates, the NASA DAST project \footnote{\label{fn:DAST}\href{https://web.archive.org/web/20230310084826/https://www.nasa.gov/centers/dryden/multimedia/imagegallery/DAST/DAST_proj_desc.html}{NASA - Firebee Drone: DAST} (accessed 07.05.2026)}, NASA X-56A UAS \footnote{\href{https://www.nasa.gov/aeronautics/x-56a-mutt/}{NASA X-56 UAS testbed} (accesed on 8.5.2026)} and the FLEXOP UAS \cite{Yu2022} are used as reference aircraft.\\
DAST was initially developed as a modified version of the Teledyne-Ryan BQM-34E/F Firebee II, with the purpose of remotely testing new aerodynamic designs in the 1980s. The aircraft was convenient for such testing due to easily replaceable wings and use of control surfaces mounted exclusively on the tail. The UAS was retrofitted with a supercritical wing aimed at achieving a flutter speed of Mach 0.95 at FL250, and used a custom control system for flutter suppression. \footref{fn:DAST} To this day, the DAST aircraft is the only unmanned aircraft that approaches the proposed flight condition of HUGO in terms of flight speed and mission. \\
The X-56A is a NASA testbed flying-wing UAS used to test aeroelastic phenomena on high aspect ratio wings in a low-speed flight regime, with a takeoff speed of 65 kts (33 m/s), maximum airspeed of 135 kts (69 m/s) during cruise and 150 kts (77 m/s) in a dive \cite{Schaefer2023}. Although the aircraft did not approach the intended flight cruise speed of HUGO, its mass and size are closer to that intended for HUGO.\\
Similarly, the FLEXOP UAS was used as a modular testbed with interchangeable wings for researching aeroelastic phenomena and design of flutter-suppressing control systems. \cite{Bauer2020} \cite{Yu2022} Analogous to the NASA X-56A aircraft, the cruise speed of $38 m/s$ of FLEXOP is significantly below the cruise speed of HUGO. However, the mission and scale of this aircraft approach HUGO much more closely, making it a reasonable reference for initial sizing. Furthermore, the scalability of the high wing-loading structure of DAST is uncertain and may not be feasible and the number of aircraft performing a similar mission to HUGO is limited. Therefore, the X-56 and FLEXOP aircraft are included in the reference aircraft list.

The design lift coefficient $C_{L_\text{des}}$ is determined based on an initial wing loading $W/S$ estimate and requirements from \cite{BaselineReport} at the sepcified cruise flight condition. Parameters of reference aircraft, including the wing loading itself, landing speed and cruise conditions are used in the estimate.\\
HUGO has to perform high subsonic flight and take-off or land on a paved runway. The initial estimate of $W/S = 1000\  N \cdot m^{-2}$ allows for sufficiently low stall speed while remaining a reasonable design value by lying between the considered reference aircraft. The take-off and landing of the X-56A and FLEXOP were performed from on paved runway. The DAST drone was launched from a carrier aircraft and captured by helicopter after completing a test flight. This allowed for a smaller wing due to the absence of ground takeoff and landing. \footref{fn:DAST} The high wing loading achieved in the DAST UAS sets an upper bound for the HUGO wing design. A significantly smaller wing is needed for HUGO compared to the X-56A or FLEXOP aircraft due to the high-speed cruise condition, yet the wing still must allow for take-off and landing at a reasonable speed. The mass $m$ and aspect ratio $AR$ are determined based on the mass requirement \textbf{HUG-GEN-001} and the aspect ratio requirement \textbf{HUG-AER-002} \cite{BaselineReport}.  The average value $AR=\frac{27+19}{2}=23$ from the range given in \textbf{HUG-AER-002} was used. The wing area $S$ can then be obtained from the wing loading. From these parameters, the wing span $b$ and mean chord length $MAC$ can be derived. The taper ratio $\lambda = 0.5$ for HUGO was selected based on guidlines by Torenbeek \cite{Torenbeek1982} and lies within the upper bound considered for jet transport aircraft in a similar flight regime. A slightly larger taper ratio is introduced due to the high aspect ratio of the wing. Torenbeek suggests that the sweep angle is determined primarily by compressibility effects and should increase progressively above a Mach number of 0.8. \cite{Torenbeek1982} An initial sweep $\Lambda_{LE} = 15 \degree$ was selected, similar to that of the X-56A and FLEXOP aircraft. This is considered a good balance between obtaining reasonable low-speed lift characteristics and delaying drag divergence at the test velocity. Relevant reference aircraft parameters \footnote{\href{https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20120015944/downloads/20120015944.pdf}{NASA X-56A parameters} (accesed on 8.5.2026)} and preliminary estimates for HUGO are shown in \autoref{tab:DAST_HUGO}. Several additional wing parameters were obtained using the AeroToolbox website \footnote{\href{https://aerotoolbox.com/wing-plot-tool/}{AeroToolbox wing plotting tool}(Accessed on 12.5.2026)} and \cite{Torenbeek1982}.

The ARW-2 wing loading is several orders of magnitude larger than that of the X-56A UAS. This is due to the launch and landing conditions of both aircraft.  After matching the required cruise and takeoff/landing conditions, an initial wing loading $W/S = 1000 \ N \cdot m^{-2}$ was selected for HUGO. This value reflects the cruise Mach number of 0.75 as well as the landing and takeoff conditions for HUGO, requiring a larger wing. At the same time, the DAST ARW-2 wing design proves this magnitude of wing loading can be designed and used. The initial wing planform is likely to be revisited at a later design iteration and serves as an initial design point.

\subsection{Design lift coefficient}
\label{subsec:cl_des}
To estimate $C_{L_\text{des}}$, \autoref{eq:C_L} can be applied for a cruise air density $\rho_{cr}$ of $0.5112 \ kg \cdot m^{-3}$ at FL270, and a cruise speed $V_{cr}$ of $230.2945 \ m \cdot s^{-1}$ corresponding to the test condition. Using the initial value for $W/S$ from \autoref{tab:DAST_HUGO}, an initial  $C_{L_\text{des}}$ of 0.074 was obtained.
%It includes the air density at the altitude of 27,000 feet $\rho_{cr}$ and the airspeed $V_{cr}$, for which it holds that $V_{cr} = M \cdot \sqrt{\gamma R T}$ at the ISA altitude of 27,000 ft.

\begin{equation}
    C_{L_\text{des}} = \frac{\left(\frac{W}{S}\right)}{\frac{1}{2} \rho_{cr} V_{cr}^2} \approx 0.074
    \label{eq:C_L}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Cruise Reynolds number}
Based on the initial estimates from \autoref{tab:DAST_HUGO}, the Reynolds number for cruise $Re_{cr}$ and stall condition $Re_\text{stall}$ was calculated using \autoref{eq:Re}.
\begin{equation}
    Re = \frac{\rho \overline{c}V}{\mu} \approx 1.15\cdot10^6
    \label{eq:Re}
\end{equation}
Using the cruise conditions $\rho_{cr}$, $V_{cr}$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu_{cr}$ corresponding to Torenbeek's ISA model \cite{AdvancedACDesign_Torenbeek}, a cruise Reynold's number $Re_{cr}$ of $1.15\cdot10^6$ was obtained for test conditions at FL270. The MAC $\overline{c} \approx 0.15 \ m$ is calculated from the wing parameters in \autoref{tab:DAST_HUGO}.

\subsection{Maximum lift coefficient}
The maximum lift coefficient, $C_{L_\text{max}}$ has to be determined to obtain the stall speed of HUGO, $V_\text{stall}$. As stated previously, the use of a supercritical wing profile is highly recommended for the considered test conditions. A preliminary estimate of  $C_{L_\text{max}}$ is made by considering a NASA SC(2)-0010 airfoil and the DATCOM method for estimating the maximum lift coefficient for finite wings. 

An estimation of $C_{L_\text{max}}$ can be made using Xfoil software\footnote{\href{http://airfoiltools.com/polar/details?polar=xf-sc20412-il-500000-n5}(Accessed on 07.05.2026)} and an expression for estimating the wing $C_{L_{max}}$ from the $C_{l_{max}}$ of the wing profile. For a $Re \approx 1.15\cdot10^6$ and a critical amplification factor $N$ of 5, a $C_{l_\text{max}} \approx 1.4$ was found \footnote{\href{http://www.airfoiltools.com/polar/details?polar=xf-sc20010-il-1000000-n5}{NASA SC(2)-0010 airfoil}(Accessed on 11.5.2026)}. $N$ is deemed suitable for this usecase due to likely disturbed airflow from the wing surface roughness. \cite{Koul} Then, according to Torenbeek \cite{Torenbeek1982}, \autoref{eq:CL_max} is used to obtain $C_{L_{max}}$. \autoref{eq:Lambda_conversion} is used to convert from the leading edge sweep angle $\Lambda_{LE}$ to the quarter-chord sweep angle $\Lambda_{0.25c}$ and derived from the expression in \cite{Torenbeek1982}.

\begin{equation}
    \Lambda_{0.25}
    =
    \tan^{-1}
    \left(
    \tan \Lambda_{\mathrm{LE}}
    -
    \frac{1-\lambda}{AR (1+\lambda)}
    \right)
    \label{eq:Lambda_conversion}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    C_{L_{\max}} = 0.9 \cdot C_{l_{\max}} \cdot \cos\Lambda_{0.25c}
    \label{eq:CL_max}
\end{equation}

This leads to $\Lambda_{0.25c} \approx 14.22$ and $C_{L_{\max}} \approx 1.22$ without high-lift devices.\\ \todo{cite this stuff}

\subsection{Stall speed}
The next step is to identify the preliminary stall speed of HUGO. With a maximum lift coefficient $C_{L_{max}} \approx 1.22$ and $S = 0.49 \ m^2$ at a mass $m = 50 \ kg$ and sea level ISA conditions, the stall speed can be calculated using \autoref{eq:Vstall}.
\begin{equation}
    V_\text{stall} = \sqrt{\frac{2W}{\rho S C_{L_\text{max}}}}
    \label{eq:Vstall}
\end{equation}
From \autoref{eq:Vstall}, a stall speed $V_{stall} \approx 37 \ m \cdot s^{-1}$. Comparing this to the stall speed of the X-56A UAS of 33 m/s, the result appears reasonable for an initial estimation.\\

\subsection{Supercritical airfoil}
Since the aircraft is expected to operate at Mach 0.75, it is necessary to take transonic phenomena into account. When flying in this region with a traditional airfoil, the airflow might locally be supersonic, which creates strong shock waves on the wing \cite{Whitcomb1974}. This leads to a large increase in drag, which is detrimental for the performance of the aircraft and introduces boundary layer separation. Such effects can be mitigated by the use of a supercritical airfoil. 

A supercritical airfoil is capable to operate at transonic conditions with only the onset of weak shock waves \cite{Harris1990}. An example of such airfoils used in the preliminary design for HUGO is a supercritical SC(2)-XXYY family developed by NASA, where XX is the airfoil design lift coefficient, and YY is the maximum thickness in percent \cite{Harris1990}. The advantage of using this system is the inclusion of the design lift coefficient, which makes it possible to narrow down the choices for HUGO using $C_{L_\text{des}}$ values found in \autoref{subsec:cl_des}.

\subsection{Airfoil selection}
Based on the data obtained in the previous subsections, an airfoil from the NASA database was selected. The ARW-2 wing of the DAST drone used supercritical airfoils with varying thicknesses along the wing span. The profile thickness varied between $\frac{t}{c} = 0.14$ at the wing root to $\frac{t}{c} = 0.10$ at the wing tip. \cite{ARW-2_NASA} Based on the initial design lift coefficient $C_{L_\text{des}} = 0.074$, the selected airfoil is based on a NASA report on supercritical airfoils. \cite{Harris1990} It was decided that the the NASA SC(2)-0012 will be used as the initial wing profile. The selected airfoil is symmetrical and designed for supercritical conditions.\\
A symmetrical supercritical airfoil is beneficial for low lift coefficient flight conditions. Only airfoils designed for $C_{l_{des}} = 0.4$ were tested, which is already significantly above the value suitable for HUGO. The symmetrical airfoil generates the desired lift coefficient with a low angle of attack. Furthermore, significant negative pitching moment is generated by non-symmetrical supercritical airfoils, like the NASA SC(2)-0412 \footnote{\href{http://airfoiltools.com/polar/details?polar=xf-sc20412-il-1000000-n5}{NASA SC(2)-0412}(Accessed on 11.5.2026)}, which could result in a larger and, consequently, heavier empannage. The reason for selecting a high thickness-to-chord ratio $t/c$ is the expected bending load on the slender wing of HUGO. The increased area moment of inertia of a thicker wing generally helps bending resistance. There are several risks associated with these aifoils with a relative high $\frac{t}{c}$ ratio. Compared to airfoils with higher $C_{L_\text{des}}$, flow separation and weak shock waves appear earlier, further from the trailing edge. However, at the same time, it has been reported that the overall drag divergence Mach number for airfoils with lower $C_{L_\text{des}}$ is generally higher. For the selected airfoil, the drag divergence Mach number $M_{dd}$ has been found to be around $M_{dd} = 0.8$, which is beyond the conditions HUGO will operate. \cite{Harris1990}

\subsection{Material Analysis of Reference Aircraft}
\label{sub:materials_reference_aircraft}
The material selection shall begin with an analysis of reference aircraft, X56-A and DASR ARW-2 previously introduced in \autoref{sub:lift_reference_aircraft}, as well as existing trends in the aerospace industry. The purpose of this research is to ensure that HUGO can model the behaviour of contemporary aircraft as accurately as possible.

X56-A, the high aspect ratio testbed developed by NASA in the 2010s, features a carbon fibre composite centrebody with honeycomb bulkheads \cite{X56-AMaterials}. Carbon fibre composites were also used for the wing spars and ribs, as well as the skin for stiff wings. Flexible wing configurations featured glass fibre skins due to their lower stiffness. It also features a stiff landing gear designed for parachute landing loads. While specific materials used for the landing gear do not seem to be publicly available, accessible footage suggests that the structure is metallic.

The DAST ARW-2, a slightly older concept from the 1980s, featured a wing using 7075-T73 aluminium spars and ribs covered with a fibreglass honeycomb
sandwich skin material (36 layers at inboard end, 27 at outboard, with 25-percent of the layers at $\pm$45 degree orientation) \cite{Eckstrom1986ARW2}. High stiffness was achieved by incorporating a thick aluminium plate in the centre section of the wing, and strain gauge bridges were mounted on the spars and ribs.

The prevalence for composite usage in external structures is also present in modern commercial aircraft. Leading aircraft manufacturers, such as Boeing and Airbus, both incorporate carbon laminates into the airframes of their products as a lighter alternative to metallic alloys\footnote{\href{https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/news/2017-02-aviations-material-evolution}{Airbus - Aviation's material evolution} (accessed on 13.05.2026)}\footnote{\href{https://www.boeing.com/content/dam/boeing/boeingdotcom/features/innovation-quarterly/2024/IQ-2024-Q3-full.pdf}{Boeing - 787 Dreamliner Difference} (accessed on 13.05.2026)}. Additionally, Airbus A330neo nacelles are made with 60\% composites, together with a honeycomb structure for acoustics\footnote{\href{https://www.safran-group.com/products-services/airbus-a330neo-nacelles}{Airbus A330neo nacelles} (accessed on 13.05.2026)}, while Boeing 777x uses fine metallic alloys instead\footnote{\href{https://www.safran-group.com/products-services/boeing-777x-exhaust-system}{Boeing 777x exhaust system} (accessed 13.05.2026)}. Aluminium, steel, and titanium alloys are also among the most commonly used materials for typical landing gear systems \cite{typicallandinggear}.

\subsection{Aerospace Materials for Airframe and Engines}
\label{sub:materials_candidates}

According to Mouritz\cite{Mouritz2012}, there exist less than one hundred types of metal alloys, composites, polymers and ceramics that exhibit properties suitable for aerospace applications. The main material types used for airframes and engines include: aluminium alloys, titanium alloys, steels, and composites.

%Since most metals used in aerospace occurs as some sort of alloy, the most relevant alloys has been chosen to limit the list of candidates \cite{Mouritz2012}.

Below is the list of the different materials that will be considered.   
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aluminium:} The most widely used material for aerospace in the last fifty years. It is characterised by its ease of manufacturing, high specific strength and stiffness. The alloy used for the analysis is Al (7075 Al-T76) \cite{LI2023944}. 
    \item \textbf{Wood:} Wood has played a part in aerospace engineering since the first powered flight. The softwood Sitka spruce was chosen based on its historical use in the construction of aircraft stemming from high specific strength and stiffness \cite{bookJohn}. The material is additionally characterised by its low density and low cost. 
    \item \textbf{Magnesium} These alloys were used in the aerospace industry for a wide range of applications. It has the lowest density of all metals commonly used in aircraft. The alloy Mg (WE46-T6) will be used in the trade-off.
    \item \textbf{Titanium} It has a high strength at medium density. The $\beta$-Ti alloy (Ti-6Al-4 V) will be considered.  \cite{Pollock2016}. 
    \item \textbf{Steel} This has high strength with high density. The alloy used for the comparisons are Steel (18Ni (350))
    \item \textbf{Composites} This consists typically of two materials the matrix and the fibres. In the case of this the chosen composite the matrix is thermoset epoxy and the fibres are carbon or glass.    
\end{itemize}

Structural properties &  When evaluating different materials  the specific modulus, specific strength and specific fracture toughness are  most relevant since weight reduction is crucial in aircraft design. These properties are listed in \autoref{tab:material-properties} In order to systematically rank these choices based on the properties, the following method was used. First, each property was normalised, with 1.0 being the maximum value, and the rest scaled accordingly. Then, the properties were assigned a weight based on their relative importance. The specific strength was assigned a weight of 1.0, since it determines the overall weight of the structure and is most driving. Specific modulus was assigned a weight of 0.75, since a stiffer structure minimizing structural vibrations and deflection, allowing for collection of clean, low-noise data. Fracture toughness was assigned a weight of 0.25, as this does not drive the design, but ensures minor impacts during landing and take off due to debris, and handling impacts do not cause structural failure. From this analysis, HUGO-MAT-06 (Carbon / Epoxy Composite) came out as the optimal material. A more detailed set of these results are presented in \autoref{tab:Material_Tradeoff_Structural_Properties}.

Durability  & The durability of a specific material will be highly dependent on the use-case and operation-conditions of the aircraft. For HUGO-MAT-01 (spruce) humidity is a know major issues. For untreated wood exposure leads to decomposing which greatly diminishes the structural properties of the material and can lead to warping. A coating would have to be applied to protect the wood. For metals oxidation and corrosion is the largest issue. HUGO-MAT-02 (Ti) posses excellent corrosion and oxidation resistance. HUGO-MAT-02 also posses a good creep resistance \cite{Mridha2016}. For HUGO-MAT-03 (Al) its reaction with oxygen leads to a protective layer ($Al_2O_3$) which prevents further oxidation. The HUGO-MAT-03 would perform well in standard atmospheric conditions (primary area of operation for the aircraft). A issue for this is galvanic corrosion when it is contact with a more noble material \cite{Zhu2023}. HUGO-MAT-04 (Mg) also forms a thin protective film when in contact with oxygen and also suffers from galvanic corrosion when in contact to more noble metals. The material also posses good creep-resistance \cite{Ouyang2020}. HUGO-MAT-05 (steel) when oxidized will also form a thin layer this is often referred to as rust. It posses a good resistance to corrosion, oxidation and creep (insert citation here). Both HUGO-MAT-06 (Carbon/epoxy comp.) and HUGO-MAT-07 (Glass/epoxy comp.) are also susceptible to oxidation and corrosion but have a good resistance to both. Though contrary to metals UV-radiation over a longer period also leads to a reduction in structural performance in the epoxy. Water absorption also pose a problem for the long term usage \cite{Dong2024}. Fatigue also poses an issue for the composites. As damages from cyclically loads gradually builds up during operation the part might experience a apparently sudden catastrophic failure \cite{Mandell1981}.

Manufacturing cost & The manufacturing cost is directly related to manufacturing complexity. Different designs can require different manufacturing processes. These processes can have different Non-Recurring Engineering costs (NRE). Due to the low volume nature of our project (<10 per production batch), the aim of this project should be to minimize these costs. A design comprising primarily of wood, as is the case with HUGO-MAT-01, the construction would likely comprise of machined spars, ribs and stiffeners, with a steam formed or bent laminate skin, bonded to the spars using adhesives. These techniques could allow for complex curves, with relatively low NRE costs for tooling and moulds. In the case of any of the metals chosen, (HUGO-MAT-02, HUGO-MAT-03, HUGO-MAT-04, HUGO-MAT-05), the construction would likely comprise of a thin, sheet metal skin, with stiffeners, ribs and spars fastened to the skin using fasteners, welded or bonded with an adhesive. If the skin has a simple bend, it can easily be laser cut and rolled to the correct radius. However, forming the sheet metal into complex, aerodynamic shapes would require a hydraulic sheet metal press with custom forms, or a stretch forming process, also requiring custom stretch blocks, both of which have high NRE costs. Incremental sheet forming and digital sheet forming techniques are beginning to gain traction, which would allow for complex shapes with no NRE costs, however this still has a low TRL and is still a niche technology. The ribs, spars and stiffeners would also have to be custom made, which would be expensive at the production volume of HUGO. Additionally, the wings, empennage and canards may have to be produced specifically for an experiment. For these cases, lead times and the cost of custom tooling for one-off parts would be prohibitively expensive. Composites require relatively inexpensive moulds which could be made of MDF or similar materials, and custom cut prepreg sheets for optimal fiber layup. This has a relatively low NRE cost. However, the layup process is laborious, time consuming and requires relatively high skill. After the layup, which could possibly involve an internal honecomb layer between the outer layers of fibre, the laminate needs to be bagged and cured in an autoclave for optimal curing, while avoiding voids and dry areas in the laminate. Autoclaves are often high demand equipment, which adds cost to the manufacturing process.

For instance, restricting the skin to only simple curves would restrict the aerodynamic design of the fuselage.

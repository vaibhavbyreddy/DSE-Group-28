\section{Operations}
\label{sec:operations}
This Section contains a detailed description of HUGO's intended operations, an overview of the system users, testing location and conditions, mission phases, and approximate timeline. A brief discussion is dedicated to operational constraints and maintenance procedures to ensure HUGO's successful, and continuous operation.

\subsection{High-level Mission Description}
HUGO's main objective aeroservoelastic phenomena in high aspect ratio wings. The mission is considered successful if a flight speed of Mach 0.75 is achieved for 5 consecutive minutes of straight flight, all control systems required by the user were activated/actuated without error, and the collected data is successfully recorded, downloaded, and delivered to the user.

Based on the identified beachhead marker in the baseline report,  a suitable airport was found in the north-east of The Netherlands for HUGO's operations, Den Helder\footnote{\href{https://denhelderairport.nl/eng/}{Den Helder Airport} (accessed on 22.05.2026)} . Several characteristics make Den Helder Airport the best choice as HUGO's base of operations. It is located 5km away from the North sea, reducing the transit time from take off to the test area over populated areas, and has controlled airspace as required by EASA \cite{EASAInitialAirworthiness2025}.  Den Helder is a joint civilian and military airport, consequently, it is not subject to the same congestion as regular airports, and permits special operations. It also has a 1.27 km runway with on call emergency services, which are beneficial for a test aircraft with potentially high take-off speeds.

At Den Helder Airfield, HUGO will take off from runway 21 and depart via FOXTROT according to ATC instructions \footnote{\href{chttps://opennav.com/pdf/EHKD/EH-AD-2.EHKD-VAC.pdf}{Den Helder Departure Map} (accessed on 11.05.2026)}. It will follow the departure regulations until it reaches a distance of approximately 6 km from the airfield over the North Sea. Once it is clear of ground, it will start climbing to test altitude and route towards the testing area at ATC discretion. The test will take place over the North Sea, and HUGO will follow the preplanned path.

Once the take-off area is free, HUGO can proceed to the test site shown in \autoref{fig:flight_area}. The site was chosen to be close to Den Helder airport to comply with the fuel and endurance requirements. The maximum distance in a straight line in the highlighted area is estimated to span 110 km, which allows for the 5 minute test flight to be successfully performed.

\subsection{Mission Phase and Timeline}
A reference test day for HUGO is assumed to include four
test flights, with 100 operational days per year\footref{url:baseline_report}. Four main phases were identified for a test day: transport to test site, followed by pre-flight, in-flight, and post-flight operations. These shall now be evaluated in more detail.


\subsubsection{Transport to Test Site}
The day before a test flight is scheduled, the Terminal Aerodrome Forecast (TAF) shall be consulted to ensure that departure will be possible. In case of adverse weather prediction, the test day (or part of it) shall be cancelled and the customer shall be informed. 

In case the weather prediction allows for a successful take-off, the test day shall proceed according to schedule. If the test requires parts provided by the client to be mounted onto HUGO, these shall be integrated in the hangar, before proceeding onto the airfield. The ground station and fuel required for the flight shall also be transported onto the airfield.

\subsubsection{Pre-Flight Operations}
The pre-flight phase begins once HUGO is unloaded from the transportation unit and ends when the aircraft is powered on, all checks are performed, and fuel is loaded on board. The objective of this phase is to verify that the mission plan, operating environment, ground support equipment, aircraft configuration, and safety provisions are suitable for the intended mission.

An overview of the pre-flight steps is provided in \autoref{fig:pre-flight_checklist}. Once HUGO is unloaded, the mission plan is reviewed, and NOTAMs; an updated list containing alerts for potential hazards, are checked. If the initial inspection is completed and no problems are found, HUGO can be configured for the mission profile, and the crew briefing and preparation checklists can be performed. Otherwise, the test shall be aborted or delayed. Once all preparation steps are checked and recorded, HUGO can be considered as ready to fly, and the test personnel can proceed to the in-flight phase of the mission.

\subsubsection{Post-Flight Operations}
Once HUGO has performed a successful landing, the post-flight data extraction and operations can commence. In this mission phase, there are two possible scenarios,  depending on the mission profile of the day: HUGO can undergo preparations for the next test flight or undergo disassembly and reclaim procedures. An overview of operations for both scenarios are shown in \autoref{fig:post-flight_ops}.

Once HUGO has finished all tests scheduled for a specific flight day, the onboard system checks shall be performed. If no errors are detected, the power supply will be turned off, and an inspection of the external structure shall be performed. Next, the official debrief shall take place, and HUGO shall be disassembled and transported back to the hangar.

\subsection{Maintenance}
\label{subsec:maintenance}
To ensure HUGO's safe and optimal operation, regular maintenance shall be performed, along with regular repairs in case irregularities are found within the workday. Based on considerations made in previous design phases\footref{url:baseline_report}, one HUGO is expected to undergo maintenance for 20\% of the working days, which comes down to 50 working days within a year when accounting for free days. Continuing airworthiness is the responsibility of the operator or, in case the operator is not able to comply with the regulations and standards imposed, an outside certified provider\footnote{\label{Easa_Maintenance}\href{https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/document-library/easy-access-rules/online-publications/easy-access-rules-continuing-airworthiness?erules-id=ERULES-1963177438-693}{Easy Access Rules for Continuing Airworthiness} (accessed on 11.05.2026)}.

\subsubsection*{General Overview}
At this stage of the design, the HUGO fleet is expected to feature five models in the initial development stage, allowing for the possibility of expansion. Having such a fleet size requires a dedicated location where maintenance can be provided. This problem is ultimately discussed in \autoref{sec:logistics}, where the storage of models is expanded upon.

The maintenance operations include: planning of operation, assessment of process, execution of repair/verification, inspection of aircraft, ensure airworthiness, updating the records and release of the model. It is worth stating that modifications brought to the structure for the purpose of fitting a new configuration to be tested will be counted as maintenance. A general maintenance schedule may include the following actions based on the number of flight hours and operating conditions, as defined in \autoref{maintenance_operation}.

\subsubsection*{Continuing Airworthiness}
As stated previously, it is the operator's responsibility to ensure continuing airworthiness of an aircraft. For operators based in the EU, this typically entails appointing an approved Continuing Airworthiness Management Organisation (CAMO) \cite{EASA_CAirworthiness}. The responsibilities of CAMO include: development and control of the maintenance program, monitoring reliability and tracking compliance with Airworthiness Directives (ADs), as well as maintaining records and coordinating with authorities.

The steps towards establishing a cycle for maintaining airworthiness typically begins before the UAV is certified for operations. The competent national authority for operations based in the Netherlands is the Inspectie Leefomgeving en Transport (ILT)\footnote{\label{url:ILT}\href{https://www.ilent.nl/ilt-luchtvaartautoriteit}{Inspectie Leefomgeving en Transport - Over de ILT-Luchtvaartautoriteit} (accessed on 11.05.2026)}. The steps towards developing a maintenance cycle include developing safety and system design assurance, and then defining a continuing airworthiness concept. At this stage, the first draft of the Aircraft Maintenance Programme (AMP) is developed \cite{EASA_CAirworthiness}. The AMP shall be approved by the appointed CAMO organisation and renewed annually \footnote{\href{https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/the-agency/faqs/amp-aircraft-maintenance-programme}{EASA - Aircraft Maintenance Programme (AMP)} (accessed on 11.05.2026)}.

The AMP is part of Instructions for Continuing Airworthiness (ICA), which define the scope of limitations and maintenance task required to demonstrate that a design is compliant with certification requirements\footnote{\href{https://www.leanairline.com/blog/instructions-for-continuing-airworthiness}{Instructions for Continuing Airworthiness (ICA): From Certification to Operation} (accessed on 11.05.2026)}. ICA cover both scheduled and unscheduled maintenance procedures, and are necessary for obtaining a Type Certificate (TC). The relevance of a TC shall be explained in \autoref{ch:certification}.

Once the UAV is certified, CAMO takes the responsibility for scheduled maintenance enforcement, AD compliance, reliability tracking and Airworthiness Review Certificates \cite{EASA_CAirworthiness}. In case maintenance or repair procedures are required, a Maintenance, Repair, and Overhaul Organization (MRO) approved under EASA Part-145 shall be contracted to perform inspections, repairs, and modifications\footnote{\href{https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/acceptable-means-compliance-and-guidance-material-group/part-145-maintenance-organisation-approvals}{EASA Part-145 - Maintenance organisation approvals} (accessed on 11.05.2026)}. The MRO is additionally responsible for issuing certificates of release to service.

\subsection{Ground Crew Required for Operation}
\label{subsec:ground_crew}
% A ground crew is required to maintain the readiness of HUGO and to set up the ground stations for monitoring. 
According to ICAO\cite{ICAO2011UAS}, a remote crew comprises licensed crew members whose duties are crucial for the in-flight operations of a remotely-piloted
aircraft. This includes the remote pilot in charge of manipulating flight controls, and visual observers (VO) who assist the operations by visually observing the UAV while it remains within line-of-sight. It is also expected that an ATC controller and personnel responsible for coordinating runway manoeuvres will have to be involved. Since there are no hard requirements regarding the minimum composition of the crew, at this stage of the design it is assumed that HUGO will require one pilot, one VO, and additional personnel as deemed appropriate by the airport authorities.

\subsection{Storage Facility}
\label{subsec:hangar}
On the ground, the aircraft will require an enclosed location to be stored in for maintenance and preparations. As mentioned in \autoref{subsec:maintenance}, the initial fleet is projected to comprise five HUGOs. This necessitates the use of a large hangar to house parts and machines needed for repairs. 

Den Helder Airport has hangar facilities that can accommodate Project HUGO, although the conditions for using these facilities are not specified on the airport website\footnote{\label{fn:denhelder_facilities}\href{https://denhelderairport.nl/eng/airport/#faciliteiten}{Den Helder Airport facilities} (accessed on 12.05.2026)}, Multiple external operators use the airport as a primary base of operations \footref{fn:denhelder_facilities}. Hangar availability is stated to be on request in the EHKD aerodrome information\footnote{\label{fn:ehkd_eaip}\href{https://eaip.lvnl.nl/web/eaip/AIRAC\%20AMDT\%2005-2026_2026_05_14/eAIP/EH-AD\%202\%20EHKD\%201-en-GB.html}{EHKD - Den Helder/De Kooy} (accessed on 12.05.2026)}. The hangar is reported to be 51 m long, 31 m wide, and 11 m high, giving a floor area of approximately \(1581~\mathrm{m^2}\) \cite{DenHelderAirport2013DHANieuwsbrief}. With a maximum wingspan of 15 m for one Project HUGO aircraft, the hangar dimensions are sufficient to place up to three aircraft in line. Once the hangar is secured, it will function as the base of operation for the storage, maintenance and verification of HUGO before and after each flight. 

\subsection{Airfield and Ground Support}
\label{subsec:ground_support}
Usage of an airfield introduces additional airfield operating costs, ATC coordination requirements, and the mapping of ground routes, taxi areas, and recovery zones. Each drone flight must be approved by De Kooy Air Traffic Control\footnote{\href{https://denhelderairport.nl/eng/flight-information/}{Flight information Den Helder Airport} (accessed on 12.05.2026)}. The departure and landing windows for HUGO will be influenced by the adjacent traffic, necessitating the assumption of multiple missed approaches or holding in the fuel budget. Additionally, Den Helder Airport charges a fee of \EUR{23.00} per landing for a fixed wing aircraft with MTOW below 1500kg\footnote{\href{https://denhelderairport.nl/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2026-fixed-wing-miv-01-01-2026-2.pdf}{Landing fees for fixed wing aircraft at Den Helder} (accessed on 12.05.2026)}.
Den Helder hosts off-shore flights above the North Sea\footref{fn:denhelder_facilities}, which fits HUGO's mission profile as detailed in \autoref{sec:operations}. %Fuel availability is limited to JET A1\footnote{\href{https://denhelderairport.nl/eng/information-for-pilots/}{Information for pilots} (accessed 12.05.2026)}; using alternative fuels such as SAF would require individual approval from the airport authorities.

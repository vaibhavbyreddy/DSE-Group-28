\chapter{Risk Assessment and Certification}
\label{ch:certification}
This chapter focuses on the analysis of technical risks associated with the operations of HUGO, as well as the steps required to obtain the certification necessary for operations testing the aeroelastic effects of high aspect ratio wings. A qualitative assessment of technical risks is performed in \autoref{sec:technical_risk_assessment}. Then, in \autoref{sec:type_certificate}, the classification process is described that places HUGO into a type class category. Following the assessment of flight class, the certification for a Design Organisational Approval (DOA) is covered in \autoref{sec: DOA}. Establishing a certification basis for HUGO allows for a construction of a preliminary flight envelope, which is the focus of \autoref{sec:flight_envelope}. Lastly, the environmental protection aspect of the certification is expanded upon in \autoref{sec:environmental_protection}.

\section{Type Certificate}
\label{sec:type_certificate}

According to the EASA eRules for Initial Airworthiness and Environmental Protection (eRules), the Type Certificate (TC) is a document issued by the competent authority declaring an aircraft meets all required airworthiness standards \cite{EASAInitialAirworthiness2025}. The eRules describes the process and all necessary documents to obtain a type certificate inside Annex I. Section A details all the obligations of the applicant, and section B details those of the competent authority (EASA). The eRules manual is large and difficult to navigate, as such,  \autoref{fig:Certification} will serve as a visual guide for the reader. The grey blocks indicate the starting point, purple indicates the obligations of the applicant, blue the obligations of the authority, and yellow any extra source of information.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{figures/TC.png}
    \caption{eRules steps for the requirements, and procedure for obtaining a Type Certificate}
    \label{fig:Certification}
\end{figure}


The requirements for a TC are explained in full in section (21.A.21). It states that the following must be met:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item demonstration of capabilities (21.A.14),
    \item demonstration of compliance with the type certification basis, operational suitability data certification basis and environmental protection requirements (21.A.20),
    \item demonstration of compliance with UAS Type Certification basis (21.B.80). 
\end{enumerate}

Section (21.A.14) will be further elaborated in \autoref{sec: DOA}, as it pertains to the process for obtaining a Design Organisation Approval (DOA) under 21.A.14(a), with further guidance in Subsection J. Both (21.A.20) and (21.B.80) are covered under the section (21.A.15): Application for a TC, and will be expanded on below.

As previously stated, (21.A.15): Application for a TC, requires compliance with section (21.A.20): Demonstration of compliance with the type certification basis, operational suitability data certification basis and environmental protection requirements Regulation. 

\subsection{Compliance with certification basis, operational suitability, environmental protection}

For compliance with (21.A.20) after the certification programme has been accepted by EASA, HUGO's design will have to demonstrate compliance requirements in the following areas:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item  type-certification basis for a TC or restricted TC (21.B.80),
    \item operational suitability (21.B.82),
    \item environmental protection (21.B.85).
\end{enumerate}

According to 21.B.80, special conditions apply to HUGO, detailing how projects falling outside the scope of the eRules should comply with the regulations (21.B.75). For HUGO, this entails using the Special Condition for Light Unmanned Aircraft Systems - High Risk \cite{EASA_SC_Light_UAS_High_Risk_2021}. Additionally, HUGO falls outside the scope of (21.B.82) due to it's unconventional design and operations.

Section (21.B.85), detailing the necessary compliance with environmental protection requirements as dictated in ICAO Annex XVI, further expanded on in \autoref{sec:environmental_protection}. 

Section (21.A.33) and (21.A.35) contain obligations for the applicant regarding inspections and tests regulation, and flight tests respectively. Lastly, (21.B.100) details the authority's obligation to provide guidance on the "Level of involvement", and the likelihood and impact of non-compliance.

In order to successfully apply for a TC, EASA shall be provided with means of demonstration of compliance with all of the discussed requirements. 


\subsection{Special Conditions for UAS}
\label{sec: special conditions}
The special conditions detailed under the Special Condition for Light Unmanned Aircraft Systems - High Risk define additional requirements for: Design and Construction, and Systems and Equipment. The scope of these additional requirements covers risk of failure, environmental hazards, robustness in the event of a lightning strike, and additional High Intensity Radiated Fields (HIRF) protection \cite{EASA_SC_Light_UAS_High_Risk_2021}.

\subsection{Application for a Type Certificate}
As mentioned in \autoref{sec:type_certificate}, the application procedure for the TC is done in accordance with (21.A.15) \cite{EASAInitialAirworthiness2025}. The detailed procedure after the project is in compliance with (21.A.21) is detailed below:

According to the guidelines, the TC application should be made in a form and manner established by the authority, including as a minimum: a preliminary description of the UAS or the Control and Monitoring Unit (CMU), and the kind of operations for which certification is requested. In addition it shall comply with 21.A.20, which outlines the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a detailed description of the design, including all the configurations to be certified;
    \item the proposed operating characteristics and limitations;
    \item the intended use of the UAS or the CMU, and the kind of operations for which certification is requested;
    \item a proposal for the initial TC basis, operational sustainability data certification basis, and environmental protection requirements in accordance with (21.B.80), (21.B.82), and (21.B.85);
    \item a proposal for the breakdown of the certification program into meaningful groups of compliance demonstration activities and data, including a proposal for the means of compliance and related compliance documents;
    \item a proposal for the assessment of meaningful groups of compliance demonstration activities and data addressing the likelihood of an unidentified non-compliance with the TC basis operational sustainability data certification basis, or environmental protection requirements and the potential impact on the non-compliance on the UAS safety or environmental compatibility or on the safety of the CMU. The proposed assessment shall take into account at least the elements set out in points 1 to 4 of (21.B.100(a)):
    \begin{enumerate}
    \item novel or unusual features of the certification project, including operational, organisational and knowledge management aspects;
    \item complexity of the design and/or demonstration of compliance;
    \item criticality of the design or technology and the related safety and environmental risks, including those identified on similar designs; and
    \item performance and experience of the design organisation of the applicant in the domain concerned.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}


\section{Design Organisational Approval (DOA)} \label{sec: DOA}
This Section discusses the approach towards certifying modifications to the original configuration of HUGO, following a successful application for a Type Certificate. Special attention is dedicated towards the DOA, its implications for the feasibility of HUGO as a flying testbed, and the necessary steps towards obtaining necessary approval from EASA.

\subsection{Certification of Design Modifications}
For the certification procedure of an aircraft, the competent authority in Europe, EASA, requires extensive documentation and validation procedures of the design. For this stage of the UAV design, several EASA-imposed operational restrictions must be considered, including the applicable airworthiness class and the permitted operational environment \cite{EASAInitialAirworthiness2025}.

DOA application is a step in the process of obtaining a Type Certificate, according to 21.A.14 (a), capability demonstration. Along with 21.A.20 (compliance documentation) and 21.B.80 (configuration demonstration), these three documents will facilitate the application for a Type Certificate.

The application for a DOA involves cooperation with EASA from early stages of the design. The steps to be taken towards successful certification are as follows:\footnote{\href{https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/aircraft-products/aircraft-certification?}{EASA Aircraft Certification} (accessed on 04.05.2026)}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Technical familiarisation and certification basis,
    \item Establishment of the certification programme,
    \item Compliance demonstration,
    \item Technical closure and issue of approval.
\end{enumerate}

Costs involved in this process and the time required for analysis of the design are not well defined, but can range from months to years, depending on the complexity of the idea\footnote{\href{https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/light/topics/idea-sky-how-easa-ensures-aircraft-safe-fly}{EASA - From idea to the sky: how EASA ensures that aircraft is safe to fly} (accessed on 12.05.2026)}. This conflicts with HUGO's mission, which requires frequent structural modifications and configuration changes. Applying for a Supplemental Type Certificate or completely re-certifying HUGO for each test case due to design changes would result in an excess of downtime of the UAV, loss in revenue, and ultimately loss of purpose as a research test-bed\cite{EU2019_2153_fees-and-charges}.

According to EASA classification, re-certification is required each time a change is implemented into the design of an aircraft, with a clear distinction between "minor changes" and "major changes"
(21.A.91)\cite{EASAInitialAirworthiness2025}. A "minor change" is classified as any type of modification that does not show any appreciable effect on weight, structural strength, reliability and other specifications. A change that is not a "minor change" is considered as a "major change". This distinction determines the steps that need to be taken in order to certify modifications to the design of an aircraft.


\subsection{The Importance of DOA for HUGO's Mission}
In case of HUGO, the expected structural changes in the wing and tail planforms between tests can be considered as "major changes" according to the EASA classification discussed previously. Typically, this would require direct supervision from EASA, significantly increasing the time and resources required to ensure that a new proposed configuration can be classified as airworthy. To facilitate this process, a DOA application can be made \footnote{\label{DOA_site}\href{https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/aircraft-products/design-organisations/design-organisations-approvals}{EASA - Design Organisations Approvals} (accessed on 05.06.2026)}.

A  DOA is a certification that permits the holder or organisation to perform the certification process internally, reducing the workload of EASA to documentation approval as the certification authority. In the context of HUGO, this would allow the design team to internally implement all "minor changes" without unnecessary delay and with minimal dedicated resources. Additionally, "major changes" to the platforms can be classified as research procedures, excluding the need for full re-certification and minimising EASA's involvement \footnote{In conversation with EASA employee}.

\section{Requirements following from CS-23 Amendment 4}
This Section describes the requirements for aerobatic aircraft following from Acceptable Means of Compliance (AMCs) detailed in EASA CS-23 Amendment 4 \cite{EASA_CS23_Amendment4}.

\subsection{Proof of Structure}
According to the AMCs for CS-23 aircraft, demonstration of compliance shall be achieved predominantly with the use of flight tests. However, analyses including FEM models are also acceptable, so long as their reliability for the certified structure is proven, and they are supplemented by a comparison of other methods regarded as sufficiently accurate and applicable. A strength test programme shall additionally be established for the wing, wing carry through, fuselage and empennage structures (e. g. via ultimate load testing). 

To account for material and production variability, a test correction factor $T_f$ shall be applied to each test based on the coefficient of variation $c_v$ provided by the manufacturer for a given product (see: \autoref{tab:test_factors}). The $c_v$ is defined by \autoref{eq:coefficient_of_variation} for a population with mean $M$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ \cite[p. 2-C-2]{EASA_CS23_Amendment4}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coefficient_of_variation}
c_v=100\cdot\frac{\sigma}{M}\hspace{0.1cm}[\%]
\end{equation}

\section{Flight envelope}
\label{sec:flight_envelope}
The flight envelope of the HUGO aircraft in symmetric flight is based on EASA CS-23 airworthiness requirements for aerobatic aircraft \cite{EASA_CS23_Am3_2012}. According to the specifications, except where limited by the stall speeds, the positive limit manoeuvre load factor is 6.0. The negative limit manoeuvre load factor is allowed to vary linearly from -3.0 at the cruise speed up to -1.0 at the dive speed. Additionally, symmetrical vertical gust loads are superimposed on the manoeuvre flight envelope, with gust velocities specified in  \cite{EASA_CS23_Am3_2012}. 

These constraints result in the flight envelope as illustrated in \autoref{fig:V-n_diagram}, when assuming parameters as shown in \autoref{tab:V-n_parameters}. Clearly, the gust loads are not constraining for this aircraft design, due to the high manoeuvre load factors. Nonetheless, the required structural performance is expected to significantly influence the material selection, as well as the final configuration selection. These shall be addressed in \autoref{ch:engineering_design} and \autoref{ch:trade-off}, respectively.

\section{Environmental protection}
\label{sec:environmental_protection}
Environmental compatibility was introduced in the European legislation Regulation (EU) 2018/1139 \footnote{\label{doc_1139}\href{https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2018/1139/oj/eng}{EU Document 32018R1139}(accessed on 13.05.2026)}. The intention was to create a common framework for the environmental certification of aircraft. A large part of the regulation was based on the Chicago Convention (insert reference here). HUGO is exempt from those rules as stated in recital 1 in Regulation (EU) 2025/870 \footnote{\href{https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX\%3A32025R0870}{EU Document 32025R0870} (accessed on 13.05.2026).}: \textit{"Aircraft, other than unmanned aircraft, and their engines, propellers, parts and non-installed equipment should comply with the environmental protection requirements. Regulation (EU) 2018/1139 provides for them by referring to specific provisions of the Chicago Convention, which contain those requirements."}
\newline
\newline
A specific rule-set has been established for unmanned aircraft: Regulation (EU) 2018/1139 \footref{doc_1139} of the European Parliament and of the Council in Annex~IX: \textit{"Essential Environmental Requirements for Unmanned Aircraft"} states that unmanned aircraft shall comply with the environmental performance requirements set out in Annex~III.
Looking at Annex~III, the main point is a general approach for the design and trade-off between minimising noise and emissions. The area and its surroundings must be taken into account for the operation of the unmanned aircraft, with an emphasis on reducing noise and emissions.

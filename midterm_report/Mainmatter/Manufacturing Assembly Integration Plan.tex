\textbf{Open Mold processes}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{\textbf{Hand layup}}: most common composite manufacturing technique. Requires minimal equipment and low cost, but skilled labor. It is an open molding process --- a single-sided mold (positive or negative) is used for fabrication. It is especially suitable for the production of large shapes. First, the mold is cleaned and coated with a gel layer. Reinforcement material is then saturated with resin and applied layer by layer until the required thickness is reached. The laminate is compacted with brushes or rollers to eliminate air pockets and distribute the resin evenly. After curing, the component is removed from the mold, trimmed, and polished.

    \item \textit{\textbf{Vacuum resin infusion}}: Vacuum pressure is used to draw resin into the mold cavity in this two-sided molding process, which combines a single-sided mold with a vacuum bag. Fiber layers are first positioned in the mold, after which resin is sucked into the cavity. Atmospheric pressure helps compact the laminate and remove trapped air bubbles.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Closed mold processes}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
    \item \textit{\textbf{Resin transfer molding (RTM)}}: Unlike vacuum resin infusion, this process injects resin into the mold cavity under high positive pressure. The reinforcement layers are placed in the mold beforehand and enclosed with a rigid top mold. The thermosetting resin cures while under pressure, and the part is removed once sufficient crosslinking is achieved, followed by post-curing. Components made with this method have smooth surface finishes and excellent mechanical properties, including high stiffness, strength, and impact resistance.

    \item \textit{\textbf{Compression molding}}: Compression molding is used to manufacture composite parts from dry chopped fibers mixed with resin. In this method, the laminate is placed between heated matched metal molds inside a compression press. Heat and pressure are applied to rapidly cure the material. The process requires minimal post-processing, reducing labor costs, and can be used for both thermosetting and thermoplastic composites. It is well suited for high-volume production of complex, high-strength parts with accurate dimensions, while built-in cooling systems help speed up the production cycle.
\end{enumerate}

Due to the large size and high strength requirements of the primary landing gear components, the forging process is divided into two sequential stages. The components first undergo open die forging to work the billet into a rough preform, refining the grain structure and achieving the approximate overall shape. They subsequently go through closed die forging to acquire the final near-net shape with the required dimensional precision and optimised grain flow alignment along the load paths. The combination of both methods leads to an optimisation of cost, material waste, and final mechanical properties of the forged aluminium alloy.

For the subsequent removal of excess material, CNC machining is selected for both the initial rough machining stage, during which the bulk of the material is removed, and the final precision machining stage, which achieves the tight tolerances and surface finish requirements of the component. CNC machining is well suited to this role due to its versatility — combining turning, milling, drilling, and grinding operations under pre-programmed software control - which minimises human error and enables the consistent production of the complex geometries present in landing gear components.

For the largest primary components, such as the outer cylinder and piston rod, dedicated deep hole drilling machines are required to produce the central longitudinal bore. These are long, slender cavities with large depth-to-diameter ratios that cannot be achieved on conventional machining. Dedicated deep hole drilling machines address these constraints through specialised tooling and high-pressure coolant systems, enabling the required dimensional accuracy and surface finish to be achieved within the bore.

Following machining, the aluminium alloy components undergo heat treatment to achieve the required mechanical properties. Surface treatment follows as the final step, providing corrosion and wear protection by building a hard, corrosion-resistant oxide layer on the surface, followed by primer and topcoat application for additional environmental protection.

\subsection{Adhesive bonding}
Bonding is a joining method that uses an adhesive to connect two components. The adhesive transfers loads between the parts, ideally through shear. Tensile loading of the joint creates high stress concentrations, which can lead to premature failure.

Some advantages of this joining method include preserving the original material strength by avoiding holes and stress concentrations, maintaining a smooth surface without protruding fasteners, enabling the joining of dissimilar materials, and providing excellent resistance to corrosion. It also allows for air- and watertight sealing. There are also several disadvantages to bonding, including the need for surface pretreatment, the inability to disassemble joints, and the fact that it primarily carries transverse loads. Additionally, the manufacturing process can be relatively complex.
\end{comment}
\begin{comment}
\subsection{Riveting}

Riveting is a widely used joining technique in the aerospace industry, where loads are transferred through fasteners or loaded pins. It is mainly used for joining thin sheet materials, typically only a few millimeters thick. However, rivets are generally unsuitable for composite materials because of bearing stress concerns; in such cases, bolts or alternative fastening methods are preferred.

Riveting offers several advantages, including high reliability, simple tooling, and straightforward inspection and repair processes. However, it also has drawbacks: rivets are susceptible to fatigue and corrosion, sensitive to leakage since they are not watertight, and may disrupt smooth aerodynamic surfaces. Additionally, they require holes that reduce material strength and are primarily designed to carry shear loads only.

\subsection{Bolting}
Bolted joints are similar to riveted joints in that they require comparable preparation steps, such as drilling and deburring, and they operate on similar load transfer principles. However, there are also several key differences: bolts can carry tensile loads, allow for disassembly, and are suitable for thicker sections. On the downside, they are generally more expensive than rivets.

\subsection{Welding}
A welding process joins two pieces of similar material to one continuous section. In the case of metals, welding involves the local melting of material. Welding and riveting (sometimes also bolting) are the most interchangeable alternatives.

Welding offers several advantages, including a continuous load path, no need for overlapping components, and a resulting smooth surface. It also provides air and watertight joints. However, it has notable drawbacks: the local melting of material alters the microstructure, which can lead to variations in quality along the weld length. For this reason, high-strength materials that rely on heat treatment to achieve their properties are generally unsuitable for welding. This is one of the key reasons why welding is rarely preferred over riveting in the aircraft industry.
\end{comment}

Following structural assembly, integration of the aircraft systems proceeds in a defined sequence driven by accessibility - items that are hardest to reach or that other systems depend on are installed first. The avionics wiring harness is installed first, before any interior or equipment obstructs access to the airframe structure. The wire harness connects every critical system from avionics to landing gear, and can be found on gas turbine engines, nacelles, and airframe fuselages - with modern fly-by-wire aircraft relying critically on these interconnects. The battery is installed next as part of the electrical system, followed by the avionics equipment itself - flight computers, sensors, and displays - which are connected to the pre-routed harness. The engines are then installed and connected to the fuel, hydraulic, and electrical systems. The interior is installed last, as it covers and protects the underlying systems while also being the most easily removable for any rework that may be required during systems testing. Arranging the integration in the proper sequence is an important objective, as improper sequencing causes rework, time delays, cost increases, and potential safety risks.

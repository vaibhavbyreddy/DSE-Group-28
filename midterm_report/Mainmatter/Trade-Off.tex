The no-go values of the criteria were also estimated. They are given in \autoref{tab: no-go} with a short rationale. A more in-depth justification is given below.

\textbf{Versatility :} his is the core mission of the testbed. A configuration that cannot test canard-dependent setups or is limited to a single stability condition is fundamentally failing its purpose. That is why a threshold of 3 was chosen.

\textbf{Performance:} specifically about reaching the required Mach number. If a configuration can't get close to the test conditions you need, the data is scientifically irrelevant.

\textbf{Reliability:} this covers both mechanical reliability and data quality. An unreliable testbed either produces corrupted data or grounds itself frequently. 

\textbf{Cost:} cost is a constraint, but not a mission-critical one. A configuration that is very expensive is painful, but doesn't make the aircraft scientifically invalid.

\textbf{Sustainability:}  lowest weighted criterion and not mission-critical.

\subsection{Trade-Off Weight Factors}
\label{subsec:trade-off_weight_factors}

To determine the weight factors, the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) was used. This was done by pairwise comparison of the criteria specified earlier. In the case of n criteria, a total of $n(n-1)/2$ comparisons contribute to the formation of a pairwise comparison matrix (PCM) of order n. The diagonal entries of the PCM are all 1, and the remaining values are simply the reciprocals of these $n(n-1)/2$ comparisons \cite{Pant 2022}.

For the pairwise comparison, a scale from 1 to 9 was used, as proposed by Saaty \cite{Saaty 1980}. These scores are defined as follows: 1 - Equal importance; 3 - Moderate importance, 5 - Strong importance; 7 - Very strong importance; 9 - Extreme importance. 

Assigning weights from 1 to 9 to the n criteria, the PCM is constructed so that the rows represent the ratios of a criterion's score to those of the others \cite{Saaty 1980}. The obtained matrix is shown below.

Once the matrix was normalised, the values of each row were averaged to obtain the normalised principal eigenvector (priority vector). The sum of its elements should also be 1.

\[
\mathbf{w} =
\begin{bmatrix}
w_{\text{Cost}} \\
w_{\text{Versatility}} \\
w_{\text{Performance}} \\
w_{\text{Reliability}} \\
w_{\text{Sustainability}} \\
w_{\text{Mass}}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.0993 \\
0.3493 \\
0.2157 \\
0.2157 \\
0.0402 \\
0.0797
\end{bmatrix}
\]

The priority vector shows relative weights among the things that we compare. The larger the entry, the higher the ranking of the respective criterion. 

The consistency can also be estimated with the use of the principal eigenvalue $\lambda_{max}$. It is obtained from the summation of the products between the elements of the priority vector and the column sums of the PCM. 

\begin{align*}
\lambda_{\max} &= 0.0993 \cdot 11.83 + 0.3493 \cdot 2.67 + 0.2157 \cdot 4.8667 \\
&\quad + 0.2157 \cdot 4.8667 + 0.0402 \cdot 23 + 0.0797 \cdot 13.3333 = 6.1943
\end{align*}

For a consistent matrix $\lambda_{max} = n$ \cite{Saaty 1980}. The degree of consistency of an inconsistent matrix can be estimated by using the consistency index (CI) \cite{Saaty 1980} defined as:

\begin{equation}
    CI = \frac{\lambda_{max}-n}{n-1} = \frac{6.1943 - 6}{6 - 1} = 0.03887
\end{equation}

The obtained CI is compared with the random consistency index (RI). For n = 6, RI = 1.24. This is done by the consistency ratio (CR) \cite{Saaty 1980}:

\begin{equation}
    CR = \frac{CI}{CR} = 0.03135
\end{equation}
If CR < 10\%, then the evaluation is consistent \cite{Saaty 1980}.
